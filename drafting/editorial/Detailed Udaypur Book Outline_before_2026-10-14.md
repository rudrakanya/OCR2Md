### **Thesis**

Udaypur is usually presented as one great building. This book argues it is a single deliberate act of place-making: a town, a tank and a Śiva temple founded together in 1059 by Udayāditya — a nephew of Bhoja from a junior branch of the Paramāra house — on Malwa's eastern frontier, eleven years before he became king. That ensemble is the most complete statement the dynasty left in stone, and the book follows it from the sandstone it was cut from to the village living around it now, through Khaljī conquest, Tughluq and Mughal re-use, colonial antiquarianism and ASI protection.

### **The frame: Malwa among its neighbours, c. 900–1305**

The Paramāras began as Rāṣṭrakūṭa feudatories and ended as a name on a Khaljī casualty list. Between those points they held the plateau every other power wanted, because Malwa is the hinge between the Gangetic north and the Deccan, and between the Gujarat ports and the eastern forests. Neighbours pressed from every side: the **Chaulukyas** of Gujarat to the west, holding the sea outlets; the **Kalachuris** of Tripurī to the east, across the frontier Udaypur guards; the **Chandelas** to the north-east; the **Chāhamānas** of Śākambharī to the north-west — whose king Durlabha III sent the cavalry that let Udayāditya retake Malwa; the **Western Cālukyas** of Kalyāṇa to the south, who killed both Muñja and Jayasiṃha I; and later the **Hoysalas** and **Yādavas** from the Deccan. Independence was won in 972 by sacking the Rāṣṭrakūṭa capital itself.

This is why a temple of this quality stands in a village of six thousand. Udaypur was a frontier seat facing Chedi, sited where sandstone, water and a defensible hill met a north–south road. Ganguly's reading of the inscriptions puts Udayāditya's recovered kingdom as reaching Jhalrapatan in the north, Bhilsa in the east and Nimar in the south — "almost the whole of the territory over which his predecessor Bhoja ruled." The civilizational claim is specific: an eleventh-century regional dynasty on a contested plateau brought the Bhūmija temple style to its most developed form, then lost the state that made it, leaving the building to outlive its makers by seven centuries.

### **4\. Udayāditya, the protagonist**

**Lineage — committed: a nephew of Bhoja, through a junior branch of the Paramāra house.** The "nephew" reading is cited to *Bhoja Paramara and His Times* (Mahesh Singh), in the chapter on economic conditions, which refers in passing to "some wall-paintings of the time of Bhoja's nephew, Udayaditya (1059–80)." The "junior branch" half is Ganguly's, in chapter IV of the *History of the Paramara Dynasty*: "Udayaditya, a scion of a junior branch of the Paramaras, stood gallantly for the liberation of his ancestral dominion." The two reconcile cleanly — the Udayapur *praśasti* gives Udayāditya his own paternal line (Sauravīra → Jñātā → Udayāditya), so his father belonged to a collateral branch, and "nephew" describes his generational relation to Bhoja rather than descent from Bhoja's own father. The narrative states this as fact; a single endnote records the debate and still lands here.

*One caution for your ruling.* The explicit word "nephew" appears only once in the whole corpus for Udayāditya — an aside in a paragraph about painting style, not a genealogical argument — and the same book calls Bhoja himself "the nephew of king Munja," which may be where the usage comes from. Ganguly's junior-branch reading is the stronger evidential base, argued from the *praśasti* pedigree. The committed formulation carries both halves; overrule it if you want the emphasis reversed.

**Reign — committed: c. 1070–1093.** The start date follows Ganguly's own reasoning: Karṇa of Gujarat only acceded in 1063, so Udayāditya "cannot be taken to have ruled in 1059 A.D. as a sovereign lord" — he built the temple in 1059 "when he was ruling his paternal territory apparently from Udayapur," and the Panhera inscription shows Jayasiṃha still ruling Malwa that year. Pande independently states that Udayāditya "ascended the throne in 1070 CE." The end date is fixed by Ganguly's note that Naravarman "began his reign some time before 1094 A.D.," while the Jhalrapatan record of 1086 still has Udayāditya reigning. The competing spans (c. 1058–1087, and the 1059–80 range in the painting aside) go to a single endnote.

**Life.** Born into a collateral line holding territory in eastern Malwa. He founded the town, temple and tank as one endowment in 1059 while still a territorial lord under a collapsing kingdom, and took the throne around 1070 after Jayasiṃha I fell. He appealed to the Cāhamāna Durlabha III of Śākambharī, whose cavalry let him break the Caulukya–Karṇāṭa occupation; the Nagpur *praśasti* likens him to the boar lifting a drowning earth. He recovered a kingdom reaching Jhalrapatan, Bhilsa and Nimar, raised the flagstaff in 1080, was still reigning in 1086, and was the only Paramāra to strike gold coins — nine of his inscriptions survive across Ujjain, Udaypur, Dhar, Un and Kamed. He died around 1093, cause unrecorded, and was succeeded briefly by Lakṣmadeva and then by Naravarman (1094–1134).

**How the book portrays him.** As a builder-restorer, not a conqueror-hero and not a footnote to Bhoja. His most revealing act came *before* he had power: on a threatened frontier, with the dynasty broken, he laid out a town, a reservoir and a temple as a single design — a man planning permanence while the centre burned. Where the sources are panegyric we read them as such; but the book knows who he was, and does not hedge.

### **5\. How disagreements between sources are handled**

1. **A measurement or count of one physical thing** — commit to the single most authoritative source (Survey for dimensions; Pande for the temple). One figure in the text, at most one endnote. *Several figures for one wall is a defect, not honesty.*  
2. **The protagonist's biography and lineage** — settled in §4.  
3. **Dynastic chronology** — the timeline in §3, identical everywhere.  
4. **A contradiction the context can resolve** — reconcile it by explanation. For example: the 2011 Census counts 6,383 residents; VMT (c. 2021–22) estimates about 8,000. The honest framing is a decade of growth — a rounded authorial estimate set against a decadal count — not a contradiction.  
5. **A genuinely open question** — for instance, whether Bhoja joined an anti-Ghaznavid confederacy: state a working position and note the debate in a line. This exception never applies to the protagonist's biography or to any measurement.

**Committed measurements.**

* **Naṭarāja monolith:** 8.2 × 4.2 m (Geo-Heritage, corroborated by the INTACH catalogue).  
* **Subsidiary shrines:** eight (Pande: "surrounded by eight subsidiary shrines, quite a few now broken").  
* **Fort wall:** 1,400 m, dry-stone sandstone with an earthen rampart stone-faced on the inside and no binding material, enclosing 56 acres (Geo-Heritage). The *fabric description replaces the contested thickness figure* — 18 ft, 8–10 ft and \~4 m are all in circulation, and none is stated.  
* **Hill heights:** mesa 567.66 m, butte 582.31 m — the butte is the higher of the two despite being the smaller.  
* **Temple:** a 32-point star of seven *bhūmi*s, red sandstone (Hardy; Pande).

**Signature objects are described from the object, never from the generic image.** The hill Naṭarāja is *not* the familiar many-armed dancer in a ring of flame. It has **six arms** (trident, *ḍamaru*, two unidentified attributes, two hands in *mudrā*), stands over Apasmāra, **reclines on its back**, is **unfinished with chisel marks still visible**, carries an undeciphered inscription, and lies beside the pillar-bases of an abandoned second structure. The INTACH catalogue titles it *Chaturbhuji* (four-armed) while its own description says six — we follow the description. The same rule holds for every signature object: arm count, attributes, posture, scale and peculiarities come from the survey plates; the dimensions come from the Survey.

## **The chapter plan**

Each chapter lists its sections with the source type each draws on and a rough word budget, then the facts it is committed to, how it hands in and out of its neighbours, and any true gap that remains. Chapters run 4,000–5,000 words.

### **C1 — Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory *(Ancient Scriptural References)***

*The book's opening question:* a village of six thousand carries a record out of all proportion to its size, and that gap is what the book sets out to explain.

* **1.1 Arrival** — the ordinary town first; the monument approached through the village. *(Gazetteer · \~900 w)*  
* **1.2 The inventory** — five regimes standing within one square kilometre. *(Survey · \~900 w)*  
* **1.3 What "long memory" means** — the honest negative: the Purāṇic register is regional, not local; no text names Udayapura before the eleventh century. *(Scripture · \~900 w)*  
* **1.4 The name** — Udayapura is not Udaipur in Rajasthan, a conflation that has corrupted the secondary literature. *(Epigraphy · \~750 w)*  
  * **1.4 Stance** — evidence kept apart from interpretation; what the book commits to, and why. *(\~800 w)*

*Committed:* 6,383 residents (2011); foundation 1059; flagstaff 1080\.  
 *Leads:* opens the book → C2 asks what country produced this town.  
 *Gap:* no pre-eleventh-century textual mention exists — state the absence, don't fill it.

### **C2 — The Betwa Valley: Land, Rivers and Seasons *(Geography)***

*Premise:* Udaypur sits in a river basin that does not water it, and that contradiction shapes everything built here.

* **2.1 The Vetravatī** — rise in Raisen, south-to-north flow, confluence with the Yamuna. *(Gazetteer · \~850 w)* **An intermittent river** — perennial only in its lower \~85 km, which is why tanks were a necessity, not decoration. *(Survey · \~950 w)*  
* **2.2 Monsoon and the working year** — the kharif/rabi cycle that governs building, farming and festival alike. *(Gazetteer · \~850 w)*  
* **2.3 The plateau** — the Vindhyan tableland, 340–480 m; the Malwa–Bundelkhand transition that makes this a frontier. *(Survey · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* elevation 340–480 m, mean 410 m; the Kewtan runs 1.8 km from the hill.  
 *Leads:* from C1's question → C3 turns from water to rock.

### **C3 — Stones and Forests: Geology, Flora and Fauna *(Geology and Landscape Report)***

*Premise:* the rock that makes the hill makes the temple; quarry, fort and shrine are one geological story.

* **3.1 Upper Rewa sandstone** — iron-pigmented and horizontally bedded: why the temple is red, and why it could be cut at all. *(Survey · \~900 w)*  
* **3.2 Mesa, butte, arch** — a decaying tableland: the mesa, the higher butte, and the natural arch INTACH calls the Third Eye. *(Survey · \~900 w)*  
* **3.3 What sandstone permits** — corbelling, lace-carving, and the absence of true arches: geology as a design constraint. *(Monograph · \~850 w)* **Quarrying** — reading the bedding planes and splitting by wedge-hole; the live quarry by Bhujariya Talab. *(Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **3.4 Forest and fauna** — Van Tulsi, Neem, Bel and Palash; vultures, langur and cave bats; the hill's reserved-forest status. *(Survey · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* mesa 567.66 m; butte 582.31 m; Upper Rewa (Vindhyan) sandstone.  
 *Leads:* from C2's water → C4 asks who used this hill first.

### **C4 — Before Udaypur: Prehistory and Early Historic Vidisha**

*Premise:* the hill was sacred and inhabited for centuries before Udayāditya; the Paramāra town intervened in an occupied landscape.

* **4.1 Besnagar and Vidiśā** — the early historic city, the Heliodorus pillar, the Śuṅga and Gupta horizons, the shift to Bhilsa. *(Gazetteer \+ Dynastic · \~900 w)*  
* **4.2 Sanchi's shadow** — what a major Buddhist landscape 40 km off implies about routes, patronage and the supply of craft. *(Dynastic · \~800 w)*  
* **4.3 The hill before the fort** — sixth-century Saptamātṛkā and Navagraha panels, a Jain Tīrthaṅkara cave, a Lajjā Gaurī petroglyph. *(Survey · \~950 w)*  
* **4.4 Forest polities** — Bhil, Gond and Sahariya presence; the forest kingdoms (Āṭavika Rājya) of the early texts. *(Gazetteer · \~800 w)*


*Committed:* the hill panels attributed to the sixth century (by comparison, not excavation); the fort predates Udayāditya.  
 *Leads:* from C3's used landscape → C5 widens from site to region.  
 *Gap:* no excavation report for Basoda tehsil exists in any source. (Note: the heritage profile wrongly places Udayagiri beside Udaypur — it is near Vidisha town; do not repeat this.)

### **C5 — From Avanti to Malwa: Dynasties, Trade Routes and Sacred Cities *(Evolution over the Years)***

*Premise:* Malwa's identity was assembled over a millennium, and Udaypur's frontier position explains why a king built *here*.

* **5.1 Avanti to Mālava** — Ujjayinī, Dhārā and Vidiśā as successive poles. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*  
* **5.2 The succession of powers** — Mauryas to Gurjara-Pratihāras; the sun-worship of Bhillasvāmin at Bhilsa. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*  
* **5.3 Routes** — feeder lines from Besnagar and Mathurā to the Deccan; the Gujarat ports to the west. *(Dynastic · \~800 w)*  
* **5.4 Sacred geography** — Ujjain as both *tīrtha* and capital, sanctity and administration reinforcing each other. *(Scripture \+ Dynastic · \~800 w)*  
* **5.5 The eastern frontier** — facing Kalachuri Tripurī and Chaulukya Gujarat: the strategic logic C7 makes concrete. *(Dynastic · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* independence in 972; Upendra a feudatory of Govinda III.  
 *Leads:* from C4's prehistory → C6 arrives at the dynasty that took the region.  
 *Gap:* the "caravan staging post" characterisation is inference, not cited evidence — present it as such.

### **C6 — The Paramāras and Bhoja: Kingship, Learning and Temple Building *(How Udaypur was formed)***

*Premise:* Bhoja made Malwa a centre of learning and left a treatise on architecture; the Bhūmija style was a deliberate act of self-distinction, and Udayāditya inherited both.

* **6.1 Origins and independence** — the fire-born (agnikula) myth as dynastic argument; Sīyaka II sacks Mānyakheṭa. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*  
* **6.2 Muñja and Sindhurāja** — the poet-warrior's campaigns and his death against Tailapa II. *(Dynastic · \~750 w)*  
* **6.3 Bhoja the scholar-king** — patronage, the Bhojaśālā, and the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, the corpus's single largest source. *(Dynastic \+ Monograph · \~950 w)*  
* **6.4 Why Bhūmija** — Hardy's argument that the style was chosen "to distinguish themselves from rival dynasties patronising mainly Shekhari temples." *(Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **6.5 The crisis of 1055** — Bhoja's death amid a joint Chaulukya–Kalachuri attack; Jayasiṃha I falls around 1070\. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*

*Committed:* Bhoja 1000–1055; Muñja 973–995; Sindhurāja 995–1000; Jayasiṃha I 1055–1070.  
 *Leads:* from C5's region → C7 opens at the dynasty's lowest point.  
 *Gap:* the anti-Ghaznavid confederacy is a genuinely open question — take a working position in one line.

### **C7 — Udayāditya's Foundation: Town, Temple and Tank**

*Premise:* the town, temple and tank were founded as one endowment in 1059, eleven years before their founder became king.

* **7.1 The man** — the nephew from a junior branch: pedigree, position, the territory he already held. *(Dynastic \+ Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **7.2 The triple foundation** — Udayapura, Udayeśvara and Udayasamudra named together; begun in VS 1116\. *(Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **7.3 Founding before kingship** — building while still ruling his paternal territory; the Panhera inscription has Jayasiṃha still ruling in 1059\. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*  
* **7.4 Recovering the kingdom** — Durlabha III's cavalry; the Caulukya–Karṇāṭa occupation broken; a realm reaching Jhalrapatan, Bhilsa and Nimar. *(Dynastic · \~850 w)*  
* **7.5 The interrupted programme** — the abandoned second temple: the pillar-bases and the unfinished colossus (described from the object). *(Survey · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* 1059 foundation; accession 1070; death c. 1093; flagstaff 1080; still reigning 1086; Udaysagar 71 acres.  
 *Leads:* from C6's crisis → C8 enters the building.

### **C8 — Udayeśvara in Stone: Plan, Structure and Style *(Neelkantheshwar Temple Architecture)***

*Premise:* the temple has no surviving precedent yet arrives fully formed as the most elaborate Bhūmija ever built — so the style must have matured elsewhere, in buildings now lost.

* **8.1 The ensemble** — east-facing red sandstone: sanctum, vestibule, a hall with three porches, a separate screened pavilion, a lofty platform. *(Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **8.2 The tower** — four vertical bands in a lacework of arch-motifs; seven tiers of miniature spires; the climbing flag-bearer figure. *(Monograph · \~950 w)*  
* **8.3 Hardy's verdict** — "already the deluxe model": a 32-point star, where Nemawar's later attempt at nine tiers failed. *(Monograph · \~850 w)*  
* **8.4 Geometry and the texts** — the rotation of the square within a circle, and what the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* prescribes. *(Monograph · \~850 w)*  
* **8.5 Comparison** — Un, Nemawar, Bijamandal and Bhojpur: the survivals and the three imperial monuments. *(Monograph · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* eight subsidiary shrines; a 32-point star; seven tiers; built 1059–1080.  
 *Leads:* from C7's foundation → C9 moves from structure to the figures carved on it.

### **C9 — Gods on the Walls: Sculpture, Iconography and Meaning *(Deities and Symbolism)***

*Premise:* the image programme is a systematic Śaiva argument across the whole building — and Naṭarāja appears twice at Udaypur, once on the tower-front and once colossal at the hill's foot.

* **9.1 The tower-front dancer** — Naṭeśa dancing in the raised-knee pose, flanked by dancing goddesses including a rare dancing Sarasvatī. *(Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **9.2 The directional programme** — the guardians of the directions, Mahābhairava, Sūrya, Umā-Maheśvara; Cāmuṇḍā concentrated in the northern quarter. *(Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **9.3 The fierce forms** — the Seven Mothers, Vīrabhadra and Dakṣa, Bhṛṅgī: a mythic narrative read across the wall surfaces. *(Monograph · \~800 w)*  
* **9.4 The hill Naṭarāja** — from the object itself: six arms, its attributes, reclining, unfinished, over Apasmāra, inscribed. *(Survey \+ Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **9.5 Dress and ornament** — sculpture as primary evidence for costume where texts are silent. *(Monograph · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* the Naṭarāja is 8.2 × 4.2 m, six-armed, eleventh century, state-protected — never "the world's largest."  
 *Leads:* from C8's fabric → C10 turns from carved figures to carved words.

### **C10 — Words in Stone: Inscriptions, Languages and the Serpentine Scimitar of Letters**

*Premise:* Udaypur is an epigraphic archive in four languages across eight centuries — and one of its inscriptions is a grammar chart shaped like a sword.

* **10.1 The Udaipur Prashasti** — the genealogy, the fire-born myth, the "rising sun" verse, read as panegyric in the published translation. *(Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **10.2 The porch archive** — the VS 1116 record; Devapāla's grants of 1229 and 1232 and the treasurer Dhamadeyava; the 1323–24 votive; the 1338 procession; the pilgrims of 1377 and 1446\. *(Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **10.3 The serpentine scimitar** — the grammar-serpent naming "kings Udayāditya and Naravarman," with its cousins at Ujjain, Dhar and Un. *(Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **10.4 Reading panegyric critically** — what a *praśasti* yields and what it withholds: genealogy against event, formula against fact. *(Epigraphy · \~850 w)*  
* **10.5 Four languages, one site** — Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian and Hindi; the 1645 slab dated in three eras at once. *(Epigraphy · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* VS 1116 \= 1059; VS 1137 \= 1080; grants of 1229 and 1232; the 1338 procession; the baoli of 13 January 1645\.  
 *Leads:* from C9's images → C11 steps outside the temple wall.  
 *Gap:* no edited inscription text is in the corpus — the standard epigraphic editions must be obtained for the readings.

### **C11 — Built Heritage: Fortifications, Mosques, Shrines and Stepwells *(Other Structures besides the temple)***

*Premise:* the temple is one building in a town of many, and the hill fort above it is older than the dynasty that made the town famous.

* **11.1 The hill fort** — wall, bastions, a single gate, the enclosed area — one set of figures, from the survey. *(Survey · \~950 w)*  
* **11.2 Gates and palace** — the fortified Mahal and its Teen Darwaza; the Chanderi, Moti and Purana Bazaar gateways; the memorial pavilions. *(Survey · \~800 w)*  
* **11.3 Islamic architecture** — the Tughluq mosque west of the temple; the Shahi Mahal and Masjid to the east, 1616–1632. *(Epigraphy \+ Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **11.4 Water architecture** — the Qanungo Baoli, the Ghod Dod Baoli, and the local reckoning of fifty-six stepwells, fifty-two wells and twelve and a half tanks. *(Survey \+ Testimony · \~800 w)*  
* **11.5 Dating the undated** — oral tradition, INTACH's "early tenth century," and masonry typology, weighed before a position is taken. *(Survey · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* wall 1,400 m, dry-stone with an earthen rampart and no binder; 56 acres; the fort predates Udayāditya; Tughluq mosque AH 737/739; Shahi Mahal 1616–1632.  
 *Leads:* from C10's inscriptions on other buildings → C12 asks what was believed inside them.  
 *Gap:* do not import Mandu's Moti Darwaza or Sher Khan stepwell — they are not at Udaypur.

### **C12 — Ritual and Knowledge: Śaiva Traditions and Intellectual Worlds *(Culture and Way of Life)***

*Premise:* the temple was built inside a specific theology, and its image programme states that theology.

* **12.1 Śaiva Siddhānta at Udaypur** — Pande's argument that the tower-front maps the "pure path" of the system. *(Monograph · \~950 w)*  
* **12.2 Sects and lineages** — the Pāśupata, Lākulīśa, Kāpālika and Siddhānta streams; the Lakulīśa panel on the hill. *(Monograph · \~850 w)*  
* **12.3 Bhoja's theology** — the *Tattvaprakāśa*, a king writing Śaiva metaphysics, joining learning to building. *(Monograph · \~800 w)*  
* **12.4 Ritual practice** — consecration and the raising of the flag; the ritual-manual prescriptions for consecrating a temple. *(Monograph · \~800 w)*  
* **12.5 Plural devotion** — Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Jain patronage side by side; the Harihara image; the hill's Jain cave. *(Monograph · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* consecration 1080; a Śaiva Siddhānta affiliation.  
 *Leads:* from C11's plural fabric → C13 turns from belief to subsistence.  
 *Note:* medicine enters only as far as Bhoja's attributed texts are actually attested.

### **C13 — Fields, Wells and Markets: Agriculture, Economy and Crafts**

*Premise:* a temple town of this scale required a surplus; quarries, black soil, tanks and grain grants explain how it was made.

* **13.1 Soil and crops** — black cotton soil; wheat and barley in the winter crop, millets and pulses in the monsoon crop. *(Gazetteer · \~850 w)*  
* **13.2 Water for fields** — stepwells and wells in the grants; lifts; the release of water from tanks. *(Gazetteer \+ Survey · \~800 w)*  
* **13.3 The quarry economy** — sandstone as the frontier town's strategic asset, the clearest economic argument available. *(Survey \+ Testimony · \~850 w)*  
* **13.4 Grants and revenue** — the king's share and the produce dues; temple-endowment land; Devapāla's grant and the treasurer Dhamadeyava. *(Epigraphy · \~850 w)*  
* **13.5 Crafts and guilds** — sculptors, master-builders and stone-cutters; the weavers' guilds; madder and indigo. *(Dynastic · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* Devapāla's grant of 1229\.  
 *Leads:* from C12's institutions → C14 turns to the people in the economy.  
 *Gap:* the Paramāra land-grant corpus is absent, and the village names in the heritage profile lack quoted readings.

### **C14 — People of the Plateau: Communities, Languages and Everyday Life**

*Premise:* the people living at Udaypur now are not incidental to its history; the neighbourhood (*mohallā*) structure is the settlement pattern still in use.

* **14.1 The town's quarters** — Shikari, Mahua, Chaudaryana, Sarenthi and Utranti; the older settlement near Hajariya Mahadev. *(Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **14.2 Communities** — the castes and communities of the district; the artisan groups the gazetteer names. *(Gazetteer · \~850 w)*  
* **14.3 Demography** — population, households and literacy: what a 2011 count can and cannot say about an eleventh-century town. *(Gazetteer · \~800 w)*  
* **14.4 Languages** — Hindi with Bundeli and Malvi; Sanskrit as the language of record against the vernacular of speech. *(Gazetteer · \~750 w)*  
* **14.5 The offices of the Paramāra town** — headman, council and merchant-elders: titles attested regionally, not at Udaypur by name. *(Epigraphy · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* 6,383 residents, 1,247 households, 54.28% literacy (2011).  
 *Leads:* from C13's economy → C15 moves from who the people are to what they tell.  
 *Gap:* the medieval half rests on regional analogy, not Udaypur evidence — say so plainly.

### **C15 — Stories of Udaypur: Festivals, Folklore and Oral Histories**

*Premise:* what the town remembers differs from what the inscriptions record, and the difference is itself evidence.

* **15.1 The living temple** — the daily worship; the "house of the gong-ringers," whose family struck the gong to keep ritual time; Śivarātri and the Mondays of Śrāvaṇa. *(Monograph \+ Testimony · \~850 w)*  
* **15.2 Founding legends** — Bhrangarajpur and the defeated chief: how a conquest becomes an origin story. *(Testimony · \~800 w)*  
* **15.3 The dancer of Udaysagar** — the rope-walker promised half a kingdom, a tale encoding a real anxiety about royal promises. *(Testimony · \~800 w)*  
* **15.4 A town that narrates itself** — VMT's reportage as a source: partisan, first-person, invaluable, and handled as testimony throughout. *(Testimony · \~900 w)*  
* **15.5 Memory against the record** — folk chronology set beside epigraphic chronology, and what each is for. *(Testimony \+ Epigraphy · \~750 w)*

*Committed:* everything here is testimony, attributed to a named teller; no medieval fact rests on it.  
 *Leads:* from C14's communities → C16 turns to the rupture that ended the Paramāra town.  
 *Gap:* no recorded oral-history interviews exist; the chapter rests on one book.

### **C16 — Conquests and Regimes: The Decline of a Socio-Economic Hub of Madhya Bharat**

*Premise:* Udaypur outlasted the dynasty that made it; its demotion from royal town to *qasba* to village is the book's second subject.

* **16.1 The later Paramāras** — Naravarman down to Devapāla; the wars with Hoysalas, Yādavas and Chaulukyas. *(Dynastic · \~900 w)*  
* **16.2 1305 — the state ends** — Alāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī before Malwa's gates; Mahlak Deo defeated; the 1310 record as residual authority. *(Dynastic \+ Epigraphy · \~900 w)*  
* **16.3 Tughluq Udaypur** — the mosque of AH 737/739; the reuse of carved material; the temple left standing. *(Epigraphy · \~850 w)*  
* **16.4 Continuity under new rule** — the 1338 procession record: religious life continuing beside a new administration. *(Epigraphy · \~800 w)*  
* **16.5 Sultanate and Mughal town** — a *qasba* in the Chanderi district; the Shahi Mahal; the Qanungo Baoli and its Kāyastha builders. *(Epigraphy · \~800 w)*

*Committed:* the 1293 Bhilsa raid; the 1305 conquest; the 1310 residual inscription; AH 737/739; 1616–1632; 1645\.  
 *Leads:* from C15's living memory → C17 arrives when the town becomes an object of study.  
 *Gap:* published readings of the 1336–39 inscriptions are needed; the *hadith* text and the reuse attribution are unquoted.

### **C17 — Archaeologists, Laws and Repairs: Making Udaypur a "Monument"; Udaypur in Independent India**

*Premise:* protection saved the temple and detached it from the town; the paperwork of preservation is also a history of separation.

* **17.1 The antiquarians** — Cunningham and Beglar; Beglar's Aurangzeb error and Cunningham's correction. *(Monograph · \~900 w)*  
* **17.2 Scindia-era repair** — the brass face-cover given to the *liṅga* in 1775: patronage before "conservation" existed. *(Monograph · \~800 w)*  
* **17.3 Becoming protected** — what ASI protection covers and what it omits, notably the hill, which is Forest Department land. *(Survey · \~900 w)*  
* **17.4 Documentation as an act** — INTACH's two volumes: some fifty listed sites, then the instrument survey of the fortification. *(Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **17.5 The costs of becoming a monument** — protection froze the temple and orphaned everything around it: the book's central irony. *(\~750 w)*

*Committed:* the brass cover of 1775 (Khanderao Appaji, general of Mahadji Scindia); the Naṭarāja state-protected; the hill under the Forest Department.  
 *Leads:* from C16's regime change → C18 arrives in the present.  
 *Gap:* no ASI notification, protected-area boundary or post-1947 repair record exists in any source — these must be obtained before drafting.

### **C18 — Present Tense: How the Town Lives with Its Past Today *(based on Udaypur Jagta Hua Kasba)***

*Premise:* several thousand people live beside a royal temple in a town that has been a village for six centuries.

* **18.1 A kasba on the margin** — VMT's opening: a town of eight thousand with a thousand years of stories and nobody listening. *(Testimony · \~850 w)*  
* **18.2 Living in the fabric** — dry-stone houses and courtyards; gateways narrowed into lanes; carved fragments lying in the streets. *(Survey \+ Testimony · \~850 w)*  
* **18.3 Services and livelihoods** — roads, schools, water and power; farming and quarrying as work; the young leaving. *(Gazetteer · \~800 w)*  
* **18.4 Custodians and officials** — the 2019 letter to the Collector and a resident's charge against the archaeology department, attributed as testimony. *(Testimony · \~850 w)*  
* **18.5 Two Udaypurs** — the monument's visitors and the village's residents share the ground and rarely meet. *(\~750 w)*

*Committed:* 6,383 (2011) growing to about 8,000 (VMT's estimate, c. 2021–22) — growth, not contradiction.  
 *Leads:* from C17's monumentalisation → the Afterword names the remedy.  
 *Gap:* no fieldwork exists — interviews with residents, the ASI custodian, the panchayat and the priests are required.

### **Afterword — Conservation Today, the Village's Struggles, and How to Do Better**

*Premise:* the hill is collapsing while the temple is protected, and the case for acting is strongest where the evidence is newest.

* **AW.1 Documented deterioration** — collapsed bastions, slope erosion, a wall whose decay "has entered a critical phase." *(Survey · \~900 w)*  
* **AW.2 Illegal mining** — dynamite blasts near the Naṭarāja and the wall, which the Forest and Mining Acts already prohibit. *(Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **AW.3 The protection gap** — a protected temple beside an unprotected hill under another department, carrying unprotected sculpture. *(Survey · \~850 w)*  
* **AW.4 Proposals and their limits** — INTACH's geo-tourism plan and the Kashi Vishwanath comparison VMT reports, tested against what is realistic here. *(Survey \+ Testimony · \~800 w)*  
* **AW.5 The argument** — what is owed to a place that was a capital and is now a village: advocacy, labelled as such. *(\~750 w)*

*Committed:* illegal sandstone mining is the principal active threat.  
 *Leads:* from C18's "two Udaypurs" → closes the book.  
 *Gap:* nothing postdates the INTACH survey; current ASI condition reports are needed.

## **Note for the writing pipeline**

When a chapter is drafted, the writer receives the chapter plan above together with the full editorial canon, which is binding rather than advisory — a draft that re-opens a settled date, lineage or measurement has failed. In practice this means: search the index afresh for each section rather than trusting the samples here; always exclude *Temple Economics*, which otherwise surfaces as a false match; read the chunks on either side of any hit, since passages straddle boundaries; obey the source matrix, so that a measurement drafted from testimony, or a medieval fact from the undated gazetteer, is returned as a routing error; and describe every signature object from the object itself, never from the generic image.
