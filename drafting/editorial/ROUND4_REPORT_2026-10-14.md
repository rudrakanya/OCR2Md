### Ch1 §1.1 (host of 1.1 P1)

**1.1 The village under the temple**

▶ About ten kilometres along the approach from Ganj Basoda, hillocks appear on the horizon; two belong to Udaypur, including a large cylindrical hill known as Shiv pindi. [C01-KB-GEOHER-004] The temple comes into view before arrival in the village, a landmark in Vijay Manohar Tiwari’s testimony, *Jagta Hua Kasba*. [C01-JAGTA-EN-013] Udaypur itself is a large village in Basoda tahsil of Vidisha district, with Ganj Basoda to the south-west and Vidisha farther in the same direction. [C01-GAZ-007; C01-GAZ-008] Its nearest railway station is Bareth, about 6.5 kilometres to the west. [C01-GAZ-008] The village contains Hindu and Mohammedan monuments left by a town of considerably greater medieval importance. [C01-MADHYABHARAT-009]

Modern Udaypur is a *panchayat*, a unit of village government, in Ganj Basoda tahsil. [C01-GEOHER-002] Its population in 2011 was 6,383, and it has about 1,247 houses. [C01-GEOHER-003; C01-KB-GEOHER-009] In 1979 the village had primary and higher secondary schools, Ayurvedic and veterinary dispensaries, and a post office; its weekly market took place on Mondays. [C01-KB-GAZ-002] These are the institutions and numbers of an ordinary large village. The village occupies low ground beneath an overlooking hill, visible from within its precinct. [C01-KB-GEOHER-003] What needs explaining is the scale of the older town within which these houses and institutions stand.

Residents have built their homes close against enormous old gateways, leaving narrow lanes where broader routes once passed. [C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-001] They gather in the courtyards of dwellings built with locally obtained stone, many of them using dry masonry. [C01-KB-GEOHER-001] Around the standing gateways, rows of older shops are filled with rubble; openings in decaying houses reveal courtyards, rooms and verandas. [C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-003] Shopkeepers sell clothing, groceries, fruit and food along the lanes. [C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-002] The old fortified enclosure no longer survives as a continuous circuit, though stretches of wall and several gateways remain, including *Teen Darwaza*, the triple gateway leading towards the former royal square. [C01-KB-GEOHER-002] Families also continue to use buildings whose extent exceeds the space they can maintain: one occupied house spreads across twelve courtyards, most of them ruined, with a temple on the north side of its central courtyard. [C01-KB-INTACH-001] Present-day habitation has narrowed some of the old routes without removing their gateways from everyday use.

Near the temple stands *Ghariyālon Kā Makān*, the house of the time-keepers, who claim to have been there for centuries. [C01-PANDE-006] Tiwari encountered the Ghadiyali family’s recollection of bells and gongs sounded from its building at the appointed times for worship, ablution and other rites. [C01-JAGTA-EN-024] Elsewhere, village memory preserves a tally of fifty-six stepwells, fifty-two wells and twelve and a half lakes—a remembered abundance rather than a verified inventory. [C01-GEOHER-012] Tiwari’s account joins present-day black-soil fields to the memory of a vast tank built by Udayāditya. [C01-JAGTA-HI-009] Another local tradition places a dancer at Udayāditya’s court; Berni Mata is associated with a reclining sculpture on a lake embankment, where worship continues at a temple. [C01-GEOHER-013; C01-GEOHER-014] In a story attached to a vermilion-smeared image, the dancer attempted to dance on a thin thread stretched across a reservoir; someone broke it, she fell to her death, and the image is regarded as her funerary memorial. [C01-JAGTA-EN-033] {{note: Narayan Vyas identified the image examined during Tiwari’s visit as a *yakṣa*, a spirit-being. [C01-JAGTA-EN-044]}} The age of that story is unknown. [C01-JAGTA-EN-033] These recollections give the temple’s rituals a place in family history and attach the founder’s name to fields, waterworks and a dancer’s fate; they do not establish events in his reign.

Morning and evening worship continues in the Udayeśvara temple, which is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India. [C01-PANDE-010; C01-GEOHER-005] Hindus and Muhammadans frequent the shrine and revere its *liṅga*, the emblem of Śiva. [C01-PANDE-004] People come daily from far away, most for the worship of Mahadev, while an annual fair draws thousands, including people from surrounding districts. [C01-JAGTA-EN-025; C01-JAGTA-EN-030] Tiwari found little interest in history among many of those living amid these remains, even as worship continued to bring people to the temple. [C01-KB-JAGTA-EN-007; C01-JAGTA-EN-025] Beyond the village, its name often directs attention elsewhere, to Udaipur in Rajasthan. [C01-JAGTA-EN-039] Yet a local founding legend remembers Udayāditya giving his own name to the city and temple he built. [C01-GANGULY-012] The village retains that royal name and a living shrine, while the king has a less distinct place in its historical knowledge than in its stories.

### Ch2 §2.1 (host of old 2.1 P5, now 2.1 P5)

**2.1 Where the water runs, and where it does not**

The Betwa flows past Vidisha, the Bhilsa of the Paramāra records, in which the river appears as the Vētravatī. [C02-CII-001; C02-CII-002] At Besnagar, the ancient city’s site occupies the fork between the Betwa and the Bes. [C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001] In Bhoja’s reign, Bhilsa marked the eastern limit of the kingdom of Malwa. [C02-KB-GANGULY-002] {{note: Ganguly reconstructed this eastern limit of Bhoja’s dominion from the inscriptions together with Al Bērūnī’s statement about Bhilsa in 1030. [C02-KB-GANGULY-002]}} Udaypur was an important centre of Paramāra government within the division bearing Bhilsa’s name. [C01-GANGULY-005] By the third quarter of the twelfth century, it was described as belonging to the *maṇḍala*, or province, of Bhāillasvāmi-mahādvādaśaka. [C01-KB-GANGULY-016] This was a political connection to the river’s great centre, not a shared position on its banks: Udaypur’s immediate surroundings lack a river through the inhabited place. [C02-GEOHER-001; C02-GEOHER-002]

The Betwa rises in the main Vindhyan range in the south-west of Raisen district and enters Vidisha district to the south of the city. [C02-GAZ-001] Across the district, the ground slopes generally from south to north, the direction followed by its major streams; the whole district belongs to the Yamuna’s drainage basin. [C02-GAZ-002] Udaypur’s nearest river connection is the Kewtan, a Betwa tributary flowing northwards to join the main river at Kotha Barrage. [C02-GEOHER-002] It runs 1.8 km from Shiva Pindi and 1 km from the village. [C02-GEOHER-002] The river network thus passes close to Udaypur without giving the village a river of its own. [C02-GEOHER-001; C02-GEOHER-002] Proximity alone leaves the essential problem unresolved. A stream within reach is useful only for as long as water remains available in it.

Today the upper Betwa is largely intermittent, becoming perennial over its final 85 km before joining the Yamuna. [C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001] Its flow begins to rise in June and falls after September, remaining relatively low before and after the monsoon. [C02-BETWA-FLOW-003] Across the basin, nearly 80 per cent of annual runoff occurs during the monsoon. [C02-BETWA-CLIM-004] These figures are used here to describe the modern regime, not to reconstruct the water supply of an earlier reign. [C02-BETWA-FLOW-003; C02-BETWA-CLIM-004] The modern upper river nevertheless makes the distinction between a drainage basin and a dependable water supply plain: much of the year’s flow passes during the wet season.

Below ground, Udaypur has another limitation. [C02-GEOHER-003; C02-KB-GAZ-004] Much of the terrain around it consists of sandstone, while wells in the Vindhyan formations generally dry up in summer and have water levels more than 15 m below the surface. [C02-GEOHER-003; C02-KB-GAZ-004] The neighbouring trap country offers a different prospect: groundwater storage there depends on fissures, dykes and the degree to which the rock has weathered and broken down. [C02-GAZ-043] Shallow wells in that country generally meet demand, except during the summer of a drought year. [C02-KB-GAZ-010] That relative advantage cannot simply be transferred to Udaypur’s sandstone. [C02-KB-GAZ-010; C02-GEOHER-003; C02-KB-GAZ-004] In the district’s 1979 setting, the Public Health Engineering Department operated a water-supply scheme at Vidisha drawing from the Betwa; no other town in the district had waterworks. [C02-GAZ-053] Elsewhere, people used wells, with some villages also taking water directly from rivers or streams. [C02-GAZ-053] Village councils and Development Blocks constructed new wells and repaired old ones to maintain rural drinking-water supplies. [C02-KB-GAZ-011] Such work addressed a recurring practical need, but the sandstone around Udaypur remained an uncertain store of groundwater even as quarrymen extracted it widely for stone. [C02-KB-GAZ-004; C02-GEOHER-003]

▶ Udaypur’s more dependable places for collecting water lie at the surface, in the natural depressions occupied by lakes and ponds. [C02-GEOHER-001] Around the inhabited higher ground, these hollows form part of a regional pattern linking village, water body and cultivated land; streets follow the fall of the ground and carry drainage towards the surrounding fields. [C02-GEOHER-028] Village and farmland therefore share more than a boundary. Water leaving the higher ground can be gathered below it, rather than being lost from use at once. The hollows provide places to retain it beyond the wet months; replenishing those stores depends on the return of the monsoon. [C02-GEOHER-001; C02-GEOHER-028; C02-BETWA-CLIM-004]

### Ch2 §2.3 (host of old 2.3 P2, now 2.3 P2)

**2.3 A seam of stone**

Udaypur stands among the western foothills of the Garhi–Teonda range, on the uneven country of the Vindhyan plateau. [C02-KB-GAZ-009; C02-KB-GAZ-008; C02-GEOHER-006] Its ground lies between 340 and 480 m above sea level, with a mean elevation of 410 m. [C02-GEOHER-005] The plateau slopes northwards, sending spurs towards the north and north-east; rivers have cut their valleys between these ridges. [C02-GAZ-007] The Garhi–Teonda range runs from south to north between the Betwa and Bina valleys, separating two drainage corridors that belong to the same river system. [C02-KB-GAZ-008; C02-GAZ-007] Udaypur occupies the foothill country above those valleys, where the exposed rock differs from much of the surrounding district. [C02-KB-GAZ-009; C02-GAZ-008]

▶ Across Vidisha district, the most widespread formation is the Deccan Trap: basalt laid down in horizontal or nearly horizontal lava flows, whose dark successive layers give the countryside a terraced appearance. [C02-GAZ-010] Erosion removed this cover from a large part of the Garhi–Teonda range, exposing hard sedimentary rocks beneath it, including sandstone and limestone. [C02-GAZ-008; C02-KB-GAZ-008] Around Udaypur, sandstone accounts for most of the terrain and is widely quarried today. [C02-GEOHER-003] The builders of Udayeśvara worked this local resource, constructing the temple entirely from hard, fine-grained red sandstone quarried in a nearby hill. [C02-PANDE-001] They were building within a wider region of stone-working: buildings at Vidisha and Gyaraspur also preserve extensive early use of sandstone and limestone. [C02-GAZ-050] The exposed beds offered materials suited to different kinds of work. [C02-GAZ-042] Across the district, the stronger Kaimur sandstone has been used for massive structures, while softer sandstone lends itself to delicate carving; most quarries work Kaimur or Rewa beds. [C02-GAZ-042] That regional distinction does not establish which beds supplied Udayeśvara, but it helps explain the range of work possible in sandstone. [C02-PANDE-001; C02-GAZ-042]

The hills themselves are interpreted as remnants of a wasting tableland, with resistant rock above weaker beds. [C02-GEOHER-008] Udaypur’s flat-lying sandstones are medium- to coarse-grained and commonly cross-bedded; between them lie thin horizons of poorly cemented sandstone, marl, claystone and other fine-grained sediments more vulnerable to weathering. [C02-GEOHER-009] In the geomorphological model applied to this country, retreating cliffs leave broad, flat-topped remnants, or mesas, which may diminish into smaller buttes and eventually pinnacles. [C02-GEOHER-008] This is a proposed sequence, not an established history of every hill: fragmentation may also leave groups of isolated crags or labyrinths of rock. [C02-GEOHER-008] The Udaypur mesa has two levels, with an intermediate bench between its lower cliff line and the upper caprock. [C02-GEOHER-010] Shiva Pindi, the large mesa, has a measured summit elevation of 567.66 m; the smaller rock hill identified as a butte reaches 582.31 m above sea level. [C02-GEOHER-015; C02-GEOHER-014] {{note: The survey also uses relative-height descriptions with different reference points: Udaypur Hill is described as rising about 100 m above the surrounding flatland, while the introductory account describes a hill rising almost 270 m above village level. These descriptions cannot safely be combined with the summit measurements into a single height profile. [C02-GEOHER-010; C02-GEOHER-020]} The survey proposes rock falls as a means by which the caprock breaks back, reducing the extent of the surviving hill. [C02-GEOHER-011]

The contrast between strong and weak beds is visible at a smaller scale in overhangs formed where weaker sandstone has weathered beneath the caprock. [C02-KB-GEOHER-010] The thickly bedded quartz-sandstone cap remains comparatively little divided along its joints, with few deep, penetrable clefts. [C02-KB-GEOHER-008] Shiva Pindi nevertheless contains natural caves as well as cavities made by people. [C02-GEOHER-016] Some curved features at the site may mark runoff cutting through softer material, although that explanation remains a proposal. [C02-GEOHER-012] Water has thus been considered among the agents shaping the hill, but its presence in a model of erosion should not be confused with a continuing supply: the valleys on the mesa are dry and lack channels. [C02-GEOHER-012; C02-GEOHER-010]

At the time of the district Gazetteer’s compilation in 1979, forests covered about two-fifths of Vidisha district, chiefly on hill ranges and steep slopes, with smaller patches beside watercourses. [C02-GAZ-027] Woodland grew on trap and Vindhyan sandstone alike, but the sandstone forests of the Shamshabad and Gyaraspur ranges were comparatively poor, rooted in shallow sandy loam and almost without teak. [C02-GAZ-027; C02-GAZ-012] These were working woods: timber and fuelwood were the district’s principal forest products, alongside bamboo, grass, gums and other produce. [C02-GAZ-037] In the Sironj and Lateri ranges, grass reserves were auctioned annually for cutting, making the use of open woodland a formally managed part of the rural economy. [C02-GAZ-032]

Udaypur’s hill is entered as reserved forest in revenue records and the Forest Department’s working plan, but its vegetation is disturbed by grazing and illegal tree-cutting. [C02-KB-GEOHER-012; C02-GEOHER-021] Dense local grasses form part of its present cover, among trees that include tamarind. [C02-GEOHER-019] The hill is therefore still being worked, through uses that remove vegetation as well as stone. [C02-GEOHER-021; C02-GEOHER-003] Its exposed sandstone supplied the temple’s builders; the dry upper valleys offered no comparable store of water. [C02-PANDE-001; C02-GEOHER-010]

## 6. Hand-off from the new Chapter 2 to Chapter 3 (draft v1)

The new Chapter 2 ends with 2.5 P2: what the land allowed, and what it did not decide, and that this country was used long before Udaypur had a name. Chapter 3 v1 opens on Shiva Pindi's caves and the Palaeolithic tools of the Betwa valleys, so **the hand-off works in direction**. Five things in Chapter 3 need changing:
1. **3.1, first sentence** ("Udaypur's Shiva Pindi hill contains both natural and man-made caves"). The new 2.3 introduces the caves. Ch3 should open on the earliest human traces and refer to the caves as already known.
2. **3.2, first sentence** ("…within the river landscape outlined in Chapter 2"). This is meta-commentary, and the new 2.1 P1 already gives Vidisha in the fork of the Bes and Betwa. Cut the clause and do not re-explain the fork.

Ch3 states them as "local evidence of a pre-Paramāra structure", which hardens a proposal. Recast them as proposals and refer back; do not re-argue the founding question.
5. **3.5, close.** Chapter 1's 1.2 now introduces the Paramāras as a polity. Ch3's closing survey of the dynasties of the tenth to twelfth centuries should hand on to Chapter 4 without re-introducing Dhārā or Bhilsa as the eastern limit.
