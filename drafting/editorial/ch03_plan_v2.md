# Chapter 3 — Before Udaypur: Prehistory, Early Vidisha and the Historical Landscape of Central India — chapter plan v2

*Rebuilt after the editor-in-chief audit (14 October 2026), on the principles approved for Chapters 1 and 2. This plan replaces outline v1 (`ch03/ch03_outline.md`, kept for the record). No chapter is split or merged; the chapter's scope is as in `chapters.md`.*

**Historical question.** What was already in this country when a king put his name on Udaypur's hill, and how much of that long past can be found at the hill itself?

**Through-line.** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur's hill stood in an old, named country whose centre, roads and carved shrines lay elsewhere and reached the hill only through proposals, and that by the tenth century its own range had become a crowded frontier.

**Arc.**
- 3.1: the oldest traces, which are district finds and not Udaypur's; the country already had names, and its centre lay elsewhere.
- 3.2: that centre, Vidisha, a walled city and a Śuṅga capital on crossing roads for a thousand years.
- 3.3: a landscape of carved stone and early shrines, from Besnagar's Vāsudeva shrine and the Greek envoy's pillar onwards. It reaches Udaypur's own range by the fifth century, and the hill itself only through proposals and Tiwari's reading.
- 3.4: the old city gives way to Bhilsa, named for a Sun image. Its name is the one the province around Udaypur would carry.
- 3.5: by the eighth to tenth centuries, Udaypur's range was a frontier crowded with temple towns and contested by three dynasties.

The chapter ends there and hands on to the Paramāras' arrival (Chapter 4).

**Centre of gravity.** Udaypur is the subject. Vidisha and Central India enter only where they change what Udaypur means:
- the country it stood in;
- the roads and sacred traditions near it;
- the name of its later province;
- the frontier on its own range.

What the old centre was, it was without Udaypur. The chapter never implies that Udaypur shared in it.

**What can and cannot be said about prehistory.** The evidence is three items from the 1979 Gazetteer:
- palaeolithic tools from the valleys of the Betwa, Bina, Dhasan and Keothan;
- a chalcolithic mound "of the District", unnamed and unlocated, dated 1000–700 BC;
- the 1963–65 explorations around Vidisha.

Context retrieval found nothing more. Nothing prehistoric is recorded on or near Udaypur's hill, and its caves have no recorded early use. The chapter says this once and plainly, in a single paragraph, and does not pad it. The Keothan is not identified with the Kewtan of Chapter 2: the items do not say they are the same.

## 3.1 Old ground, older names *(Prehistory and the first names · 550 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the limits of the oldest evidence and what the country was called before Udaypur existed:
- stone tools from the valleys of the Betwa and its tributaries;
- a chalcolithic mound somewhere in the district, and a sequence around Vidisha running from the Chalcolithic to the early medieval;
- nothing prehistoric recorded on Udaypur's own hill;
- the country's names: Daśārṇa in literary tradition and Ākara in inscriptions. Vidisha was its capital, so the hill stood in a named country whose centre was elsewhere.

**Purpose:** Begin where Chapter 2 ended, on a country used long before Udaypur had a name, and say honestly how little of that use reaches the hill.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Tools in the valleys, nothing on the hill.**
   - **Point:**
     - The oldest human traces in this country are district finds: palaeolithic choppers, handaxes and cleavers from the valleys of the Betwa, Bina, Dhasan and Keothan.
     - A chalcolithic mound in the district, its wares dated 1000–700 BC, also yielded microliths.
     - Explorations around Vidisha in 1963–65 found cultures from the Chalcolithic to the early medieval.

     Nothing prehistoric is recorded on Udaypur's hill. Its caves, already familiar from the hill's geology, have no recorded early use. Say this once, as a limit of the evidence and not as a mystery. Do not locate the mound, and do not equate the Keothan with any stream near Udaypur.
   - E: C03-GAZ-002, C03-GAZ-001, C03-GAZ-003. C: C03-KB-GEOHER-001.
2. **P2 — A named country.**
   - **Point:**
     - The country had names long before the town. In literary geography it was Daśārṇa, from the Buddha's day to Kālidāsa's, with Vidisha as its capital; the name survives in the Dhasan, which joins the Betwa.
     - Inscriptions call eastern Malwa Ākara, distinct from Avanti in the west. The Nasik praśasti lists both among the lands a Sātavāhana king claimed from Nahapāna: a claim of conquest, told as such. Vidisha was the capital of the Ākara-janapada.
     - Purāṇic tradition gives the region's first rulers, a Haihaya branch and then the Pradyotas: one sentence, as tradition.

     For Udaypur, this places the hill in a country that was named, ruled and remembered from a centre some way off.
   - E: C03-MADHYABHARAT-018, C03-MADHYABHARAT-019, C03-CII-004, C03-CII-002. C: C03-GAZ-041, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001, C03-MADHYABHARAT-001. X: C03-JAGTA-EN-005.

**Transition:** That centre was a city in the fork of the rivers downstream.

## 3.2 The old centre and its roads *(Vidisha · 600 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows Vidisha as the country's centre for more than a thousand years:
- a walled city lived in from before Northern Black Polished ware to post-Gupta times;
- the Śuṅga capital of Ākara;
- rich from the roads that crossed there.

The roads it stood on still ran in the eleventh century. The city is the measure of what Udaypur was not: the region's old centre, in which the hill had no recorded part.

**Purpose:** The old centre, kept to what it means for Udaypur: the roads and the stone-working traditions of the country it stood in.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A thousand years of city.**
   - **Point:**
     - Besnagar (Vessanagar in the Pali books, Vidisha in Sanskrit) was lived in across six periods, from before Northern Black Polished ware to post-Gupta times.
     - It was walled, probably from the second century BC.
     - Under the Śuṅgas it was the capital of Ākara. In Kālidāsa's play the crown prince Agnimitra governs there: literary memory, told as such.
     - By the Gupta age it was still a prosperous town, and its monuments make the district the richest in antiquities in Madhya Bharat.

     Chapter 2 already set the city in the fork of the rivers: refer to it as "the city in the fork" and do not explain the fork again. Give no distance from Udaypur (Chapter 1 gives the settled range).
   - E: C03-MADHYABHARAT-015, C03-GAZ-037, C03-GAZ-039, C03-MADHYABHARAT-003. C: C03-MADHYABHARAT-017, C03-MADHYABHARAT-010, C03-CII-004. X: C03-JAGTA-EN-001 (Tiwari's sense of Vidisha as the older and larger place: memory, attributed).
2. **P2 — On the roads.**
   - **Point:**
     - The city's wealth came from where it stood, at the crossing of two trade routes: one from Pratiṣṭhāna through Ujjayinī and Vidisha to Kauśāmbī, and a branch down the Betwa valley.
     - It was a town of merchants on the caravan road from Śrāvastī through Kauśāmbī towards Avanti.
     - The great stūpa at Sanchi was raised largely from the donations of Vidisha's merchants, though general belief credits Ashoka. Frame that disagreement around the monument, in a clause.
     - The roads outlived the city: Nemawar lay on the route from the north to the Deccan in the early eleventh century.

     Do not say or imply that Udaypur lay on these routes. No item says so.
   - E: C03-GAZ-061, C03-MADHYABHARAT-029, C03-GAZ-009. C: C03-RAJABHOJ-EN-008, C03-MADHYABHARAT-027, C03-HARDY-001. X: C03-GAZ-063, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-002, C03-CII-006, C03-RAJABHOJ-EN-006.
**Transition:** The city's shrines began a tradition of worship in stone that later reached the hills around Udaypur.

## 3.3 Carved rock and early shrines *(The sacred landscape · 900 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the country around Udaypur as one of the oldest landscapes of worship in stone:
- Besnagar's early Vaiṣṇava shrine, its lime mortar and the Garuḍa pillar a Greek king's envoy set up before it;
- early Śaiva images;
- the Gupta rock-cut shrines at Udayagiri;
- small Gupta-form shrines at Badoh and Ramgarh;
- a fifth-century panel of the Seven Mothers, with a contemporary inscription, cut into the hill between Badoh and Pathari, on the far slope of Udaypur's own range.

On Udaypur's hill the survey dates a cave, with unfinished figures believed to be Jain Tīrthaṅkaras, to the sixth century. Tiwari reads Viṣṇu images found between Muradpur and Sonari as Gupta. These are a proposal and a reading. They place the hill inside that landscape without showing that anyone settled there.

**Purpose:** The stone-and-shrine tradition of the region, carried to Udaypur's range and, only as proposals, to the hill.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Shrines at the old centre.**
   - **Point:**
     - Besnagar has the earliest archaeological evidence of a Vaiṣṇava temple: an elliptical shrine, possibly of timber, with a stone fence and a brick foundation containing the earliest historical lime mortar.
     - Before it, Heliodorus, envoy of the Greek king Antialcidas, set up a Garuḍa-dhvaja to Vāsudeva and declared himself a Bhāgavata. Its date is a range: first or second century BC, with 140 BC from the Greek king's chronology; the readings go in one note. The pillar is of the region's Vindhyan sandstone, the stone Chapter 2 traced to Udaypur's hill: refer back by content.
     - Its colossal free-standing statues are dated anywhere from pre-Mauryan to Śuṅga times; give that as a range.
     - Its images of Durgā, the Seven Mothers and one-faced liṅgas are the earliest Śaiva images in the region. They date to the fourth century AD, or perhaps a century earlier.

     Śaiva worship in stone in this country is centuries older than the Paramāras. State it as a fact of dates, not as a cause of anything at Udaypur. Not a catalogue of antiquities: three things (the shrine, the pillar, the Śaiva images), each in a sentence or two. (Plan note, run 2.)
   - E: C03-GAZ-016, C03-KRAM-1-002, C03-MADHYABHARAT-013, C03-GAZ-028, C03-MADHYABHARAT-012. C: C03-KRAM-1-001, C03-GAZ-027, C03-MADHYABHARAT-006, C03-TEMPLES-IND-001, C03-MADHYABHARAT-007, C03-KRAM-1-003, C03-MADHYABHARAT-004, C03-MADHYABHARAT-008, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-007. Note: C03-MADHYABHARAT-013, C03-GAZ-029. X: C03-MADHYABHARAT-005.
2. **P2 — Gupta kings cut into the hills.**
   - **Point:**
     - The Allahabad inscription claims Samudragupta's victory over the Nāga rulers: a royal claim.
     - Two inscriptions at Udayagiri attest Chandragupta II's sovereignty, one dated Gupta year 82 (AD 401). Its caves include the earliest Brahmanical rock-cut temple and the Varāha panel.
     - Small flat-roofed shrines continuing the early Gupta form have been found at Ramgarh, Chhapara and Badoh in this district.

     Never connect Udayagiri with Udaypur by name.
   - E: C03-MADHYABHARAT-022, C03-GAZ-050, C03-KRAM-1-005, C03-HARDY-002, C03-TEMPLES-IND-004. C: C03-MADHYABHARAT-025, C03-MADHYABHARAT-026, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-010, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-011, C03-KRAM-1-004. X: C03-GAZ-048, C03-JAGTA-EN-006.
3. **P3 — On Udaypur's range.**
   - **Point:**
     - **The nearest dated work:** a fifth-century panel of the Seven Mothers, with a contemporary Sanskrit inscription, cut into the south face of the hill between Badoh and Pathari. Pathari stands near the eastern slopes of the range in whose western foothills Udaypur lies.
     - **Udaypur's own hill:** the survey dates a cave to the sixth century. Its unfinished figures are "believed" to be Jain Tīrthaṅkaras, and its two lines of inscription remain undeciphered. The caves are already known to the reader; this is the one the survey dates.
     - **Tiwari's surmise:** from Viṣṇu images found between Muradpur and Sonari, Tiwari infers that building went on here five centuries before Udayāditya. Attribute it as Tiwari's reading, a matter of memory and interpretation.

     These are a proposal and a reading. They place the hill inside the region's carved landscape. Whether anyone lived on it before the Paramāras is the question Chapter 1 leaves open; refer to it in a clause and do not re-argue it.
   - E: C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-009, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-006, C02-KB-GAZ-009, C03-KB-INTACH-001, C03-KB-INTACH-003, C03-KB-INTACH-002. C: C03-JAGTA-EN-007, C03-KB-GEOHER-001.

**Transition:** Meanwhile the old city itself was emptying.

## 3.4 From Vidisha to Bhilsa *(A new town and its name · 650 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things about the change from Vidisha to Bhilsa:
- the old capital was still a royal seat in 608;
- it was deserted at some point between the seventh and tenth centuries, and Bhilsa rose on the east bank, its walls said to be built from the old city's stone;
- Bhilsa was named for a Sun image, Bhāillasvāmin, whose name is recorded from 878;
- in the twelfth century the province around Udaypur carried that name.

**Purpose:** The town that replaced the old centre, and the origin of the name Udaypur's province would bear.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The last of the old city.**
   - **Point:**
     - The Kalachuri king Buddharāja issued his grant of 608 from Vaidiśa: the old city was still a seat of royal business.
     - It was then deserted, and Bhilsa rose across the river. The dating runs from after the seventh century (with a deluge or a political catastrophe proposed) through the eighth or ninth, to "by the tenth". One account has Bhilsa as a branch-town from the ninth.
     - Bhilsa's outer walls are said to have been built from the old city's square stones: reported, not established.

     Give the change as a range around the event. The readings go in one note.
   - E: C03-GAZ-053, C03-MADHYABHARAT-017, C03-KB-GAZ-001. C: C03-GAZ-024, C03-MADHYABHARAT-014. Note: C03-GAZ-019, C03-KB-GAZ-001, C03-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-002, C03-MADHYABHARAT-017, C03-RAJABHOJ-EN-003.
2. **P2 — A town named for the Sun.**
   - **Point:**
     - Bhilsa took its name from Bhāillasvāmin, an image of the Sun worshipped in a temple there. In the CII editor's reading, the name means "the store-house of lustre".
     - The name is first recorded in 878.
     - The Sun temple was standing in the tenth century: an inscription of the reign of a king Krishna, probably the Rāṣṭrakūṭa Krishna III (939–68), refers to it. The identification is the Gazetteer's "probably"; keep it.
     - About 1030 Al-Bīrūnī noted that the town bore the name of its idol. Use him here only for the name; Bhilsa's political place is Chapter 2's and Chapter 1's.
     - The 1172 record places Udayapur in the province called the Bhāillasvāmi-mahādvādaśaka. Recall it in one clause, without explaining the province again.

     For Udaypur this matters: the name of its later province was a Sun temple's at Bhilsa.
   - E: C03-CII-004, C03-KB-CII-001, C03-CII-001, C03-CII-008, C01-GANGULY-010. C: C03-GAZ-054, C03-KB-CII-002. X: C03-KB-GAZ-015, C03-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-001, C03-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-003, C03-RAJABHOJ-EN-007.

**Transition:** By then the powers that mattered on Udaypur's range were nearer than Vidisha had ever been.

## 3.5 A frontier on Udaypur's range *(Eighth to tenth centuries · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader sees Udaypur's own range as a frontier crowded with temples in the two centuries before the Paramāra foundation:
- Malwa passed from the Pratihāras to a Rāṣṭrakūṭa vassal in 808–812, in Ganguly's reading;
- a Rāṣṭrakūṭa minister set up a Garuḍa pillar at Pathari in 861;
- Gyaraspur, in the same foothills as Udaypur and on the old road to Sagar, had temples of the eighth to tenth centuries;
- Badoh–Pathari was a temple town round its tanks, its rulers unknown;
- on Udaypur's hill, a two-line inscription is assigned to the eighth century and tentatively read;
- the Chandellas claimed Bhilsa by 954, and three dynasties competed for the region.

Who held the hill before the Paramāras is not known.

**Purpose:** Udaypur's immediate neighbourhood just before the Paramāras: the rivals, the temple towns and the hill's own record.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Pratihāras and Rāṣṭrakūṭas.**
   - **Point:**
     - Between 808 and 812, Malwa passed from the Gurjara-Pratihāras to a vassal of the Rāṣṭrakūṭas. This is Ganguly's inference, attributed in the note.
     - The Rāṣṭrakūṭas shared out their conquests north of the Narmada among members of their family, and the plateau near Bhilsa was for a time under their rule.
     - In 861 the chief minister of a Rāṣṭrakūṭa king, Parabala, set up a Garuḍa-dhvaja at Pathari, on the far slope of Udaypur's range.
     - How far the Pratihāras held this district is not known.
   - E: C03-KB-GANGULY-002, C03-MADHYABHARAT-024, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-013, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-014. C: C03-KB-GANGULY-001, C03-KB-GAZ-016, C02-KB-GAZ-009. Note: C03-KB-GANGULY-002.
2. **P2 — Temple towns on the range.**
   - **Point:**
     - **Gyaraspur:** in the same western foothills as Udaypur, on the old high road to Sagar, it has monuments of the eighth to tenth centuries. Its Maladevi, partly rock-cut, is a mature temple in the Pratihāra style.
     - **Badoh:** now a petty village, it was a prosperous medieval town of temples round a large tank, its ruins shared with Pathari. Its Gadarmal keeps a ninth-century basement and porch; a shepherd legend explains the name, as legend. Its Daśāvatāra group runs from the eighth to the tenth century.
     - **Pathari:** its Varāha and Śiva temple run from the ninth to the eleventh.
     - Little is known of who ruled Badoh or who built its monuments.

     Never merge Gyaraspur, Badoh, Pathari and Udaypur into one place, or their dates into one. Not a temple-by-temple inventory: the point is a range crowded with temple towns whose rulers are unknown. (Plan note, run 2.)
   - E: C03-KB-TEMPLES-IND-001, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-012, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-003, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-016. C: C03-KB-GAZ-017, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-004, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-005, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-006, C03-TEMPLES-IND-006, C03-TEMPLES-IND-007, C03-TEMPLES-IND-005, C03-KB-MADHYABHARAT-015.
3. **P3 — A record on Udaypur's hill.**
   - **Point:** The survey assigns a two-line inscription on Udaypur's hill to the eighth century. It opens with the *siddham*, and in the survey's tentative reading it names a *sūtradhāra*. This is a proposal, stated as one; Chapter 1 weighs what it can bear, so do not re-argue it here. It is a different inscription from the cave's undeciphered lines in 3.3: never connect the two. A short paragraph.
   - E: C03-KB-GEOHER-002.
4. **P4 — Three powers.**
   - **Point:**
     - The Khajuraho inscription of 954 claims that the Chandella kingdom reached Bhilsa, under Dhaṅga (954–1002): a royal claim.
     - As Pratihāra power faded, the Chandellas, the Kalachuris of Dāhala and the Paramāras of Malwa administered parts of this region at the same time, competing in war and in building temples. Central India was "thickly studded" with temples, of which a small fraction survives.

     End on the contested frontier around Udaypur's hill by the late tenth century. Hand on by content. Do not re-introduce the Paramāras as a polity, Dhārā, or Bhilsa as the kingdom's eastern limit.
   - E: C03-GANGULY-001, C03-CII-005, C03-TEMPLES-IND-008. C: C03-GAZ-055, C03-JAGTA-HI-010. X: C03-JAGTA-HI-009.

**Transition:** Chapter 4 turns to the Paramāras' arrival in this frontier and the foundation of Udaypur.
