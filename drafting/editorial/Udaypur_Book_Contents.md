# Udaypur: History, Monuments and the Life of a Historic Town

*Contents, Chapter 1 to the Afterword: each chapter's units, what each covers and why it matters, and the books the chapter draws on, most-cited first.*

---

## Chapter 1. Udaypur: Historical Memory and the Making of a Place

1. **The Village Today.** Udaypur as it is now: a large village panchayat below the Shiv pindi hill, reached from Ganj Basoda and Bareth, with 6,383 people in 2011. This is the present-day ground the book works back from.
2. **The Living Temple.** The Udayeśvara temple as a place of daily worship and an annual fair, revered by Hindus and Muslims alike in the 1870s. It shows that the monument has never been only a ruin.
3. **The Walled Town and Its Memories.** The time-keepers' house, the remembered tally of stepwells, wells and lakes, the old tank's bed now under fields, and the legend of Berni Mata. This is what the village remembers, kept apart from what the records show.
4. **The Names of the Place.** Udayapura, Udaypur and the confusion with Udaipur in Rajasthan; the temple's names, Udayeśvara in the inscriptions and Nīlakaṇṭheśvara later; and the other names the records give the town. The book's subject depends on getting these right.
5. **Udayāditya and the Paramāra Kingdom.** The founder as Bhoja's brother, the dispute over that kinship, and the kingdom he recovered after Bhoja's death. It places the founding inside Paramāra kingship before Chapter 4 tells the story in full.
6. **The Foundation of Town, Temple and Tank.** The inscription crediting Udayāditya with founding Udayapura, building Udayeśvara and digging Udayasamudra, all named after himself, beside the legend of the snake in the fire. This is the founding act the whole book turns on.
7. **The Date of the Foundation.** Why 1080 is the earliest firm date and 1059 only a reading, and the three ways the 1080 record has been read. It settles what the dates can and cannot bear.
8. **Udaypur in the Eastern Province.** The town as a seat of government: the Bhilsa province and its sixty-four villages, a subordinate Nēmaka house, the governors who ruled from it for the Caulukya kings of Gujarat, and Devapāla's residence there. It shows Udaypur as more than a temple town.
9. **Later Rulers and Patrons.** The town's later masters in outline, from the mosque of 1336–38 in the temple court to Mughal building and the Scindia general's brass cover for the *liṅga*. This chronology is developed in Chapter 10.
10. **The Udaypur Praśasti.** The two broken slabs of the dynasty's poem of praise, its genealogy from the first king to Udayāditya, and the finding of its second half in 1925–26. It introduces the dynasty's own account, read throughout as a claim.
11. **The Inscriptions and Their First Readers.** The temple as an archive of eight centuries of writing in Sanskrit, Persian and Hindi, and the scholars from Beglar and Cunningham to Bühler who first read it. It shows how Udaypur came to be known.
12. **Stories of the Temple's Survival.** The legends of Tughluq's gunpowder, Aurangzeb's order, a temple raised in a night, buried treasure and the flour-grinder woman. They are memory of survival, tested later against the record.
13. **Udaypur among Paramāra Monuments.** How scholars have placed the temple and town among the dynasty's works, from the model of the Bhūmija style to one of the towns that grew under Paramāra rule. These assessments explain why the place has drawn attention, and each is attributed to its author.
14. **An Earlier Settlement?** Survey proposals of an older place on the site set against the argument that the town began with Udayāditya, and the traditions of a conquered chief. This is the open question that runs through the book.

## Chapter 2. The Betwa Valley: Geography, Geology and Natural Environment

1. **The Betwa and Its Streams.** The Betwa (the Vētravatī of the inscriptions), its course and tributaries, and the stream that passes near the village. It shows that Udaypur belongs to the river's country but not to its banks.
2. **Plateau, Ranges and Rocks.** The Vindhyan plateau, the Garhi–Teonda range in whose foothills Udaypur lies, and the trap and sandstone beneath. This is the regional frame for the hill and its stone.
3. **The Udaypur Hill: Mesa, Butte and Caves.** The survey's account of the local landform, including Shiva Pindi and its natural and cut caves. It explains the hill that gave the town stone and shelter.
4. **The Monsoon and Its Failures.** The single wet season, the runs of dry years, famine and sudden deluge, with the modern record of rain and river flow as today's regime. Every arrangement for water and farming had to meet this climate.
5. **Black Soil and the Two Harvests.** The black cotton soil, the autumn and spring crops, and why the winter crop depends on water kept from the rains. It shows what the land could feed.
6. **Forest, Grass and Grazing.** The forest on the hill ranges, the reserved forest above Udaypur, and the grazing and felling that have thinned it. Plant life appears here as land use.
7. **Water and the Siting of Settlement.** How settlement followed water in a country with no river at the door, from ponds in natural hollows to the treatise's rules for a town's water. Udaypur's own tanks are seen here at landscape level.
8. **Bhoja's Lake at Bhojpur.** The great dams and lake on the Betwa built under Bhoja, and their later breach. It is a comparison for Paramāra water engineering, not evidence for Udaypur itself.
9. **Sandstone for Building.** The district's building sandstone and the temple's red stone "quarried in the hill close by". It explains where the town's material came from.
10. **A Town of Its Own Stone.** Stone houses and shops built with almost no brick or cement, and the quarries working today. It closes the chapter on the land as setting rather than cause.

## Chapter 3. Before Udaypur: Prehistory, Early Vidisha and the Historical Landscape of Central India

1. **Prehistory and the Country's Ancient Names.** The earliest stone tools from the river valleys, none from the hill itself, and the country's old names, Daśārṇa and Ākara. It names the land Udaypur later stood in.
2. **Vidisha in Literature and Tradition.** The epics, Purāṇas and Kālidāsa's works that remember Vidisha, none of which mention Udaypur. It shows the region's long literary standing.
3. **Besnagar: The Excavated City.** The ancient city in the fork of the Betwa and Bes, its habitation periods, palace wall and early coins. This is the material record of the region's old centre.
4. **Vidisha under the Mauryas and Śuṅgas.** Aśoka's connection with the city, the Śuṅga princes who ruled from it, and its standing as the capital of east Malwa. It gives the early political history of the regional capital.
5. **Trade Routes, Merchants and Sacred Centres.** Vidisha at the crossing of the great routes, its merchant donors to Sanchi, and its standing as a place of pilgrimage. It describes the network the region belonged to.
6. **Heliodoros's Pillar and the Early Vaiṣṇava Temples.** The early temples of Viṣṇu at Besnagar and the Garuḍa pillar set up by a Greek envoy. These are the region's earliest built shrines.
7. **Images and Rites at Besnagar.** The colossal statues, early Śaiva images of the Mothers and the *liṅga*, and sacrificial pits. They show worship in stone of Śiva centuries before the Paramāras.
8. **From the Sātavāhanas to the Guptas.** Vidisha's changing masters after the Śuṅgas, from Sātavāhana and Nāga rulers to the Gupta conquest. It connects the early capital to the Gupta age.
9. **Udayagiri and the Gupta Shrines.** The Gupta cave temples of Udayagiri and the small flat-roofed shrines in the countryside near Udaypur. They carry the tradition of worship in stone close to the town.
10. **From Vidisha to Bhilsa.** The desertion of old Vidisha, the rise of Bhilsa, and the Sun image whose name passed to the town and later to Udaypur's province.
11. **A Contested Frontier, Eighth to Tenth Centuries.** The Rāṣṭrakūṭas, Pratihāras and Candellas around Gyaraspur, Badoh and Pathari before the Paramāras. It ends on the open question of who held Udaypur's hill.

## Chapter 4. The Paramāras and the Foundation of Udaypur: Kingship, Patronage and Urban Formation

1. **The Origin of the Paramāras.** The legend of the hero born from a sacrificial fire, which Udaypur's praśasti is the first record to tell, and the modern debate over where the family came from. It opens the dynasty's history with its own claim about itself.
2. **From Feudatories to Kings of Malwa.** The early line from the first king to Sīyaka II, its grants of 949, the war that brought independence, and a kingdom bounded on the east by Vidisha. It brings the dynasty to Udaypur's country.
3. **Muñja and Sindhurāja.** Vākpati-Muñja's wars and death as a prisoner of the Cālukya king Tailapa II, his fame as a poet, and Sindhurāja's victories. These are weighed against what the praśasti claims for them.
4. **The Branch Lines of the Dynasty.** The collateral Paramāra houses of Vāgaḍa, Ābū, Jālor and Bhinmal. They show how the dynasty governed outlying provinces through its own kin.
5. **Bhoja's Reign and Kingdom.** Bhoja's accession, his dated reign from 1011 to 1047, and the eastern frontier at Bhilsa that placed Udaypur's country inside his kingdom.
6. **Bhoja's Wars.** Bhoja's campaigns against the Caulukyas, Candellas, Kalacuris and Cālukyas. These are the hostilities his successors inherited.
7. **Bhoja as Builder.** The rebuilding of Dhārā, the temple and dam at Bhojpur, and the Vāgdevī image. Together they make the model of royal building the region inherited.
8. **Bhoja's Learning, Faith and Court.** The works attributed to Bhoja, the doubts about his authorship, and the scholars of his court. They form the ideal of the learned king against which his successors were measured.
9. **Bhoja in Udaypur's Praśasti and in Memory.** What Udaypur's stone says of Bhoja, cut after his death, and how later tradition and modern writing draw him close to a town where no act of his is recorded.
10. **Bhoja's Last Years and the Reign of Jayasiṃha.** The attacks at the end of Bhoja's reign, his death before 1055, and the reign of Jayasiṃha, whom the dynasty's own lists leave out. This is the gap just before the king whose name Udaypur bears.
11. **The Three-Sided War and the Recovery of Malwa.** The coalition that overwhelmed Jayasiṃha and Udayāditya's recovery of the kingdom, as the inscriptions and historians tell it. It is how the founder came to the throne.
12. **Udayāditya's Descent.** The inscriptions of his son's time that make Udayāditya Bhoja's brother, and the rival pedigree from Udaypur that its editor rejects. It explains why the kinship matters for the town.
13. **Dating the Beginning: 1059 and 1080.** The familiar 1059 date traced back to late or faulty records and set against the firm record of 1080. It decides whether Udaypur was begun before Udayāditya's reign or within it.
14. **Udayāditya's Reign and Realm.** The dated records of the reign from 1080 to 1093 and the realm they show, with his accession c. 1070 as the working position.
15. **The Royal Family and the Succession.** Udayāditya's sons, marriages and ministers, his death in 1093, and the succession of Naravarman. It closes the founder's life.
16. **Royal Temple Building under the Paramāras.** The royal temple as a gift that earned merit, and the wave of temple building across the region. This is the practice within which Udayāditya's temple was given.
17. **Town Planning in Bhoja's Treatises.** What the Paramāra-era treatises prescribe for a town, and Dhārā as the model city. It frames the question of whether Udaypur was planned, without claiming that it was.
18. **The Name Udayapura and the Founding Claims.** The records that give the town, temple and tank the king's name, the later statements that he founded the town, and the village's memory of a conquered place. Whether the town was founded or renamed stays open.
19. **Udayapura as a Seat of Government.** The damaged slab naming a subordinate Nēmaka house at Udayapura, and the later province of the 1170s, kept apart from the founder's reign.
20. **The Frontier Town in Local Testimony.** The modern local picture of Udaypur as a frontier "mini-capital" with a palace and planned streets. It is testimony, set beside the record.

## Chapter 5. The Udayeśvara Temple: Architecture, Sculpture and Religious Iconography

1. **The Parts of a Northern Temple.** The standard elements of a north Indian temple: sanctum, vestibule, hall, porches and platform. These are the terms needed to read Udayeśvara's plan.
2. **Sanctum, Vestibule, Hall and Porches.** The temple's main body on its east–west axis, built of red sandstone, with its three balconied porches. It describes the building as it stands.
3. **The Vedī.** The separate low building on the temple's axis and the disputed accounts of its purpose. It is one of the temple's distinctive features.
4. **The Court and Its Attendant Shrines.** The paved court, its enclosure and the ring of attendant shrines, whose number differs by source, and what has been lost.
5. **The Northern Spire and Its Parts.** The curved north Indian spire, its spines, antefix and crowning stones. This is the vocabulary needed to describe the tower.
6. **The Star Plan and the Seven-Tiered Tower.** How a rotated square gives the star-shaped plan, and how the wall rises into seven tiers of miniature spires. It is the design that defines the temple.
7. **The Bhūmija Style and Its Origins.** The style of chained miniature spires, its regions, and the competing views of where it began.
8. **Udayeśvara among the Paramāra Temples.** The temple compared with Un, Nemawar, Onkar Mandhata and other Bhūmija temples, and the claim that it served as their model.
9. **Khajuraho or the Deccan.** The long debate over whether the temple's closest relations lie with Khajuraho or with the temples of the Deccan.
10. **Base Mouldings and Wall.** The sequence of mouldings at the base and the wall of pillar-like buttresses, niches and carved bands. They show the quality of the masons' work.
11. **Doorways, Pillars and Ceilings.** The sanctum and porch doorways, the hall's pillars and the carved ceiling. They are the building's most closely worked surfaces.
12. **Ornament and Attendant Figures.** The standard repertoire of temple ornament, from lattice windows to celestial figures. It is the visual vocabulary of the temple's walls.
13. **Stone, Masons and Workshops.** How such temples were built and who built them, with local memory of the work reported as memory only.
14. **The Dancing Śiva of the Antefixes.** The great eastern antefix with its dancing Śiva, and the same image repeated on the other faces. These are the temple's dominant images.
15. **Guardians of the Directions.** The eight guardians of the quarters, each with his mount, placed by rule around the walls.
16. **The Mother Goddesses.** The eight Mothers gathered on the sanctum's spines with Vīrabhadra and Cāmuṇḍā.
17. **Gods and Goddesses of the Hall Walls.** The main images of the hall's niches and upper tiers, from Bhairava and Sūrya to Brahmā and Viṣṇu.
18. **Composite Images.** The images that combine several gods in one figure, paired across the hall.
19. **Divine Couples of the Lower Niches.** The small base niches, most of them holding seated Śiva and Pārvatī.
20. **Porch Balustrades and the Hall Interior.** The figures of the porch balustrades and lintels, and the images and *liṅga* inside the hall.
21. **The Damaged Images.** The mutilation recorded across the temple's sculpture, as part of its present condition. Who damaged them is left to Chapter 10.
22. **The Sculptural Style of Paramāra Malwa.** The temple's sculpture placed within the art of the region, and the scholars' differing judgements of it.
23. **Images and the Iconographic Canons.** The textual rules for making images, and where the temple's carvings follow them.
24. **Building Norms in the Texts.** The ideal grid, proportions and building rites set out in the architectural texts. They are norms, not a record of how Udaypur was built.
25. **The Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra and Its Temple Rules.** The treatise attributed to Bhoja and its rules for temples, including the Bhūmija class. It is read as a statement of norms.
26. **Śaiva Siddhānta Doctrine.** The doctrine of the Śaiva school dominant in the region, on which the main reading of the temple depends.
27. **Readings of the Sculptural Programme.** Pande's reading of the temple as a path to Śiva, and the interpretations of single images, including the figure near the summit, each attributed to its author.
28. **The Temple as Cosmos.** The general symbolic reading of the Hindu temple and the scholars who doubt it. It closes the chapter on how far meaning can be read into the building.

## Chapter 6. Inscriptions and Intellectual Life: Epigraphy, Languages and Literary Culture

1. **The Temple Inscription of 1080.** The six-line record at the east door of the flag-hoisting in V.S. 1137, signed by its poet. It gives the temple's first firm date and its contemporary name.
2. **The Long Inscription of V.S. 1116.** The twenty-two-line inscription full of errors that is the source of the 1059 reading. Its poor quality is why that date stays tentative.
3. **The Amērā Tank Inscription.** The verse record of a Brāhmaṇa's tank about 3 km south of Udaypur, dated V.S. 1151. It is the nearest dated Sanskrit composition after the temple's own records.
4. **The Udaypur Praśasti: The Stones and the Text.** The two slabs of the dynasty's poem, now in Gwalior, and their editions. It sets out what the object is before it is read as history.
5. **Royal Virtue in the Praśasti.** The poem's picture of its kings as eloquent, learned, pious builders of temples, read as the dynasty's account of itself.
6. **The Genealogy and Its Critics.** The praśasti's unbroken line of kings and fire-born origin, and the historians who test it. This is the central measure of how far the poem can be trusted.
7. **The Conquests Claimed.** The victories the praśasti claims for its kings, checked against other records. It shows which claims hold and which rest on the poem alone.
8. **The Makers of Paramāra Praśastis.** The lost name of the Udaypur poem's author, compared with other praśastis that name their composers and engravers.
9. **The Serpent-Sword Inscription at Udaypur.** The slab that sets the whole Sanskrit alphabet and its grammatical endings in the body of a serpent. It is the town's one inscription that is itself a grammar.
10. **Serpent Charts across the Kingdom.** The matching charts at Ujjain, Dhār and Ūn, and the debate over whether they were teaching aids or royal emblems.
11. **The Halls of Learning at Dhārā.** Bhoja's halls of Sarasvatī at Dhārā, with their inscribed poems and grammar charts. They are the background to the learning Udaypur's serpent displays.
12. **Bhoja as Author.** The many works attributed to Bhoja and the doubts about his authorship. It is context for the praśasti's praise of him as a poet-king.
13. **Poets and Scholars of the Paramāra Courts.** The poets and scholars of Muñja's and Bhoja's courts, among them the author of a sun-hymn at Vidisha. This is the literary world the dynasty's inscriptions drew on.
14. **Learning in the Later Kingdom.** The poets, plays and teachers of the later Paramāra kingdom, in the years when Devapāla resided at Udayapura.
15. **The Nāgarī Script and Lettering.** How the shapes of letters are used to date Paramāra inscriptions.
16. **The Inscriptions of the Temple Porches.** The records and pilgrims' notes on the porch pillars and seats, introduced as one body.
17. **Reading a Paramāra Grant.** The standard opening verses, titles and officials of Paramāra grants. They are needed to read the porch records correctly.
18. **The Porch Records under Caulukya Rule and under Devapāla, 1163–1232.** The records made while governors ruled for the Caulukya kings of Gujarat, then the two records of 1229 and 1232 that show the Paramāra king Devapāla back as master and residing at Udayapura.
19. **The Last Paramāra Records.** Two later records, of V.S. 1311 and V.S. 1366, known only at second hand. They are the last sign of Paramāra rule in the porch.
20. **Persian on the Temple's Stones.** The Persian inscriptions of 1336–38 naming Muhammad bin Tughluq, cut on doorways built from the temple's own stones. They are the town's second language of record.
21. **Inscriptions of the Hill.** The pilgrims' records, the Jain cave inscription and the undeciphered lines on the hill. They show writing outside the temple and in local speech.
22. **The Limits of the Record.** How the inscriptions are known, mostly from copies and selections, and what remains unread. It closes the chapter on what the stones cannot say.

## Chapter 7. The Wider Built Heritage of Udaypur: Fortifications, Shrines, Mosques and Water Structures

1. **The Monuments Counted.** The surveys and inventories that list Udaypur's buildings, about fifty in the latest count. Their blanket dates are proposals, and every later unit has to read them that way.
2. **The Hill Wall.** The dry-laid sandstone wall of about 1,400 m on the southern hill, with its bastions, gates and present condition. It is the largest structure outside the temple.
3. **Inside the Wall.** The cells around a reservoir, the paved approach and the summit shrine enclosed by the wall. They show the hill was lived on and visited, not only defended.
4. **Dating the Hill Wall.** The competing proposals that the fort is older than Udayāditya or his own work, and the suggestion that lichens on its stones could date it. No source yet settles the question.
5. **Forts and Towns in Bhoja's Treatise.** The treatise's rules for ramparts, gates and the ideal town of roads, palaces and quarters. They are norms against which the surviving wall and lanes can be compared.
6. **The Jain Cave and the Rock Panels.** The panels of the Mothers and the nine planets on the hill, the Jina images, and the cave with its seated Tīrthaṅkara. They show that worship on the hill was not Śaiva only.
7. **The Unfinished Colossus and the Cut Hill.** The huge unfinished Śiva carved in a boulder, and the cut hill read as an abandoned rock temple. The reasons given for their abandonment are proposals.
8. **The Walled Town and Its Gates.** The town wall and its gates, named for the roads and towns they faced. It is the frame for the palace, houses and mosques.
9. **The Palace and the Governor's Mansion.** The ruined palace enclosure, built in several periods, and the early Mughal mansion beside the Shahi Masjid, kept apart as two buildings.
10. **Lanes, Gateways and the Old Bazaar.** The lane of stone shops leading to the temple, the old gateways across streets, and the rows of the old bazaar. They are what survives of the town's plan.
11. **Houses Built of Older Buildings.** The courtyard houses and mansions of the old town, many built from the stone of earlier buildings. They show a town that reused itself.
12. **The Bijamandal.** The two-storeyed building beside the temple with its unfinished inscription to the Sun, distinct from the mosque of the same name at Vidisha.
13. **The Barakhambi and the Pisanhari Temple.** A twelve-pillared hall of a lost temple and a plain later shrine tied by tradition to a woman who ground flour.
14. **Later Hindu Shrines.** The town's lesser temples, some centuries later than the survey's listed dates. They show that temple building continued.
15. **Jain Images, Chhatris and Memorial Stones.** The Jain temple precinct, the memorial pavilions, and the memorial stones near the Chanderi gate. They record worship and remembrance beyond Śaiva practice.
16. **The Mosque in the Temple Court, 1336–38.** The mosque built on the western side of the temple court from reused pillars. It is the earliest dated building in the town after the temple.
17. **The Bilingual Mosque of 1488.** The small mosque outside the eastern gate whose Persian and Sanskrit inscriptions record its building under the sultan of Mandu.
18. **The Shahi Masjid, 1632.** The large mosque east of the temple, begun under Jahāngīr and completed under Shāh Jahān.
19. **The Undated Muslim Monuments.** The small mosques, dargahs and graves of the town and hill, none of them dated.
20. **Water on the Hill.** The stepwell cut against the hill and the plastered reservoir on its summit. They show how the hill was supplied with water.
21. **Stepwells and Wells in the Town.** The town's stepwells, among them one with steps shallow enough for horses. Their dates are proposals, and their inscriptions are unread.
22. **The Udaysagar and the Udayasamudra.** The great tank north of the village, now under wheat, set against the tank the inscriptions name. The two are not shown to be the same.
23. **The Bhujariya Talab and Other Tanks.** The plainer tank south-east of the village, proposed as older than Udayāditya's time, and the other tanks of the area.
24. **Vikrama's Tank at Amērā.** The one Paramāra-period tank with a contemporary record, built in 1094 for bathing, worship and offerings to ancestors, and the regional records of water given for merit. It leads into the gifts of Chapter 8.
25. **Water Structures in the Treatise and the Region.** Bhoja's types of tank, stepwell and *kuṇḍa*, and the region's other old tanks. These are the terms in which Udaypur's water works can be classed.

## Chapter 8. Religion and the Material Life of Udaypur: Ritual, Agriculture, Trade and Craft

1. **The Udaypur Endowments, 1163–1229.** The three dated gifts to the temple, made under Caulukya governors and under Devapāla, and the absence of any founding endowment. They are the only direct evidence of how the temple was maintained.
2. **How Paramāra Temples Were Endowed.** Gifts of villages, land, dues, shops and tolls to temples across the kingdom, and who gave them. This is the regional pattern, not shown for Udaypur itself.
3. **The Temple as an Institution.** Why temples were endowed at all: the merit of building and giving, and temples as landowners and employers. It frames the small Udaypur gifts.
4. **Land, Measure and Water in the Grants.** How the grants measured land, the crops it grew, and water-wheels and wells given as gifts. It is the only medieval evidence for how land and water were counted in the region.
5. **Stone, Cloth and Craft.** Udaypur's red sandstone vessels, the builders' roles in the treatises, and Malwa's cloth trade.
6. **Markets, Goods and Money.** What Malwa's markets traded, how traders were taxed, and the coin in use. It describes the commercial world of the Paramāra centuries.
7. **Farming and Irrigation in the Modern District.** The farming economy of the twentieth-century district and its slight irrigation. It is the documented agricultural base nearest to Udaypur.
8. **Later Trades and Crafts.** The district's tobacco, floor-cloths and small industries, and Udaypur's stone quarries.
9. **What Worship Required.** The daily services, offerings and festivals of a Paramāra-period temple, as regional records describe them.
10. **Śaiva Schools in Malwa.** The Śaiva schools active in the region, with the Śaiva Siddhānta dominant, and Bhoja's treatise on it.
11. **Śaiva Monasteries and Ascetic Lineages.** The monasteries and teaching lineages of Central India, the nearest evidence for the religious setting of a royal Śiva temple.
12. **Gods beside Śiva.** Royal tolerance of other faiths, the goddesses favoured by later kings, and the Jain presence. It places Udayeśvara among the region's other cults.
13. **Temple Service at Udaypur.** The stone that may have held the temple drum, the time-keepers' family and its claim to hereditary service. This is the only direct evidence of the temple's servants.
14. **The Living Temple.** Daily worship, special rites and the Śivarātri fair today. It shows a temple in continuous use and leads into Chapter 9.

## Chapter 9. Society and Cultural Life: Communities, Languages, Festivals and Oral Traditions

1. **Communities Today.** The Hindu, Muslim and Jain households of Udaypur as one writer describes them, set against the district's census figures. It shows what can be said about who lives in the town, and on what evidence.
2. **Identities Remade.** A family's claim of Paramāra descent and the Bagdi tradition, reported as family and community traditions.
3. **Society in Paramāra Malwa.** The ranked society and migrant Brāhmaṇa families recorded in the Paramāra grants, as the regional background to the town's founding.
4. **Languages of the District.** The census record of Hindi, Urdu and the regional dialects. The village's own speech is not documented.
5. **Festivals, Fairs and Everyday Life.** Śivarātri and its fair, the Bhujariya rites, court festivals known from texts, and the dress, food and dance of the region.
6. **The Founder and the Temple in Local Stories.** The stories that give the place to Udayāditya by treasure, conquest or wonder, and the similar tales told elsewhere.
7. **The Legend of Berni Mata.** The versions of the tale of the dancer on the thread, set side by side to show how one story grows into several.
8. **Bhoja in Proverb and Song.** Bhoja as the Paramāra king of the region's folklore, against Udaypur's own tales, which name only Udayāditya.
9. **The Memory of Decline.** Local stories of dacoits, treasure-seekers and a deserted market. They are the town's memory of its later centuries, tested in Chapter 10.

## Chapter 10. Political Change and Economic Decline: Udaypur under Successive Regimes

1. **A Caulukya Seat on the Eastern Frontier, c. 1093–1190.** The last years of Paramāra control of the east and Udaypur's period as a headquarters of governors for the Caulukya kings of Gujarat. The first stone at Udaypur to name a king of another house dates from this period.
2. **Devapāla's Line and the Delhi Raids, c. 1190–1305.** The recovery of Bhilsa from Delhi, the pressure from three sides, and the fall of Malwa in 1305. This is the frame in which Udaypur lost its royal standing.
3. **Udaypur after 1305.** The mosque of 1336–38 in the temple court, with the temple left in worship. Here the survival legends of Chapter 1 are tested against the dated record.
4. **Temples Turned to Mosques across Malwa.** The fate of the great temples at Vidisha, Dhār and Ujjain. It shows why the survival of Udayeśvara is the exception the chapter must explain.
5. **Bhilsa under the Malwa Sultans, 1401–1562.** The regional sequence of the Malwa sultanate and the Afghan wars, during which the documents stop naming Udaypur.
6. **A Qasba of Sarkar Chanderi.** Udaypur's place in the Mughal order, the reported farman, the Shahi Masjid of 1632, and the qazi's office.
7. **Aurangzeb at Bhilsa, and at Udaypur in Legend.** Aurangzeb's recorded acts at Bhilsa set against the legend that places him at Udaypur.
8. **Afghans, Nawabs and Marathas, 1708–1775.** The turnover of eighteenth-century rulers through which the district passed to the Scindias.
9. **The Brass Cover of the Liṅga.** The brass sheath given by a Scindia general and dated V.S. 1841, the one dated act of patronage at the temple in the Maratha period.
10. **Bhilsa District in Gwalior State and After, 1827–1956.** Revenue administration under Gwalior, the district's formation, and its passage into Madhya Pradesh.
11. **Famine, the Railway and a Feeder Road.** The famines of 1899–1900 and the railway that drew trade away from Udaypur.
12. **What Decline Means.** The chapter's conclusion: Udaypur lost rank in steps, while its economic decline cannot yet be dated.

## Chapter 11. Udaypur in the Modern Period: Archaeology, Heritage Administration and Conservation

1. **The First Archaeological Reports, 1871–1914.** The tours of Beglar and Cunningham and the first editions of the inscriptions, which fixed how the temple has been understood.
2. **Gwalior State Archaeology.** The princely department's finds, removals to the Gwalior museum and restorations, including the second slab of the praśasti.
3. **Protection after Independence.** The division of the monuments between the national and state authorities, the money spent, and the gaps in the record.
4. **The Temple's Clearance under the ASI.** The one remembered intervention at the temple after independence, known only from an officer's recollection.
5. **The INTACH Documentation, 2020–21.** The first survey of the whole town, recording about fifty sites. Its dates are proposals, and listing a site does not give it legal protection.
6. **The Palace and the 2021 Revenue Order.** A dispute over the old palace and the eviction order that followed, told as an administrative matter.
7. **Stolen and Defaced Sculpture.** Thefts, graffiti and damage to the town's carved heritage up to 2021.
8. **Quarrying, Blasting and Grazing on the Hill.** The threats to the hill and its fortification wall, including mining near the wall and a recent repair already cracking.

## Chapter 12. Udaypur Today: A Living Community and Its Historic Environment

1. **Population and the Census Record.** The census counts from 1901 to 2011, the only reliable figures for the town's growth.
2. **Roads, Stations, Schools and the Panchayat.** The village as the state records it: its panchayat, roads, nearest railway station and schools.
3. **Living Inside the Old Town.** Households living in old carved-stone dwellings and new houses built on ruins. They show that the old town is inhabited, not abandoned.
4. **Fields, the Market and the Quarries.** Farming for the Ganj Basoda market, the pressure of fields on the forest, and quarry work. They are the livelihoods of the present village.
5. **The Living Hill.** The bats in the caves, the birds recorded on the hill, grazing, and the villagers who climb to its shrines.
6. **The Temple's Visitors.** Who comes to the temple, what they do there, and how few are shown the rest of the town.
7. **The Town Becoming Visible, 2020–21.** Heritage walks from Ganj Basoda, a widely watched video, and a presentation in Bhopal that brought the town new attention.
8. **The Lane to the Temple, 2021.** The widening of the lane by residents who set back their own shopfronts. It is the one dated episode in which residents' own actions are recorded.
9. **Outsiders' Views of Residents and Officials.** The judgements visiting writers made of the townspeople and local officials, each attributed. They show that the record speaks about residents more than for them.
10. **Expectations of a Tourist Town.** The predictions that Udaypur will become a destination, recorded as attitudes rather than plans. They lead into the Afterword.

## Afterword. Conserving Udaypur: Community, Heritage and the Future

1. **Where the Record Stops.** The latest dated evidence, from a 2019 letter to the district Collector to the events of 2021, which marks the limit of the book's history.
2. **Agencies, Owners and Coordination.** The authorities responsible for different parts of the site, and the proposals for bringing them together.
3. **Translating the Inscriptions on Site.** The proposal to display Hindi and English translations of the inscriptions at the temple, so that residents and visitors can read the town's history.
4. **Guides, Signs and Heritage Walks.** Proposals for trained village guides, signboards, regular heritage walks and skilled staff at the site.
5. **Clearing, Excavating and Recording.** Proposals to clear and excavate the ruins, document the heritage of every village, and monitor the fort.
6. **Schools and the Next Generation.** Proposals for school heritage walks, publications and films on local history.
7. **Residents and Citizens as Keepers.** The calls on residents, merchants and regional organisations to share in looking after the town, set against what local people have already done.
8. **Professionals and Researchers.** The shortage of scholars able to read new inscriptions, and the book's open questions as an agenda for research.
9. **Tourism Circuits and Partnerships.** The larger proposals for a Paramāra temple circuit and public–private partnerships, each attributed to its author.
10. **A Heritage Town.** A historian's proposal that the whole town be conserved as a heritage town, and the question of who would bear the cost.
11. **Testing the Proposals.** The proposals measured against the book's evidence, above all on who would bear the cost of any clearance.
12. **Conservation by Use.** The book's single stated argument: a town that survived because it was lived in is best conserved as a lived-in town and hill.
