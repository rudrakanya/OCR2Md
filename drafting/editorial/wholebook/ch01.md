# Chapter 1. Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

**Target:** 4,000 words (as written: 3,301 in 5 sections) · **Evidence:** `evidence_index/ch01.jsonl`, with KB items (C01-KB-…) and borrowed Ch 4, 8 and 10 items in 1.2–1.3 · **Rules:** R1–R7, R10, U1, U6, U10, U11, U13.

## 1. Chapter function

- **Central question:** How did an ordinary village in Basoda tahsil come to hold the remains, records and memories of a far more important place, and what kind of royal act stands at its beginning?
- **Argument:** An ordinary village lives inside a Paramāra king's town. Its remains, records and memories all reach back to one eleventh-century act of naming, by Udayāditya, Bhoja's brother (U11). The evidence leaves open whether that act founded the place or took it over. The chapter sets out the polity in outline (Ch 4 tells it in full), the layers of rule to 1784–85, how the place is known, and the open question.
- **Establishes for later chapters:**
  - the village today (census 2011, the walled town, the time-keepers, Berni Mata, the fair);
  - the polity and the crisis, the kinship, the triad of names;
  - the governance terms;
  - U1 (Rājyapāla and Lūṇapasāka under the Caulukyas);
  - the later sequence to the Shahi Masjid and the brass cover;
  - the praśasti's slabs, the 1080 readings and the first readers;
  - the survival legends (told, not tested);
  - the founded-or-renamed question, with the Bhujariya Talab, the eighth-century inscription and Record A.

  Later chapters recall these in clauses only (Part I, I.4).

## 2. Arc

The present puzzle (1.1) → what the place was: a king's town on the eastern edge of the Paramāra kingdom (1.2) → how it changed across eight centuries (1.3) → how it is known (1.4) → the question the evidence leaves open, ending on the stone and water both answers stand on (1.5, into Ch 2).

The scale moves from the village to the kingdom and back to the ground.

**Opening:** “On the road from Ganj Basoda, hillocks begin to dot the horizon almost ten kilometres out, and two of them belong to Udaypur; nearer the village, one proves to be the big cylindrical hill known for its shape as Shiv pindi.”

**Closing:** “Whoever first lived here, under his rule or before it, had to make terms with that stone and that water.”
