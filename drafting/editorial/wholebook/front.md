# Udaypur: The Complete Book Outline, Chapter 1 to the Afterword

*16 October 2026. One outline for the whole book. It supersedes, for planning, the Chapter 4–Afterword outline v2 (`Chapter_4_to_Afterword_Master_Drafting_Outline_v2.md`), which is kept unchanged as the record. The changes are listed in `OUTLINE_WHOLEBOOK_CHANGES.md`.*

## Purpose

This outline sets out, for every chapter of the Udaypur history, what each section establishes, from which evidence items, and under which book-level rules.
- **Chapters 1–4 are written.** Their entries are derived from the text as written: its sections, its paragraphs and the evidence each paragraph cites.
- **Chapters 5 to the Afterword are planned.**

Both are given in one format, so the book can be read as a single design. Only the status table shows which chapters are finished.

Every direct item in the two evidence maps has one of three statuses (Appendix):
- assigned to a paragraph;
- a cross-reference to its home elsewhere;
- excluded, with a reason.

The outline was built without paid model calls or vector-store queries; its checks are free scripts.

## How to read this document

- **Part I** holds everything book-wide:
  - the user's decisions, U1–U13, dated;
  - the evidence rules, R1–R11;
  - the homes of the recurring conflicts and of the shared evidence;
  - the boundaries between chapters;
  - the length decisions;
  - the retired material;
  - the decisions still needed;
  - the resolution log.

  Chapter entries cite these by number and never restate them.
- **Part II** has one entry per chapter:
  - function;
  - arc, with the opening and closing moves;
  - sections, each with its statement, its paragraphs (title, point, and evidence line) and its transition;
  - conclusion;
  - evidence checklist;
  - the chapter's own research tasks;
  - cross-chapter notes.

  A section heading gives its scope and target, and for a written chapter also its length as written.
- **Evidence lines** give item IDs by role:
  - **E** establishes, in its home unit only;
  - **C** corroborates or cross-refers;
  - **X** gives context;
  - **Note** feeds a footnote.

  Tiers are in brackets: T1 is the corpus of Paramāra inscriptions (CII), T5 the scholarly monographs, T7 the Gazetteer and the surveys, T8 testimony and tradition, and T9 popular accounts.
- **Parts III–VI:**
  - the chain of chapters and the threads;
  - the drafting protocol;
  - the single Research Register;
  - the continuity checklist.
- **The Appendix** gives each chapter's coverage by sub-topic and its exclusion list.

## The book's through-line

The book begins in an ordinary village and asks how it came to hold the remains, records and memories of a far more important place (Ch 1). It then follows the evidence outward and back:
- **The land** gave the town its stone and made it build its water, without deciding anything (Ch 2).
- **The country** was old, named and carved long before, but its past reaches the hill only as proposals, and by the tenth century its range was a crowded frontier (Ch 3).
- **The founder.** A Paramāra house took that frontier, and Bhoja's brother Udayāditya, recovering the kingdom after a three-sided war, put his name on a town, a temple and a tank. The record makes the name and the temple certain by 1080; it does not make the town his creation (Ch 4).
- **The building and the writing.** The book reads the temple for its own making (Ch 5) and the inscriptions for who made them speak (Ch 6).
- **The rest of the place:**
  - the town's other works, read as layers more than as a calendar (Ch 7);
  - the gifts that kept the god (Ch 8);
  - the stories the town tells of itself (Ch 9).
- **The later centuries.** Udaypur did not fall into ruin. It lost rank in steps under successive masters, the Caulukya kings of Gujarat among the first (Ch 10).
- **The modern record.** The place was described, collected and protected in pieces from 1871 to 2021 (Ch 11), and it lives on as a working village in an old town, seen mostly by others (Ch 12).

The Afterword turns, once and labelled, from history to argument: a place that survived by being used is best conserved as a lived-in town and hill.

Two questions run open through the whole book: whether Udayāditya founded Udaypur or renamed an older place, and how far a king's claims in stone can be taken at their word.
