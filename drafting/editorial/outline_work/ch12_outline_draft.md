## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** How do the people of Udaypur live with a thousand-year-old town that others visit as a monument, and whose account of that life does the evidence give us?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** Udaypur is not a site with a village beside it but a town lived *inside* its own fabric: houses are built on and out of ruins, the temple is a working shrine, the hill is grazing ground, quarry and habitat. The everyday uses that conservation calls "encroachment" or "pressure" are the same uses that kept the place inhabited. The intervention is twofold: (1) to describe the present as a continuation of the long story of reuse the book has told, not as a coda of decline; (2) to show, frankly, that the modern record speaks *about* residents far more than *for* them, so that the book's account of "local indifference" is an outsider's judgement, to be attributed and weighed rather than adopted.
- **What changes over the chapter:** It moves from the house (people inside the fabric) to the fields and quarries (how the village earns), to the hill (a living environment under the same pressures), to the temple's visitors (the Udaypur outsiders see), and finally to the 2021 lane where both Udaypurs met and residents' choices became visible. Intellectually it moves from description to a question of voice: the reader ends knowing what residents did, what outsiders said about them, and how little residents' own words survive.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–11:**
  - *Builds on:* 1.1 (the village under the temple: census, gates, lanes, shops, houses in dry masonry, the Ghadiyali family, worship, the fair, the name); 2.3 (the hill's forest, grazing, *van tulsī*, caves) and 2.5 (a town of its own stone; Govind Saxena's report of illegal quarries); 7 (the palace, gates, tanks as structures); 8 (the temple as a living cult, Śivarātri and Sāvan Mondays, C08-PANDE-011); 9 (communities, languages, oral tradition); 10 (reuse under successive regimes); 11 (ASI clearance of 1972; the 2021 palace private-property episode and eviction orders; INTACH's documentation; state spending on four protected monuments; image theft).
  - *Must NOT repeat:* the 1.1 tour (census 6,383 / 1,247 houses / literacy; gates and Teen Darwaza; shops in the lanes; the twelve-courtyard house; Ghariyālon Kā Makān; the water tally; Berni Mata; the fair; "Udaipur in Rajasthan"); 2.3's plant list and grazing sentence; 2.5's quarry paragraph; Ch 11's palace and eviction narrative; Ch 9's community composition, green cloths and *mazār* prophecy.
- **What it must establish for the Afterword:** (a) that the town, the hill and the temple are inhabited and used, so any proposal must deal with people's houses, fields, shrines and livelihoods; (b) that residents did act when asked (the lane) and hoped for trade from visitors; (c) that the record's harshest judgements on residents are outsiders' testimony; (d) the evidence horizon (c. 2021). The Afterword takes up "what should be done, and by whom" without re-describing any of this.

## 2. Chapter Arc

Opening on one house → 12.1 living inside the fabric (the house, the numbers across seventy years, the ruins become homes) → 12.2 work: fields, the market town, the quarries → 12.3 the hill as a living environment (bats, vultures, birds; the 1968 shooting block; grazing, pilgrims, trekkers, blasts) → 12.4 the temple's Udaypur: worshippers, officials and the first walkers in the lanes → 12.5 the lane of 2021 and the question of who speaks for the town → close on residents' own hopes, handing the "what next" to the Afterword.

*Why this order:* it begins at the smallest scale (a household), widens to the village economy and then to the hill, then turns to the outsiders' view and finally lets the two views meet in one dated episode. Putting the wildlife third (not last) keeps the hill inside the chapter's argument about use and pressure rather than letting it read as a natural-history appendix; putting the lane last gives the chapter a dated event to end on and the Afterword a concrete hinge. The alternative order of the old canon (opening on Tiwari's "kasba on the margin") was rejected: it would make the chapter open on an outsider's lament and repeat 1.1's approach.

*Scale shifts:* household (12.1) → village and *tahsīl* economy, Ganj Basoda *mandi* (12.2) → the hill and the district's 1968 forest ranges (12.3) → Bhopal and the district officials (12.4) → back to one lane (12.5).

*Chronology:* runs only in 12.1 (1901 → 1950–51 → 1963–64 → 1971 → 2011) and in 12.3 (1948 → 1968 → 1979 → the survey). 12.4–12.5 are anchored in a dated cluster (January–March 2021). Elsewhere chronology is suspended; the present is "at the last record".

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur in the early 2020s was a working village living inside and on top of its medieval town and hill, that the temple's visitors and the town's residents rarely met, and that when residents did act on their heritage the record mostly describes them through outsiders' eyes.

**Opening move:** A family's house in Udaypur that had lain buried in an excavation decades earlier, with fine old carving, was cleaned out "with devotion and effort" and is now their home (C12-JAGTA-HI-014, p. 73). Chapter 11 ends (by its own plan) with official and institutional attention arriving in 2021; Chapter 12 opens on the opposite scale, a household that had already made the old town its home without any department. The first paragraph must not restage 1.1's approach to the village; it begins inside.

### 12.1 Living inside the old town *(the settlement, 1901 to c. 2021 · ~750 w)*

- **Purpose:** Establish the present settlement as the latest layer of a town that has been continuously lived in and remade, without re-touring 1.1.

### 12.3 The living hill *(wildlife, grazing, pilgrims and blasts · ~700 w)*
- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the hill above Udaypur is a habitat as well as a monument: its caves hold thousands of bats over deep guano, it shelters vultures and, by the survey's count, more than 58 bird species with winter migrants on the water; that in 1968 a forest shooting block named "Udaipura" still reported tiger, sambar and chital; and that the same hill is grazed, climbed by pilgrims and trekkers, and shaken by quarry blasts.
- **Purpose:** Carry the wildlife material moved from Chapter 2 (14 Oct 2026) as part of the living environment, and show that the hill's non-human residents share the pressures of 12.2.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** From the caves (bats) to the sky (vultures, birds) to the past half-century (the 1968 table and the decline of game) to the hill's human uses today. The hill is not a ruin in empty country; it is a used and inhabited place.

## 4. Chapter Conclusion
- **What has been demonstrated:**
  - Udaypur at the last record (c. 2021) was a growing village of farmers, traders and quarry workers living inside and on top of its medieval town; its numbers roughly trebled after 1971.
  - Its hill is a habitat for bats and vultures as well as a grazed, quarried and visited monument.
  - Its temple draws worshippers and officials who rarely enter the town.
  - When the lane to the temple was widened, residents gave ground, rebuilt and hoped for visitors.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - What residents themselves think. The corpus has no interviews; the old canon's gap note (interviews with residents, the ASI custodian, the panchayat, the priests) stands.
  - Present services and livelihoods data after 1979.
  - Whether the 1968 "Udaipura" shooting block included the hill.
  - What has happened since 2021.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** Conservation here has advanced by residents' consent and at their cost, but has been planned and narrated by outsiders. Can a plan for Udaypur be made with its residents rather than about them?
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content (and what it must not do):** It ends on the two sides of one hope: residents expecting visitors and trade, outsiders drawing up plans to bring them. The Afterword opens on those plans. It must not summarise the chapter, preview the Afterword, ask a rhetorical question, or end on a lament ("waiting to wake", "gasping") borrowed from the sources.
