# Chapter 8. Religion and the Material Life of Udaypur: Ritual, Agriculture, Trade and Craft
## Jagta Hua Kasba / कथा उदयपुर — English translation (JAGTA-EN, 3)
In some period, some craftsman had carved deities out of stones for some king. Priests had sung their praises for centuries. Every section of society did something or other, in its own way, for their devotion. The gardeners must have tended those gardens from where fresh flowers were brought every morning for the adornment of Neelkanth.
C08-JAGTA-EN-003   ≈ p. 88 (Hindi original)
Santosh Kumar Verma tells us that the provisions of the *akshayanivi* (permanent-endowment) system for the management of religious institutions appear in the Satavahana period. Evidence of their extensive allotment of agricultural land, too, begins to be found — the first mention of which is in the inscriptions of Gautamiputra Satakarni and in the accounts of the Chutu rulers, who gave such donations to the *viharas* of Nagarjunakonda and Amaravati. In the Gupta period their number began to grow, where various Smriti-writers and text-authors mentioned *akshayanivi* in the form of wealth and, in later times, *nidhi*, *nyasa* and *nikshepa*. In the south, too, it was in vogue. After the Gupta period, land-donations were given to temples on a large scale — and that with special privileges such as '*sa-dandashapa-aparadha*' and '*bhutopata-pratyaya*', which was, in one way or another, indeed to some extent a transfer of the absolute exclusive rights of the royal power.
C08-JAGTA-EN-006   ≈ p. 145 (Hindi original)
Now the question: after building so vast a temple as Udaipur — whose construction went on for two decades — what could be the meaning of a king donating to the temple only such a small piece of land as 4 *biswa*? Verma tells us that this seemingly trivial land-donation is not the only example of its kind. In that era, in Rajasthan and Gujarat, in some writings of the Chauhans of Nadol and Shakambhari and of the Chalukyas of Gujarat, there are records of the donation of small parcels of land for religious works.

## The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta — Anupa Pande (PANDE, 8)
In the epics, especially the Mahābhārata, the cult of Paśupati is one of the five major cults. Paśupati combines in himself the god who not only creates the world, but transcends it, and yet who is the teacher of all spiritual aspirants. Just as Bhāgvatism attracted foreign rulers, so also the Śaiva cult. Wima Kadphises is known to be a devotee of Māheśvara. In the Gupta period, Śaivism is one of the major religious cults. The Purāṇas which are generally held to have been compiled in the Gupta age contain Śaiva mythology and theology in many versions.
C08-PANDE-001   p. 100
Between the 8th and 12th centuries, several distinct schools of Śaiva worship and philosophy came to be formulated. The commentators on Śaṅkarācārya mention four distinct Śaiva schools- Pāśupata, Māheśvara, Śaiva Siddhāntis and Kālāmukhas. Of these, it is the Śaiva Siddhāntis who dominated Central India and the South, i.e. from Malwa to the south. In the 11th century CE, Bhoja Paramāra wrote his Tattvaprakāśa expounding the tenets of Śaiva Siddhānta.
C08-PANDE-002   p. 100   also Ch 4, Ch 5
The Pāśupatas, who were perhaps the most ancient Śaiva cult, are described in the Vedānta Sūtras as clearly dualistic. God is the lord or pati, man is the paśu who is bound by pāśa or fetters. When man is freed from these bonds by the grace of god, he attains a state which is god-like but he does not become god or Śiva himself.
C08-PANDE-003   p. 100
The sources of Pāśupata philosophy are *Pāśupata Sūtras*, *Pañcārthabhāṣya* of Kaundinya, the *Vāyavīyasaṃhitā* of the *Śiva Purāṇa*, *Gaṇakārikā* attributed to Haradatta and its commentary *Ratnaṭīkā* of Bhāsarvajña, and the *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* of Mādhavācārya.
The Pāśupatas desired liberation to give them a condition like that of god. This is possible by a knowledge of the five objects or *pañcārtha*. These are *kāraṇa* (cause), *kārya* (effect), *yoga* (spiritual praxis), *vidhi* (religious rituals) and *dukhānta* (cessation of sorrow).
