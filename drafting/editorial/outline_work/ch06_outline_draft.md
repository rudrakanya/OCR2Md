## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** What do Udaypur's inscriptions say, in what language and form, and who made them say it? What can these words establish about authority, patronage and learning, and what can they not?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** The stones are several archives, each with a different maker, register and reliability:
  - the short working records of named *paṇḍita*s (1080, 1094);
  - the king's poem of descent;
  - a grammar-serpent that turns the alphabet into a royal weapon;
  - officials' and governors' grants (1163–1232);
  - pilgrims' notes in local speech (V.S. 1696);
  - Persian building records of a new order (1336–1632).

  Learning at Udaypur is evidenced as something *displayed and used* by rulers and their literate servants. It is not evidence of a school or a court at Udaypur. "Intellectual life" is tied to the place's own texts; Bhoja's Dhārā is the frame, not the subject.
- **What changes over the chapter:** The reader moves from the inscription as a *source of facts* (Chs 1, 4) to the inscription as an *act*: composed, commissioned, placed, altered. Narratively, the language of authority moves from Sanskrit verse, to Sanskrit prose, to Persian and local Hindi on the same stones.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–5:** It builds on 1.4 (the *praśasti* as object, the 1080 readings, the pilgrims, the first readers, the reset slab), 1.2–1.3 (grants, Devapāla, the mosques) and Ch 4 (the *praśasti*'s Bhoja verses as event-claims). It **must not repeat**:
  - the agnikula;
  - the unconfirmed names;
  - the slabs' find-story;
  - the 1059/1080 argument;
  - Beglar/Cunningham/Bühler;
  - the survival legends;
  - the conquest catalogue as history;
  - Devapāla's residence.

  Each is recalled in a clause at most.
- **What it must establish for Chapter 7:** The texts that date buildings outside the temple: Amērā, V.S. 1151 [C06-CII-011]; the mosque behind the temple, A.H. 737–39 [C10-MADHYABHARAT-006]; the bilingual mosque of 1488 [C07-MADHYABHARAT-012]; the Shahi Masjid, A.H. 1041 [C07-MADHYABHARAT-011]. Chapter 7 then dates and describes the buildings without re-arguing the words.

## 2. Chapter Arc

The signed six lines of 1080 at the east door (6.1) → the king's poem of descent (6.2) → the serpentine scimitar of letters (6.3) → the porch archive, 1163–1639, where the register falls from verse to prose to local speech (6.4) → Persian as a new language of authority, and the limits of an archive largely unpublished (6.5).

**Why this order:**
- Chapter 5 ends inside the temple, and the 1080 record is the temple's own dated, signed voice. Opening there poses the chapter's question (who wrote, for whom, in what form) through an object.
- The *praśasti* then arrives as a contrast in scale. The serpent shares its world and turns "learning" from a claim into a made thing.
- A purely chronological arc would scatter the founding generation's three learned texts. A purely language-based arc would set the *praśasti* beside the 1229 grant as "Sanskrit" and lose the change of register that is the chapter's finding.

**Scale:** a doorway → the dynasty → four royal Śiva temples (Ujjain, Dhār, Ūn, Udaypur) → the town and its villages → Delhi, Mandu and the Mughal court.

**Chronology:** Suspended in 6.1–6.3 (one generation, c. 1070–c. 1100, arranged by kind of text). It runs in 6.4 (1163–1639) and 6.5 (1336–1632).

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur's inscriptions are several archives made by different hands for different ends. They show kings, officials and *paṇḍita*s using Sanskrit learning to make authority visible, and a new order later writing itself onto the same stones in Persian. They establish claims, gifts, names and dates, but not a school, a court or a reading public in the town.

**Opening move:** Chapter 5 ends inside the Udayeśvara (editor: confirm its last line). Chapter 6 opens at the east door, on the six lines of V.S. 1137 to its right:
- the wish that Udayāditya make the earth "one umbrella";
- the statement that Udayeśvara's flag-hoisting "is complete";
- the name of the verse's maker, Paṇḍita Mahipāla, son of Paṇḍita Śṛṅgavāsa [C06-CII-006; C06-JAGTA-HI-008; C06-JAGTA-HI-009].

The first paragraph's work is the *writer*: the temple's first dated words carry an author's name. The date's readings are recalled from 1.4 in a clause.

## 3. Sections

### 6.1 Six lines at the door *(the 1080 record, its writer, and the other early writing on this ground · ~800 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the temple's first dated words are a short, mostly prose Sanskrit record that names its own poet: Paṇḍita Mahipāla, son of Paṇḍita Śṛṅgavāsa. Within a generation, verse was also being composed and cut for a Brāhmaṇa's tank 3 km away, by a *paṇḍita* whose son engraved it. Court Sanskrit of a far higher polish had already been carved in the province, at Vidisha. The reader also knows that the V.S. 1116 text, the other "founding" record, is by its readers' account a poor and error-filled one, and that this matters for what it can carry.
- **Purpose:** Begin "intellectual life" with people, not with Bhoja: name the literate men whom Udaypur's own early records name, and set the registers side by side.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** From a single signed record at the door, out to the nearest other early writing (Amērā, 1094) and the province's court verse (Chhittapa at Vidisha), and back to the V.S. 1116 text. The section establishes a scale of literacy, from polished court poetry to working records, before the praśasti (6.2) shows the top of it.

### 6.2 A poem of descent *(the Udaypur praśasti read as composition and claim · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows how the *praśasti* works as a poem. It is Sanskrit verse throughout after one obeisance, it opens with benedictions to Śiva, and it builds each ancestor out of images and puns. It makes learning a royal virtue, rising to "Bhoja the poet-king" and to Udayāditya as "another sun". The reader also knows what its editors credit and doubt, and why it is set at Udaypur. Who composed it is not known.
- **Purpose:** Do the work 1.4 deferred: read the text's language, metre and rhetoric. Show that its value for intellectual history lies in what it *claims* about kingship and learning, not in the events it lists (Ch 4).
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** From the object (recalled) to the verse: form, then rhetoric, then authorship and its comparanda, then the critical reading, then its placement. The section ends on what the poem cannot tell.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — Form.** **Point:** Establish the text's physical and literary form from CII, recalling the two slabs in a clause (1.4). The language is Sanskrit. Apart from a short opening obeisance to Śiva, the whole is metrical. Twenty-two unnumbered verses survive on the first slab, and the inscription breaks off ten syllables into the next. The second slab's 27 lines are largely obliterated. The script is eleventh-century Nāgarī, sharing a hooked head-stroke with other records of the time. The record is undated and its purpose is lost; the editor takes it as a *praśasti* "intended to be put in a part of the temple", which is where such poems were usually set.

### 6.3 A serpentine scimitar of letters *(the grammar-serpent as a learned royal object · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that a slab from the courtyard wall carries the whole Sanskrit sound-system and the endings of nouns and verbs, laid out in the body of a serpent shaped as a sword. Its verse calls it a "serpentine scimitar of letters". Identical charts at Ujjain, Dhār and Ūn tie it to Udayāditya and Naravarman in the late eleventh century. Scholars disagree whether such charts taught boys grammar or served as emblems of a king who protected "letters and social order"; at Udaypur the evidence favours the emblem.
- **Purpose:** The chapter's centrepiece: the one Udaypur object in which learning itself is the content. Tie Bhoja-era grammar culture to Udaypur through this object, not through a survey of Bhoja.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Object → its words → its family of four → the dispute over its use → the court learning it advertises, kept to one frame paragraph.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The diagram.** **Point:** Describe it as SERPENT does, as a text to be read top to bottom. The hood holds the fourteen vowels in short and long pairs. The neck and knotted body hold the semivowels and the five classes of stops, and one leg holds the sibilants. *Ha* stands at both ends of the consonant series, as in Pāṇini's Śiva-sūtras. The base holds three aspirate signs, but not the *anusvāra*. In the tail, triads give 21 noun endings rising and 18 present-tense verb endings, active and middle, falling. Recall in a clause that this is the slab 1.4 mentioned as reset in restoration. The block belonged to the parapet but originally faced into the courtyard.

  2. **P2 — The words beside it.** **Point:** The short inscriptions name and empower the diagram. The palaeography matches local records of the late eleventh century: inch-high, rectilinear letters whose verticals bend sharply right. The orthography shows the period's habits (*va* for *ba*, *sa* for *śa*, a doubled consonant after *r*).

### 6.4 The porch archive *(grants, governors and pilgrims, 1163–1639 · ~800 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the pillars and seats of the three porches hold a long series of records: a dozen or so of historical weight and many pilgrims' notes. They are listed in reports far more fully than they are published. Read as texts, the edited ones show the register falling from court verse to working prose: a governor's and a prince's gifts, a treasury officer's four *biswa* of land, a king given imperial titles that his other records do not give, the town under more than one name, and at last visitors writing in Nāgarī and their own speech.
- **Purpose:** Turn from the founding generation to the centuries. Show what kind of writing the porches hold and what it records about authority and patronage, without retelling the politics (1.3, Ch 10) or the economy of gifts (Ch 8).
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Chronological, 1163 → 1639, and also a movement down the scale of language.

Counts in the sources range from eleven or a dozen to "around a hundred" or "more than a hundred". ### 6.5 New languages of authority, and the limits of the stones *(Persian inscriptions, 1336–1632; what the archive cannot say · ~650 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that Persian came to Udaypur's stones as the language of a new order's building records: under Muhammad bin Tughluq in A.H. 737–39 (1336–38), in a bilingual Persian-and-Sanskrit record of 1488, and in the Shahi Masjid record of 1632. These texts establish who built and in whose reign; they do not establish who damaged what. The reader also knows how much of the whole archive remains unread or unpublished, and so what the chapter's conclusions cannot reach.
- **Purpose:** Decide what Ch 6 says about the Persian inscriptions *as texts*: their language, who they name, what they can and cannot carry. Leave the buildings (Ch 7) and the politics (Ch 10) to their chapters. End on the limits of the evidence.

## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:** Udaypur's inscriptions are several archives, each with its own maker and purpose. They comprise a signed six-line record of a completed rite (1080); a king's poem of descent that makes learning a royal virtue; a grammar-serpent that turns the alphabet into a royal emblem shared with Ujjain, Dhār and Ūn; a porch of terse grants and claims (1163–1232) and pilgrims' notes in Nāgarī and local speech (V.S. 1696); and Persian building records of the sultans and emperors (1336–1632). Learned culture at Udaypur is evidenced as display and service: *paṇḍita* families writing to order, kings advertising their learning, officials recording gifts.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - who composed the *praśasti*;
  - whether the V.S. 1116 text is the late record its critics describe;
  - whether the serpent charts were ever used for teaching;
  - what the unpublished Gwalior and ARIEp lists hold;
  - the texts of the Persian inscriptions;
  - whether the 1163 and V.S. 1220 records are one;
  - the lost V.S. 1311 stone;
  - the hill's undeciphered lines.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** The same stones carry Sanskrit and Persian. The Persian ones stand on buildings set against, beside and outside the temple. What those buildings were, and how the town's built fabric changed around the temple, is a question about structures, not words.
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content (and what it must not do):** The last paragraph ends on the mosques and gates that carry the Persian inscriptions: the mosque built against the temple's rear, the bilingual mosque outside the eastern gate, the Shahi Masjid to the east. These are buildings with dates but not yet descriptions, which is Chapter 7's ground. It must not announce Chapter 7, summarise Chapter 6, describe the mosques' architecture, or narrate the Tughluq conquest (Ch 10).

### Research / Verification Flags

1. **Old-canon porch dates are absent from every map.** The 1323–24 votive, the 1338 procession, the pilgrims of 1377 and 1446, and the 13 January 1645 slab "in three eras" are all dropped. They can return only if they turn up after ingest of the Gwalior reports or ARIEp. Ch 7 should not use the 1645 Qanungo Baoli slab either.
2. **The old canon's "1310" record** is very probably Ganguly's "Sam. 1366 = 1309" Jayasiṃha record, inside the east entrance (the OCR prints "1909") [C06-GANGULY-032; C06-GANGULY-037]. It comes from a tier-5 handlist with no edition. Wording: "Ganguly lists an inscription naming Jayasiṃha as ruling in 1309."
3. **The gap "No edited text in the corpus" is now only partly true.** CII edits the *praśasti*, the V.S. 1137 record, Amērā and the two Devapāla records. Everything else is known from studies or lists. Bühler (EI I), Kielhorn (IA), the Gwalior reports of 1923–29 and ARIEp are "to be added after ingest" only.
4. **"-pāla" at Amērā and Mahipāla.** Both names are four *akṣara*s ending in *-pāla* [C06-CII-011; C06-JAGTA-HI-008]. They are compatible, not identical, so the draft must not hint at a link.
5. **"In the fort (*gaḍh*)"** [C06-JAGTA-EN-035; C06-JAGTA-HI-039] contradicts the pillar by the east door [C06-JAGTA-HI-032]. It is probably a misreading of *gadya*, "prose". Check the Hindi page image before writing "prose".
6. **The V.S. 1116 text** [C06-JAGTA-EN-011]: 22 lines, full of errors. Ganguly calls his 1513 record "perfunctorily executed" [C01-GANGULY-020], and CII rejects a sixteenth-century genealogy [C01-CII-014]. These are compatible but not identified. Pānāheḍā also bears V.S. 1116 [C06-CII-022; C06-PARTEMPLES-008]; keep that coincidence out of the text.
7. **Adhikari** calls the six-line 1080 record a *praśasti* with construction and consecration details [C06-PARTEMPLES-003]. Note only.
8. **SERPENT's "temple and tank … founded … in 1080"** [C06-SERPENT-009] is not adopted; canon keeps the founding open. It goes in a note as Singh's dating, if at all.
9. **SERPENT line counts.** The study gives both "5 lines" [C06-SERPENT-002] and "these two lines" [C06-SERPENT-005]. Write "short inscriptions, one a two-line verse".
10. **Chhittapa's date.** The Gazetteer's "V.S. 935 (A.D. 992)" [C06-GAZ-004] is inconsistent with itself, and CII gives "Date lost?" [C06-CII-048]. Give no date.
11. **Conflicts from the map's §5:**
    - inscription counts (11 to 100+): range in a note;
    - Lūṇapasāka 1171, 1172 or 1173: "early 1170s", with the Gazetteer's attribution to Udayāditya [C06-GAZ-001] noted as an error;
    - the *praśasti*'s date and reliability: CII's "undated" outranks GEOHER's 1059 [C06-GEOHER-001] and Ganguly's c. 1072;
    - temple founding/consecration: deferred to 1.4 and Ch 4;
    - Udayāditya's reign and kinship, and the town founder: Ch 4.
12. **Kumārapāla, 1163** [C06-GANGULY-034] and **V.S. 1220** [C10-PARTEMPLES-001] are probably one record, but no item says so. Cite both, with a note.
13. **"Arabic"** appears only in Tiwari [C06-JAGTA-HI-019]. The old canon's "four languages" is unsupported.
14. **Turasadasa and Ustonatha** [C06-GEOHER-007; C06-GEOHER-008] share the same place and date. They may be one record read twice or two records; do not merge them. GEOHER's "500 years ago" [C06-GEOHER-006] is wrong for V.S. 1696.
15. **Verse numbers of the *praśasti*** vary between authors, so give none. **ROADMAP:** a Sanskrit reader for Bühler's transcript would let 6.2 quote the edition directly.
16. **Graded wording:**
    - serpent date: "probably in the last decade of the eleventh century";
    - charts: "have been read as teaching charts";
    - the *praśasti*'s author: "is not known";
    - placement: "it may be no accident that …";
    - 1336–38: "record the mosque's building in the reign of …", never "record the destruction".
