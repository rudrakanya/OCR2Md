- MADHYABHARAT, Patil, *Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat* (1952; tier 5): the only systematic mid-century account of the six principal monuments (C07-MADHYABHARAT-022).

- JAGTA-EN / JAGTA-HI, Tiwari (tier 8): 104 items. Testimony of what is standing and remembered; never a measurement, date or count.

**What is thin:** no excavation; no dated inscription on any fortification, gate, palace, shrine (other than the Sun-praising fragment on the Bijamandal) or stepwell at Udaypur in the maps; the survey's period column assigns "11th Century" to nearly every gate, house and stepwell, which is a blanket proposal, not a finding. The only contemporary record of a Paramāra-period tank (C07-CII-003) belongs to a tank about 3 km south of the village, not to any tank in the survey lists. Normative texts (SAMAR, RAJABHOJ-EN, BHOJDEV-HI, BHOJA-TIMES: 83 items) describe what a fort or town *should* be and are never evidence for Udaypur's buildings.

## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** What do Udaypur's buildings other than the great temple (the wall on the hill, the town's gates and palace, the lesser shrines, the mosques and tombs, the tanks and stepwells) record about who held the town, when, and for what, and how much of that can actually be dated?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** Udaypur's built fabric is a sequence of layers whose order is clearer than its dates. Only the Muslim buildings carry dated inscriptions (1336–38, 1488, 1632). Everything else is dated by survey proposal, style or tradition, and the survey's blanket "11th century" hides real differences of build. Read as evidence, the buildings show (a) a fortified hill whose wall may be older than Udayāditya or may be his, and which no record names; (b) a walled town with a palace enclosure used and rebuilt "for centuries with different rulers"; (c) shrine-building that went on after the eleventh century; (d) mosques set first into the temple court, then outside the town gate, then beside a governor's mansion, each marking a different relation between rulers and town; (e) a water system of more than one age whose single securely documented tank is not the one tradition credits to the founder.
- **What changes over the chapter:** The reader begins with Chapter 1's picture of a walled town and a remembered tally of water, and ends able to say which buildings are dated by inscriptions, which by fabric, which only by proposal or memory, and what each layer implies about the town's changing masters. Narratively the chapter descends from the hilltop to the town's gates, through its shrines and mosques, to its water, and moves forward in time inside each section.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–6:**
  - Builds on 1.1 (walled city, Teen Darwaza, gates, houses pressed against the gateways, the water tally as memory), 1.3 (the 1336–38 mosque; Sher Khan's agent's mosque, 1488; Shahi Masjid, 1632; the governor's mansion; the palace-founder note), 1.5 (Bhujariya Talab, reused columns, the uncemented wall as the case for an older place), 2.4 (water at landscape level: *kund*s between settlement and tank; the Pindi Hill stepwell; the list's eleventh- and seventeenth-century proposals).
  - Must NOT repeat: the tally of 56/52/12½; the snake legend; the grinder-woman legend (1.4); the mosque's four rows of twelve pillars and *mihrāb* (1.3); the 1336–38 dates, the north-west/north-east note and the Aurangzeb legend (1.3); the farman/claimant dispute over the mansion (1.3 note); the Bhujariya Talab's 8th–9th-century proposal and the reused 7th–8th-century columns as an argument for an older place (1.5); the Pindi Hill stepwell's three sides and twenty steps (2.4); the twelve-courtyard house (1.1 = C07-INTACH-021).
  - From Chapter 4 (not yet drafted; per `chapters.md`, Paramāra Udaypur): the town plan as a Paramāra creation. Chapter 7 treats the plan only through the standing gates and wall; Tiwari's grid sketch (C07-JAGTA-EN-031) stays with Ch 4.
  - From Chapter 5: the temple and its court. Chapter 7 enters the court only for the mosque's fabric.
  - From Chapter 6: the inscriptions. Chapter 7 uses an inscription only as a dating fact for a building.
- **What it must establish for Chapter 8 (Religion and the material life):** that the town's water was built and used for drinking, fields, bathing and rites; that shrines of several faiths and many dates stood in the town; and that tanks and stepwells were works of merit that kings, Brahmins and villagers alike paid for. Chapter 8 can then ask what was done in and around these buildings.

## 2. Chapter Arc

**Opening → close.**
- 7.1 starts on the highest and least documented work, the wall on the hill: its fabric, its gate and bastions, what lies inside it, and why no one can yet date it, ending on the lichen proposal.
- 7.2 comes down to the walled town: its gates, the palace enclosure and the stone houses, a fabric used and rebuilt by successive holders.
- 7.3 turns to the shrines other than Udayeśvara, which show building for Śiva, the Sun, Rāma and the Jinas continuing long after the eleventh century.
- 7.4 reads the mosques and tombs as the only dated sequence in the town: the temple court (1336–38), the eastern gate (1488), the governor's quarter (1632), and the undated dargahs and graves on the hill.
- 7.5 ends on water: the hill's reservoirs and stepwells, the town's stepwells, the tanks, and the one Paramāra-period tank whose purpose a contemporary record states.

**Why this order:** It runs from the least to the most closely dated kinds of building. The undated fortification problem is set up first, and the dated mosques then supply the chapter's only fixed points. Water comes last because it spans every layer and hands over to Chapter 8's ritual and agriculture by content. A strictly chronological order is impossible: most buildings have no secure date, and arranging them by proposed date would adopt the survey's dates as fact. A tour order would duplicate 1.1. Scale shifts: Malwa's forts and mosque-building enter once each (7.1 P2, 7.4 P1) and only where they change the reading of Udaypur's own building. Chronology runs inside 7.3, 7.4 and 7.5; it is suspended in 7.1 and 7.2, where the question *is* the date.

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur's buildings beyond the temple record a long sequence of holders (a fortified hill no record names, a palace enclosure used for centuries, shrines built after Udayāditya, mosques dated 1336–38, 1488 and 1632, and water works of several ages), but that only the mosques and one tank carry dates of their own, so most of the town's built history is an order of layers, not a calendar.

**Opening move:** Chapter 6 has read the town's inscribed stones; Chapter 7 opens on the largest work at Udaypur that carries no inscription at all. A colonial-era observer, quoted but not dated in the survey (C07-GEOHER-002; probably Beglar's tour of 1871–72, but the item does not say so: name and date only after the ASR VII ingest), found on the hill south of the town a wall of huge uncemented stones with several gates, the place deserted, and "it has not even a name that I could hear of". Open on that wall and that silence: the contrast with Chapter 6's written record carries the chapter's question (what can unwritten buildings tell?). Do not give the visitor's thickness figure (see 7.1 guidance).

## 3. Sections

### 7.1 A wall without a name *(the fortified hill and its carvings · ~1,050 w; raised from ~900 for P3a)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows what the wall on the hill south of the town is (a dry-stone sandstone wall of about 1,400 m with bastions, a main gate and a rear gate, enclosing about 56 acres on the north, east and west), what lay inside it on the middle and upper levels, and why its date is open: the survey proposes both "before Udayāditya" and "in his time", its list assigns it to the tenth century, no record names it, and a study of the lichens on its stones has been proposed but not made.
- **Purpose:** Give the fabric once, from the survey, in a single set of figures; turn the old canon's "the fort predates Udayāditya" into the open question it is; place the lichen proposal (moved from Ch 2) where it belongs, as one possible route to a date.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The hill was fortified and lived on, with water, cells and a Śiva temple inside its wall; no inscription, chronicle or tradition dates or names the work. Movement: the wall seen from outside → its fabric → what it enclosed → how it might be dated.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The unnamed wall.** **Point:** Establish the object and its silence: a wall of huge uncemented stones with several gates, partly enclosing the hill south of the town, found deserted, nameless and unexplained by the townspeople when a colonial observer saw it; the 1979 Gazetteer took its traces, running about 1 km south to a rocky hill, as evidence that Udaypur is an ancient place; Patil saw the town's own fortifications continue south to join a hill whose top was also fortified. On that reading the hill's defences and the town's wall were parts of one system. Recall 1.5's "uncemented wall" in a clause, without re-arguing the older-place case.

### 7.2 Gates, palace and houses *(the walled town · ~750 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the town's named gates and what their names record; that the palace enclosure is a building of more than one period (the survey lists it as eleventh-century, compares a temple's style to it to propose the thirteenth, and describes pointed arches and iron joists inside it), and that whether it is the early-Mughal mansion Patil described is not established; and that the town's stone houses reuse older carved stone and show their layers in the walls, but cannot be dated from what has been written about them.
- **Purpose:** Read the town's secular fabric as evidence of continuous use and rebuilding by successive holders, building on 1.1 (walled city, Teen Darwaza, houses pressed against gateways) without re-describing it.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The walled town was not a single foundation preserved; it was a fabric kept in use, altered and reoccupied. Movement: gates (what the names say) → palace enclosure (one building, several periods) → houses and lanes (reuse) .
- **Paragraph plan:**

### 7.3 Shrines after the great temple *(Hindu, Jain and memorial buildings · ~700 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that shrine-building at Udaypur did not stop with Udayeśvara: the Bijamandal (probably contemporary with it, with an unfinished inscription praising the Sun), the Barakhambi hall of a lost eleventh-century temple (with a pillar inscription of V.S. 1625), the plain and later Pisanhari temple, a Rāma temple proposed as thirteenth-century, a shrine assigned by its images to the eleventh–fifteenth centuries, a Jain precinct with broken images, and memorial *chhatrī*s and sati stones; and that the survey's blanket "11th century" for the town's temples is a proposal its own text contradicts.

### 7.5 Water of many ages *(tanks, kunds and stepwells in detail · ~950 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows Udaypur's water works structure by structure, from the reservoirs and stepwells inside the hill wall, through the stepwells of the slopes and town (two of them carrying unread inscriptions), to the tanks; that the Udaysagar is identified with the inscription's Udayasamudra only by proposal; and that the one tank near Udaypur whose builder, cost and purposes a contemporary record states was a Brahmin's work of merit in Naravarman's reign.
- **Purpose:** Deliver the detail that 2.4 reserved for this chapter (`chapters.md`, 14 Oct), building on 2.4's landscape account (tanks for fields and animals, wells and stepwells for drinking, *kund*s between settlement and tank) and 1.5's dating of the Bhujariya Talab, without repeating either.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Water works were built, used and abandoned at several dates and by several kinds of patron; their purposes reached from the garrison's needs to bathing and rites for the dead. Movement: down the hill → into the town → out to the tanks → to the one written record.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — Water inside the wall.** **Point:** The fortified hill held its own water. ## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:** Udaypur's buildings beyond the temple form an order of layers: a fortified, inhabited hill; a walled town with gates named for its roads and a palace enclosure rebuilt over centuries; shrines built well after the eleventh century, Jain as well as Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava; mosques dated 1336–38, 1488 and 1632, placed in the temple court, at the town gate and in a governor's quarter; water works of several ages. The mosques and Vikrama's tank are the only works dated by their own inscriptions.
- **What remains unresolved:** the hill wall's date and builder (pre-Paramāra or Udayāditya's); whether the Raja Mahal and Patil's Mughal mansion are one building; whether the listed Moti Masjid is Sher Khan's mosque; whether the Udaysagar is the Udayasamudra; the readings of the stepwell inscriptions and of the Bijamandal fragment; the lichen study.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** the buildings show who built and rebuilt, but not what was done in them; the town's water and shrines were built for rites, fields and trade.
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content:** it ends on Vikrama's tank and its stated uses (bathing, worship, offerings to ancestors, water as merit), material that Chapter 8 takes up as ritual and agriculture. It must not summarise the chapter, announce Chapter 8, or close on an aphorism about stone and water (1.5 already ends that way).
