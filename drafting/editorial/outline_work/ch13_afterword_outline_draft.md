## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** Given what the book has shown about how Udaypur was made, used, remade and recorded, what would conserving it mean, who would have to do it, and which of the proposals on record fit the place as it actually is?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** The Afterword is the one place where the book argues in its own voice, and it says so. Its argument comes out of the book's evidence rather than from the sources' enthusiasm:
  - Udaypur survived nine centuries not by being protected but by being **used**: worshipped in (Chs 1, 8), reused (Ch 10), lived in (Ch 12).
  - The modern protection that saved the temple also **separated** it from its town and hill (Ch 11).
  - A future worth the name must therefore conserve the **town and hill as inhabited places**, with residents as participants rather than obstacles, and must close the jurisdictional gap that leaves the hill, the wall and the palace outside the protection the temple enjoys.
  - The proposals on record are sorted, not endorsed. Some are within reach and need little money (translations of inscriptions, guides from the village, clearing, monitoring). Others would require clearances that the 2021 lane shows to fall on residents (corridor-style schemes).
- **What changes over the Afterword:**
  - It moves from the end of the record (what we last know) to what is at stake, then to the proposals, then to the people who would carry them out, and finally to the book's own case.
  - Intellectually it turns from history to judgement, and keeps the two visibly apart.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–12 (callbacks named; what it must NOT repeat):**
  - *Callbacks (each a clause or a sentence, never a retelling):*
    - 1.1, the village under the temple (the book's first image, returned to at the close);
    - 1.4, the survival legends and how Udaypur is known;
    - 1.5 and Ch 4, the founding question still open;
    - 2.3/2.5, a town of its own stone, and quarrying;
    - Ch 5, the temple as the fullest Bhūmija statement;
    - Ch 6, inscriptions read by scholars but not displayed;
    - Ch 7, the wall, tanks and palace, and the lichen proposal;
    - Ch 10, reuse under successive regimes;
    - Ch 11, protection and its gaps;
    - Ch 12, the two Udaypurs and the 2021 lane.
  - *Must NOT repeat:*
    - Ch 11's narrative of the INTACH survey, the palace private-property episode, the eviction order, the state spending on four monuments, the image theft, and the listing of each threat;
    - Ch 12's lane-widening, visitors' habits and wildlife;
    - any chapter's conclusions in summary form.
- **What it must establish (as the book's close):**
  - a clear statement of where the evidence ends;
  - a sorted view of the proposals;
  - an allocation of roles grounded in what each party has actually done in the record;
  - a labelled argument.

  The final paragraph closes the book by content, on the village and the temple together.

## 2. Chapter Arc

AW.1 where the record stops (dated, flat) → AW.2 what is at stake and who answers for which part (the protection map) → AW.3 the proposals on record, sorted by scale and by who bears the cost → AW.4 roles: residents, professionals, researchers, administrators, visitors, each tied to evidence of what they have done → AW.5 the argument, labelled, ending on the village under the temple.

- **Why this order:**
  - Fixing the evidence horizon first prevents any later sentence from implying a current condition.
  - The protection map has to precede the proposals, because most proposals are answers to the gap it shows.
  - Roles come after proposals, because proposals without agents are the record's commonest failure: Tiwari's own complaint, C13-JAGTA-HI-022.
  - The argument comes last and is the only part in the book's advocating voice.
  - The old canon's order (deterioration, mining, gap, proposals, argument) was rejected: its first three sections now belong to Chapter 11 (chapters.md moved the conservation history there) and would duplicate it.
- **Where scale shifts:**
  - AW.2 moves between the temple precinct, the hill and the district departments.
  - AW.3 moves from a signboard at the temple to a statewide Paramāra circuit.
  - AW.5 moves from nine centuries back to one village.
- **Chronology:** runs only in AW.1 (2019 → January–March 2021 → the survey → Mishra's death and the plan in his memory). It is suspended elsewhere.

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows where the record of Udaypur stops, which parts of the town and hill were protected and which were not at that point, which proposals are realistic and who would have to carry them out, and why the book argues that Udaypur should be conserved as a lived-in town and hill rather than as a temple with surroundings.

**Opening move:** The letter Tiwari wrote in 2019 to a Collector of Vidisha, asking that the history "surviving alive in this town" be looked after and proposing work on the model of the Kashi Vishwanath corridor (C13-JAGTA-EN-001). It takes up Chapter 12's last turn: residents hoping for visitors, outsiders drawing up plans. It is the earliest dated proposal in the record, and it carries the Afterword's whole question: conservation of what, for whom, and at whose cost. The opening states the letter and its date only; its model is assessed later (AW.3).

### AW.2 What is at stake, and who answers for it *(the protection map at the last record · ~600 w)*
- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows in one view which parts of Udaypur were protected and by whom at the last record, and which were not:
  - the temple, by the ASI;
  - four monuments by the state: two mosques, Pisanhārī's temple and the Naṭarāja at Rāvaṇaṭol;
  - the hill, as reserved forest under the Forest Department;
  - the palace, with no protection at all;
  - the fortification stretch, for which notification was proposed.

  The reader also knows the survey's own short list of what threatens each: mining blasts, an accelerating decay of the wall, new cracks in fresh repairs, monsoon drainage, grazing and graffiti.
- **Purpose:** Give the implications that emerge from the book as a whole in institutional form: Udaypur is one historic place divided among several authorities, and the gaps between them are where the damage is reported.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** From the protected centre outward to the unprotected edges. The pattern Chapter 11 traced historically is restated here as a present map. The risks are named only as the survey named them, without re-narration.

### AW.4 Who keeps Udaypur *(roles: residents, professionals, researchers, administrators, visitors · ~550 w)*

### AW.5 What the history argues *(advocacy, labelled · ~450 w)*
- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the book's own case:
  - Udaypur survived because it was used, worshipped in and lived in, not because it was set apart;
  - conservation that separates the temple from its town and hill would repeat the separation that left the hill, the wall and the palace exposed;
  - the realistic future is the one the record already points to: small measures, a closed protection gap, and residents treated as the town's keepers.
- **Purpose:** The interpretive movement the evidence justifies, and the book's close. The movement goes from Udaypur as a capital or a monument to Udaypur as a continuously inhabited town whose layers are all its history.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** From the long view to the single place. The section opens by declaring its register, makes the case in three steps, and ends on the image the book began with.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The register, and the lesson of the long view.** **Point:** Say in one plain sentence that what follows is the book's argument, not a finding. Then draw the interpretive movement from the chapters (callbacks by content, no summary):
     - a king's undertaking on a frontier hill (Chs 1, 4–6);
     - kept in worship while its corner shrine and hall were replaced and its town passed through other hands (1.3, Ch 10);
     - remembered by legends of how it survived (1.4);
     - protected, at last, as a temple apart from its town (Ch 11);
     - lived in by people who built on its ruins (Ch 12).

     The constant is use. Avoid "capital": the canon's "a place that was a capital" is not established (1.2 records Udayapura as Rājyapāla's eastern headquarters only; Devapāla resided there). Use "a royal town" or "a king's town".
     - E: (callbacks; no new items) · X: C13-JAGTA-HI-014 (Tiwari's question whether the temple is only carved stone for governments; quote at most a phrase, attributed)
  2. **P2 — The case.** **Point:** The book's advocacy, labelled ("this book argues"):
     - conserve the town and the hill as inhabited and working places;
     - close the gap that leaves the hill, wall and palace outside the temple's protection, as the survey proposed;
     - begin with the measures that cost residents nothing and tell visitors the town's history where it stands;
     - make any clearance a matter of residents' consent and benefit, as the one clearance on record was.

     Mishra's vision of a "heritage town" is endorsed only in that sense: a heritage *town*, still a town. His dream may be quoted once here ("a historian's last dream"), attributed to the tribute in Tiwari's book.
     - E: C13-JAGTA-HI-038 · C: C13-JAGTA-EN-022, C11-GEOHER-002, C13-JAGTA-EN-019 · X: C12-JAGTA-EN-010 (recall)
  3. **P3 — The close.** **Point:** Return to the village under the temple, the first image of the book (1.1), by content: the temple still in daily worship, the houses built on and out of the old town, the hill above with its bats and vultures and its broken wall. End on a concrete statement of what Udaypur is at the last record, not on an exhortation. Do not end on Tiwari's "resolve" (C13-JAGTA-HI-039) or on "waiting to wake"; those are the sources' cadences, and the book's last word is its own.
     - E: (recall only) · X: C12-JAGTA-HI-014 (the house), C01-PANDE-010 (worship continuing; 1.1's item, recall)

## 4. Chapter Conclusion
- **What has been demonstrated:**
  - The record of Udaypur ends c. 2021, in a burst of outside attention and a technical survey, with no evidence of what followed.
  - At that point the place was divided among several authorities, and the reported damage lay mostly where protection did not reach.
  - The proposals on record range from cheap and local to transformative. The realistic ones are those that cost residents nothing and close the protection gap.
  - Every party is asked to act; residents' consent (the lane) and documentation are the only forms of action the record actually shows.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - The current condition of the wall, the hill and the sculpture.
  - Whether any proposal was implemented.
  - What residents want.
  - The historical questions listed in AW.4 P3.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** The book ends on the question it cannot answer from its sources: whether Udaypur will be conserved with its residents or around them. The final paragraph states what Udaypur is, not what it should become.
- **How the last paragraph leads out (and what it must not do):** There is no next chapter. The last paragraph returns to the opening image of 1.1, the village under the temple, by content. It must not summarise the book, exhort the reader, quote a source's rallying cry, ask a question, or claim any present condition beyond the last record.
