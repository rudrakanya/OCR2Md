**Honest summary.** The Udaypur-specific evidence for society and culture is **one book of testimony** (Tiwari's *Jagta Hua Kasba*, tier 8), **a heritage survey's folklore notes** (GEOHER, tier 7 recording tier 8), and **the 1979 Gazetteer's district baseline** (tier 7). No census item gives Udaypur's own religious, caste or language composition; no oral-history interviews exist; no record names the town's quarters (the old canon's Shikari, Mahua, Chaudaryana, Sarenthi and Utranti have **no item in any map**). ## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** Who lives in Udaypur, what do they speak and celebrate, and who keeps which memories of the town?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** Udaypur's society can be described only at two removes: the district through the Gazetteer's numbers, the town through one writer's testimony. What the town does keep in abundance is narrative. Its stories divide by subject and by teller. Tales about the founding attach to a king (Udayāditya) and to worshipped objects (an image on a tank's bank, a temple, a hill). Tales about the later centuries remember the Garhi as a place of extraction, rebellion, deserted villages and buried treasure. The record supports almost none of their events, but the stories are evidence of what the town has chosen to explain. The chapter treats Tiwari's book as both the main source and a part of the subject: the town narrating itself.
- **What changes over the chapter:** It moves from people (a lane of neighbours; the district's communities) to speech (languages of talk and of record), to the shared calendar (festivals and the fair), to memory (who tells what about the past). Intellectually it moves from "who are the people" to "what do they say the past was", and ends on the gap between the remembered later centuries and the documented ones, which Ch 10 takes up.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–8:** Builds on 1.1 (the village, the shrine shared in the 1870s, Berni Mata), 1.4 (survival legends, Pisanhari), 1.5 (Bhringraj, Gond), Ch 7 (the Jain precinct; the Garhi and the hill as structures), Ch 8 (the time-keepers' family as the one hereditary service; the *panchakula* in Dhāmadeva's record; the Śivarātri as the temple's draw). **Must not repeat:** any legend's plot (one clause at most); the census 2011 figures; the fair as scene; the Pisanhari tradition (recall only).
- **What it must establish for Chapter 10:** that the town remembers its post-Paramāra centuries as decline, extraction and desertion (the Garhi's "threads", Daulatpur's vanished market) without dates or names it can check. Ch 10 tests that memory against the record of regimes, patronage and economy.

## 2. Chapter Arc

Opening on a lane outside the temple → 9.1 communities (today's neighbours, back to the Paramāra social order, forward through remade identities to the district baseline) → 9.2 languages (speech in the census, record in stone) → 9.3 the year (Śivarātri and the fair's place in the district; other rites; Paramāra festival culture as distant frame) → 9.4 the town that narrates itself (tellers; the founder's tales against the record; Bhoja and Udayāditya in folk memory; the Garhi's threads) → close on the deserted village of Daulatpur, which hands to Ch 10.

**Why this order:** people before what they say. Language sits between community and festival because it is the medium of both and its evidence is thinnest (a short bridge). Memory comes last because it is the chapter's strongest material and because its last subject (the remembered later centuries) leads into Ch 10. The alternative, the old canon's C14 + C15 order, would end on "memory against the record" as an abstract essay; this order ends on a concrete remembered place.

**Scale shifts:** 9.1 shifts from one lane to Paramāra Malwa to the 1961 district. 9.2 is district-scale throughout and says so. 9.3 moves from Udaypur's fair to the district's list of fairs and to the eleventh-century court, and returns. 9.4 shifts from Udaypur to Dhārā (Bhoja's folk fame) and back.

**Chronology:** 9.1 runs eleventh century → later centuries → 1941/1961 → today. 9.2 runs 1908 → 1961. 9.3 is calendrical, not chronological. 9.4 suspends chronology deliberately: memory has no dates, and the section says so once.

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur's people can be counted only at district scale and described only through one writer's testimony, but that the town keeps a dense body of stories which remember its founder through worshipped objects and its later centuries as loss, stories that are evidence of memory rather than of events.

**Opening move:** The lane on the left as one leaves the temple: in Tiwari's account, a Muslim household, then others, Hindu and Muslim, side by side, and Tiwari's claim that the ancestors of all of them together built the monuments a thousand years ago (C09-JAGTA-HI-005). It takes up Ch 8's last line (the time-keepers' family and its hereditary claim) by moving from one family's memory of its place to a whole lane's. **Do not list the residents' first names**; describe the households by community only (privacy; the names add nothing historical). Tiwari's claim is testimony about belonging, not a fact about the eleventh century.

### 9.2 Speech and the written word *(languages · ~450 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the district's people speak Hindi (with Urdu the main minority language in 1961), that the local speech has been returned in censuses as Bundeli in 1908 and as Malwi from 1921, Malwi then all but vanishing from the returns after 1951, that the town stands where Bundeli and Malwi meet on Patil's dialect map, and that Udaypur's own record was cut in Sanskrit and later in Persian and in Hindi in Nagari. The reader also knows that no item records how Udaypur itself speaks.
- **Purpose:** A short bridge that states the evidence and its limit; the epigraphic languages are Ch 6's subject.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Census names for the local speech changed faster than the speech can have; the town's stones record the languages of power and pilgrimage, not of daily talk.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — Speech in the census.** **Point:** In 1961 Hindi was the mother tongue of about 91 per cent of the district, Urdu of about 8 per cent, Marathi a small remainder, and thirty-three other speeches among under one per cent. The colloquial dialect was returned as Bundeli in 1908, as Malwi (most widely) in 1921 and still second in 1931 and 1941, and almost not at all in 1951 and 1961. Patil places Bundeli in the centre of northern Madhya Bharat and Malwi, a southern form of Rajasthani, as once the speech of almost the whole plateau. Udaypur, on the plateau's north-eastern edge, lies where the two meet (graded: "lies near the meeting of"; a reading of the map, not a record of its speech). The collapse of Malwi in the returns probably says more about how answers were classified than about what people spoke (interpretive; one clause).

### 9.3 The god's days and the town's year *(festivals and fairs · ~650 w)*

### 9.4 The town that narrates itself *(oral traditions and memory · ~950 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows who keeps Udaypur's memories (a family, the villagers whose folklore the survey recorded, the district's compilers, and one writer whose book is itself the town's fullest self-account), how the founder's stories attach to worshipped objects and how their versions differ, that one founding tale has twins elsewhere, that the Paramāra king of regional folk memory is Bhoja while Udaypur's tales name Udayāditya, and that the town remembers its later centuries through the Garhi as a place of extraction, rebellion, desertion and buried treasure.
- **Purpose:** Make memory the subject: not retelling legends but asking who tells them, what they attach to, and what they choose to explain.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Udaypur's traditions fall into two bodies: tales of the founder, tied to objects that are still worshipped, and tales of the later centuries, tied to the Garhi and to places that have vanished. The first body competes with the inscriptions; the second fills a gap the record leaves.

## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:** Udaypur is a village of several faiths whose composition is recorded only at district scale; its medieval society is known regionally and through one local institution in a record; its speech is recorded only as district census categories, its stones in the languages of power and pilgrimage; its calendar centres on the Śivarātri, whose fair ranks among the district's largest; and it keeps a dense, structured body of traditions, the fullest written collection of which is one writer's testimony.
- **What remains unresolved:** the town's own religious, caste and language composition; its quarters; whether any medieval office besides the *panchakula* existed at Udaypur; how long the dancer's story has been told; what lies behind the Garhi's threads (Daulatpur, the "Gyasuddin" name, the Bagdis); any recorded oral history beyond Tiwari.
- **The tension carried forward:** the town remembers its later centuries as extraction and desertion; the record of those centuries (regimes, patronage, revenue) is Ch 10's, and the two may not agree.
- **How the last paragraph leads into Chapter 10:** by content: it ends on the deserted village of Daulatpur where markets were once held. It must not announce Ch 10, summarise the chapter, or moralise about memory.
