## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** How did a frontier ridge on Malwa's eastern edge come to carry a king's name, and what can the record actually say about whether Udayāditya made Udaypur before he was king, as king, or by placing his name on a place others held?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** The Paramāras reached this frontier as a western house pushing east; Bhoja held it and made the dynasty's reputation, but the town takes its name from the man who recovered the kingdom after him. The chapter argues that the familiar story (Udayāditya founds town, temple and tank in 1059, eleven years before his accession) is a chain of inferences: a late, error-ridden record of V.S. 1116, a pedigree from a sixteenth-century inscription that the CII editor rejects, and Ganguly's reconciliation built on both. The firm ground is narrower and still substantial. By 1080 a temple at Udaypur bore his name. He reigned from c. 1070 to 1093 over a kingdom reaching from Jhālrapāṭan to the Vidisha country. Udayapura appears, in the editor's reading of a damaged slab, as the seat of a subordinate house under him. The town's making is best told as a royal naming and endowment on a contested border, whose physical urban form the evidence of this chapter does not date.
- **What changes over the chapter:**
  - Intellectually: from a dynasty told in its own panegyric to the same dynasty read against its rivals' records and its editors' doubts. From "founded in 1059" to "named, endowed and held by 1080; begun earlier on a reading that cannot yet bear weight".
  - Narratively: from a western house's arrival on a frontier where Chapter 3 left no named holder, through Bhoja's height and the collapse after him, to a kingdom recovered by a kinsman whose name the town keeps.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–3:**
  - *Builds on:* 3.5 (three rival houses; the Chandellas at Bhilsa in 954; "Who held the hill itself … is not known"); 1.2 (the polity, the crisis, Jayasiṃha's restoration, the c. 1070 accession with the note of reign dates, kinsman with the note of readings, the boar image, the realm to Jhalrapatan/Bhilsa/Nimar, the triple naming, V.S. 1116/1137 as readings, the "lord's seat or new king's undertaking" question, *maṇḍala*/*bhoga*/*pāṭhaka*, Bhāillasvāmi-mahādvādaśaka, Rājyapāla, the Nemak reading); 1.4 (the praśasti as object, its two slabs, agnikula, Ray's doubt on Bhoja's buildings, the three readings of 1080, the "1059—1080" caption); 1.5 (older place versus new town; Bhringraj and Gond traditions; the 1513 record).
  - *Must NOT repeat:* the 1.2 crisis narrative (Bhoja's death in war → Dhārā taken → Jayasiṃha restored by Vikramāditya VI → Karṇa's return fifteen years later), the 1.2 kinship note's list of readings, the boar quotation, the full 1.2 note of reign datings, the 1.4 description of the praśasti's find and slabs, the 1.4 agnikula account, the 1.5 Bhujariya Talab/two-line inscription/Bhringraj case. Each may be recalled in one clause, and then deepened: Chapter 4 adds *why* the sources disagree and *what rests on what*.
- **What it must establish for Chapter 5:** that the Udayeśvara is the one securely dated royal work at Udaypur (V.S. 1137 record), named after its constructor. Its start date is a reading, and whether the 1080 record marks consecration, a new staff or a repair is open (1.4). The question of who built it (king, or a subordinate house in his reign) stays open. Chapter 5 then enters the building without having to re-argue patronage or dates. Chapter 4 must also hand Chapter 6 the praśasti and the 1080 record as *texts* still to be read, Chapter 7 the Udayasamudra/Udaysagar question unresolved, and Chapter 10 the succession of 1093.

## 2. Chapter Arc

Opening on the frontier (4.1) → Bhoja's kingdom at its height, and how Udaypur's own stone carries his praise (4.2) → the broken succession and the recovery, and who Udayāditya was (4.3) → the dates: founding before kingship, and the dated reign 1080–1093 (4.4) → what Udayapura was in his kingdom, and what can and cannot be said of the town's making (4.5).

**Why this order:** Chronology runs straight through 4.1–4.3 because the reader needs the dynasty's arc to understand the recovery. Chronology is **suspended** in 4.4, which turns from narrative to evidence and tests one inference chain against the dated records. 4.5 changes scale from kingdom to town, so the chapter ends on Udaypur, the centre of gravity, and hands Chapter 5 its building. The alternatives were weaker. Opening on Udayāditya (the old C7 plan) would bury the arrival question Chapter 3 hands in. Putting the town before the dates would let "founded 1059" stand unexamined for a section. A separate Bhūmija section (old 6.4) would anticipate Chapter 5's architecture.

**Scale shifts:** region (4.1) → kingdom and its capital (4.2) → kingdom under attack, with Udaypur's stone as witness (4.3) → Udaypur's inscriptions against the kingdom's dated records (4.4) → the town and its province (4.5).

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that the Paramāras reached this frontier as Rāṣṭrakūṭa feudatories turned kings. They knew it as their eastern limit under Bhoja and nearly lost the kingdom after him. The town carries the name of the kinsman who recovered it, who held a temple there in his name by 1080. Whether he began the work before he was king is a question the late record of 1059 raises but cannot settle.

**Opening move:** Chapter 3 ends on the Chandellas' claim of 954 to Bhāsvat (Bhilsa) and on the unknown holder of the hill. Chapter 4 opens on the next claim made about the same country: the Gazetteer's statement that the Chandellas were ousted from Malwa by the Paramāra Sīyaka II, whose known date is 972, and that his kingdom ran east to Vidisha [C04-GAZ-001]. Ganguly makes the same eastern limit, Bhilsa [C04-GANGULY-035]. The first paragraph keeps the gap Chapter 3 named: these sources give the kingdom's eastern edge, not the holder of Udaypur's hill. The chapter's question follows: how a western house came to set its frontier here, and how, two generations after Bhoja, a member of it put his name on the ground.

## 3. Sections

### 4.1 From feudatories to kings of Malwa *(c. 800–1000; regional, then dynastic · ~700 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the Paramāras entered Malwa as subordinates of the Rāṣṭrakūṭas, made themselves independent under Sīyaka in the 970s, and by the end of the tenth century held a kingdom whose eastern limit was set at Bhilsa. The reader also knows that nothing in the record yet names Udaypur.
- **Purpose:** To answer Chapter 3's open line with the house that eventually took the frontier, briefly and on the strongest evidence, and to show the dynasty's own self-account (genealogy, fire-birth) as a later construction set against earlier grants.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** A western, Gujarat-based feudatory line moves into Malwa during the Pratīhāra–Rāṣṭrakūṭa contest and turns on its overlords. Sīyaka's title in 949 and the sack of Mānyakheṭa make it sovereign. Muñja and Sindhurāja extend and then strain it. The eastern edge, toward the country of Chapter 3, is reached early but held as a frontier.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The claim on the east.** **Point:** pick up 3.5's last line. Later writers credit Sīyaka II with ousting the Chandellas from Malwa and with a kingdom bounded on the east by Vidisha. Say plainly that this gives a frontier, not a holder of Udaypur's hill.
     - E: C04-GAZ-001, C04-GANGULY-035 · C: C04-PARTEMPLES-002 · X: (3.5 recalled in a clause: the Chandella claim to Bhāsvat, 954) · Note: C04-GAZ-001 (Gazetteer's statement, tier 7; date "972" is "known date")
  2. **P2 — Feudatories in Gujarat.** **Point:** the earliest records place the family not in Malwa but in Gujarat as Rāṣṭrakūṭa subordinates. The Harsola grants of 949 call Sīyaka "crest-jewel among the feudatories" while giving him a sovereign's title. The editor reconstructs a three-generation move from Gujarat into Malwa, capturing Ujjain probably from a Pratīhāra governor.

  3. **P3 — Independence by war.** **Point:** the title provoked a Rāṣṭrakūṭa expedition, and the Paramāras answered by sacking Mānyakheṭa in 972–73, a date fixed independently by Dhanapāla's colophon. The first charter issued from Malwa itself follows in 975.

- **Evidence sequence:** frontier claim first (because it answers 3.5), then back to the grants (where the family actually starts), then independence, then the two kings. The genealogy is placed last so that the dynasty's self-account is read *after* the evidence that contradicts its tidiness.
- **Analytical payoff:** The house that would name Udaypur was not of this country. It came from the west and treated the Vidisha country as its eastern limit from the start. The town's later importance is therefore a frontier's importance.
- **Drafting guidance:**
  - Compress. This is the most "Central India" section of the chapter; Udaypur must be named in P1 and P5 and nowhere invented elsewhere.
  - Introduce Sīyaka (II), Vākpati-Muñja, Sindhurāja, Rāṣṭrakūṭas of Mānyakheṭa, Harsola once each. Sīyaka I is already in the ledger (1.4); call this ruler "Sīyaka II" at first mention.
  - Rāṣṭrakūṭa origin of the family: Ganguly's argument, in a note only.
  - The Gazetteer's "ousted the Chandellas" is a claim of a tier-7 source. Phrase it as "The district Gazetteer credits Sīyaka II with…", never as narration.
  - Do not narrate Muñja's capture as a scene. Do not use the Merutuṅga grass-baby story (GANGULY-038) or PARMAR-RAJPUT's "16 times" (PARMAR-RAJPUT-006). Both are tradition, and neither touches Udaypur.
- **Classification:** Feudatory status and the 949 title: documented. The 972–73 sack: documented. The migration route: interpretive (CII editor). The Chandella ouster: interpretive (Gazetteer). Who held Udaypur: **gap**, kept open.
- **Transition:** Within a generation the eastern limit set under Sīyaka belonged to a king whose reputation would outrun his kingdom. The first royal grant in the Bhopal country shows Bhoja holding it by 1018, which is where 4.2 begins.



### 4.2 Bhoja's kingdom and Bhoja's name *(c. 1000–1055; dynastic, with the eastern frontier in view · ~800 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows Bhoja's dated reign (1011–1047 in his own records, dead before 1055). They know that his kingdom reached the Bhopal country by 1018 and had Bhilsa as its eastern limit c. 1030. They know he rebuilt Dhārā and patronised building and learning on a scale his successors measured themselves against, though defeats marked his later years. They also know that much of his praise reaches us through the praśasti found at Udaypur, which is panegyric, and that the books credited to him, including the architectural treatise, are attributed and partly doubted.
- **Purpose:** To give Bhoja his due in a book about Udaypur without making him its founder. He matters here for three things: the frontier he held, the model of royal building he set, and the way the town's memory and popular writing attach him to Udaypur.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Bhoja's kingdom at its widest, then its limits (reverses in the north and south; Dhārā burned). Then the praise, read as praise. Then the books, read as attributions. Then the memory that ties him to a town founded, on any reckoning, after his death.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The reign in its own records.** **Point:** Bhoja's grants run from 1011 to 1047; he died some time before 1055, when his successor's first grant appears. One sentence on the longer traditional reign (55 years, 7 months, 3 days) as tradition, with the range in a note.

  2. **P2 — The eastern edge under Bhoja.** **Point:** this is the only paragraph of Bhoja's politics the town strictly needs. The first Paramāra grant issued in the Bhopal territory (1018) puts that region inside his kingdom. Al-Bīrūnī, as Ganguly reads him, gives Bhilsa as the eastern limit in 1030. Udaypur lay at or just beyond that edge; write "on the eastern edge", never "inside", on this evidence.
     - E: C04-CII-049, C04-GANGULY-051 · C: C04-GANGULY-022 · Note: C04-GANGULY-051 (Al-Bīrūnī via Ganguly)
  3. **P3 — The builder and his reverses.** **Point:** Bhoja rebuilt Dhārā and moved the royal residence there. He had the Vāgdevī image made in 1034. He began, and did not finish, a colossal temple at Bhojpur tied to a dam (Chapter 2's comparison recalled in a clause only). His later reign also saw defeats: Candella and Kacchapaghāta reverses in the north, and Someśvara I sacking Dhārā. This prepares the crisis without narrating it again.
     - E: C04-GANGULY-052, C04-CII-063, C04-HARDY-001 · C: C04-GANGULY-055, C04-GANGULY-056, C04-GANGULY-068, C04-GANGULY-069 · X: Ch 2 §2.4 (Bhojpur dam), recall only
  4. **P4 — The praise from Udaypur.** **Point:** the fullest eulogy of Bhoja, his victories over Chedi, Gurjara, Lāṭa, Karṇāṭa and Turuṣka, and his temples "all around" to Kedāra, Rāmeśvara and Somanātha, is in the praśasti found at Udaypur. The town's own stone is thus a main witness to Bhoja's reputation, and a partisan one.

### 4.3 The broken succession and the recovery *(c. 1055–c. 1075; dynastic, read against rival records · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things.
  - The dynasty's own praśastis leave Jayasiṃha out. He is known from his own grant of 1055 and from others' records, so the line from Bhoja to Udayāditya was not a smooth succession.
  - One record of V.S. 1116 suggests a struggle for the throne in which Udayāditya may have been Jayasiṃha's adversary; this is the editor's presumption.
  - The second invasion was a coalition of three enemies, whose identities the CII editor and Ganguly reconstruct differently. Ganguly's Cāhamāna cavalry is his reading, not a recorded fact.
  - Udayāditya's relation to Bhoja remains "kinsman, exact relationship disputed". The "junior branch" view rests on a sixteenth-century pedigree that the editor rejects.

### 4.4 Before the throne? The dates and what rests on them *(1059 / c. 1070 / 1080–1093; evidential, chronology suspended · ~850 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things.
  - "Founding before kingship" depends on the year V.S. 1116 (1059). That year reaches the literature through late or error-ridden records at Udaypur, and through survey readings of temple inscriptions not yet edited in the corpus.
  - Ganguly's reconciliation (a territorial lord building in 1059 from Udayapura) rests on the same rejected pedigree as the junior branch.
  - In the CII record, the earliest known date for Udayāditya is the Udaypur temple record of 1080.
  - His reign is dated in his own and his sons' records from 1080 to the latter part of 1093. The accession c. 1070 is the working position, and the 1059 founding remains possible but unproven.

### 4.5 A town with a king's name *(Udayāditya's reign and after; scale shifts to Udaypur · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things.
  - The contemporary record ties Udayāditya to Udaypur through a temple named after its constructor. The eponymous town–temple–tank triad reaches us through an inscription reported by Patil and Ganguly.
  - The CII editor reads Udayapura as the capital town of a subordinate Nēmaka house under Udayāditya, with a temple built by or under one of its princes. This leaves open whether the king or his subordinate built.
  - The administrative geography often attached to Udayapura (a *maṇḍala*, a sixty-four-village *pāṭhaka*, Rājyapāla's eastern headquarters) comes from a record of the 1170s, not from his reign.
  - Nothing in this chapter's evidence dates the walls, streets or palace. Udaypur's "urban formation" in his reign is a naming, an endowment and a seat of local rule, while the physical town is Chapter 7's to date.
- **Purpose:** To bring the chapter home to Udaypur and to answer honestly the "urban formation" in the chapter title: say what is known, mark what is inferred, and refuse what is invented.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:**
  - From name to holder: who held the place under the king.
  - From holder to province, and a correction of dates.
  - From province to the town as later writers imagine it.
  - From the town to the king's death and the one thing of his the stones of Udaypur certainly keep.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — What the name proves.** **Point:**

## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:**
  - The Paramāras came to this frontier from the west, as Rāṣṭrakūṭa feudatories turned kings, and set their eastern limit at Bhilsa by Bhoja's time.
  - The succession after Bhoja was contested and is edited out of the dynasty's own praśastis.
  - A coalition was repulsed by Udayāditya c. 1070, and he reigned to 1093.
  - The one contemporary record of him at Udaypur (1080) gives him a temple bearing his name.
  - Udayapura was, on the editor's reading, the seat of a subordinate house under him.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - Whether anything was built at Udaypur before he was king (1059).
  - His exact relation to Bhoja.
  - Who the three enemies were, and whether a Cāhamāna force helped.
  - Whether the king or the Nēmaka prince built the temple the damaged slab mentions.
  - Whether Udaypur was founded or renamed (1.5's question, now sharpened but not closed).
  - When the walled town took shape.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** A king's name and a king's temple are certain; a king's town is not yet shown. The building must now be read for what it can say about its own making.
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content (and what it must not do):** The last paragraph ends on the temple of Udayeśvara as the single work at Udaypur that a contemporary record assigns to the reign. It must not summarise the chapter, announce "the next chapter", describe the temple's architecture or style (Chapter 5), quote the 1080 record as a text (Chapter 6), or mention the tanks and walls in detail (Chapter 7).
