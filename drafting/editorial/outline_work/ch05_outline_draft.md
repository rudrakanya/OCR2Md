**Books that carry the chapter**
- **Pande** (PANDE, tier 5): the primary authority on the temple; 124 direct items.
- **Kramrisch** (KRAM-1, KRAM-2, tier 5): the plan, tower and wall plates of 1946; the "country-born" reading. Her general symbolism is context only.
- **Hardy** (HARDY, tier 5): the 32-point star, seven *bhūmi*s, "no surviving precedents", the Bhūmija workshops.
- **Krishna Deva, *Temples of North India*** (TEMPLES-IND, tier 5) and **Sharma** (ARTPAR, tier 5; Hindi OCR, damaged) and **Adhikari** (PARTEMPLES, tier 5): the Malwa series and dating by comparison.
- **Patil / Gazetteer / Ganguly** (MADHYABHARAT, GAZ tier 7, GANGULY tier 5): the court and shrine counts, much of it Cunningham and Beglar at second hand (tier 6 inside a tier 5–7 source).

**What is thin**
- No survey measurement of the temple itself is in the map (overall length, height of the spire, platform height): the GEOHER/INTACH items concern the hill, not the temple. Plan dimensions survive only as proportions (KRAM-1-032) and the hall pillars (KRAM-2-045; ARTPAR-014).
- No item describes the *garbhagṛha* interior beyond the *liṅga* "on a high pedestal" (MADHYABHARAT-005) and its later sheath (KRAM-2-012).
- Construction method (dry masonry, clamps) appears only as general statements (ARTPAR-001; TEMPLES-IND-002; KRAM-1-008), none observed at Udaypur.
- Interpretation of the programme rests on one scholar (Pande). There is no second reading of the image programme in the map, only Kramrisch's general theory and Hardy's report of Granoff's critique (HARDY-032).

## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** What does the building itself (its plan, its star-shaped tower, its carved surfaces and the order of its images) show about the foundation that raised it, and how much of the meaning scholars have found in it can the stone carry?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** Udayeśvara is the fullest surviving statement of a style that appears with no surviving precursor (Hardy), so the building is the best evidence for what Udayāditya's foundation could command: a mature workshop, a single coherent design, and an image programme ordered by rule and symmetry. The order of the images is documented (Pande's catalogue). The Śaiva Siddhānta meaning given to that order is one scholar's reading. The chapter keeps the two apart, and lets the second rest on the first.
- **What changes over the chapter:** The reader moves from the court as it stands now (entered from the west, with gaps where shrines were) to the geometry that generates the whole, then to the craft of its surfaces, then to the gods placed on them, and only last to what those gods have been made to mean. Intellectually, "the most beautiful temple" of Chapter 1's assessments becomes a set of particular, checkable features: a rotated square, 32 points, seven tiers of five, eight Mothers round three spines, guardians matched by mount. The superlatives give way to evidence of design and labour.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–4:**
  - Builds on Ch 1 §1.3 (Adhikari's judgement of Udayeśvara as the fullest Bhūmija statement; the mosque replacing a corner shrine and the western hall; the brass sheath of V.S. 1841), Ch 1 §1.4 (V.S. 1116 and 1137 as readings; the 1080 record read three ways) and Ch 2 §2.3 (red sandstone "quarried in the hill close by").
  - Takes from Ch 4 the founding act and patronage context, and does not re-argue them.
  - **Must NOT repeat:** the mosque's history, inscriptions and dates (Ch 1 §1.3; Ch 7/Ch 10); Adhikari's and Hardy's rank judgements as such (Ch 1 §1.3 note), which may only be recalled in a clause; the survival legends (Ch 1 §1.4); the reading of the dates (Ch 1 §1.4; Ch 4); the brass cover and its donor (Ch 1 §1.3; Ch 10).
- **What it must establish for Chapter 6:** The temple carries words as well as images: a record at the east doorway naming the god Udayeśvara, Sanskrit pilgrims' lines on the porch pillars and seats, a lettered slab in the parapet wall. Chapter 5 points to where they are on the fabric and leaves their reading to Chapter 6. It also establishes the vocabulary Chapter 6 and Chapter 8 will assume: *garbhagṛha*, *maṇḍapa*, *śukanāsa*, *latā*, *kūṭastambha*, *khattaka*, *dikpāla*, *aṣṭamātṛkā*, Bhūmija.

## 2. Chapter Arc

Opening on the court as it now stands → (5.1) the ensemble as designed: platform, enclosure, attendant shrines, the separate *vedī*, the hall with three porches, and what has gone → (5.2) the generating geometry and the tower: rotated square, 32-point star, seven *bhūmi*s, and where this stands in the Malwa series → (5.3) the surfaces: mouldings, wall niches, doorways, pillars and ceilings as evidence of craft → (5.4) the image programme described from the building: what stands where, by quarter and by buttress → (5.5) what the programme has been made to mean, Pande's Śaiva Siddhānta reading set beside its limits; close on the flag-bearer figure and the temple's own written name, handing to Chapter 6.

**Why this order:**
- Plan before tower, because the star plan is what the tower continues: Kramrisch and Pande both describe the wall-buttresses rising into the *kūṭastambha* chains. The tower cannot be explained before the plan.
- Comparison sits in 5.2, not at the end, so that the Malwa series (Un, Nemawar, Amareśvara) explains the form while it is in view. Ending on comparison would turn the chapter into a ranking, and Ch 1 has already ranked it.
- Description of images (5.4) is a separate section from interpretation (5.5). This is the user's requirement in chapters.md, and it lets the drafting model write 5.4 without any symbolic vocabulary.
- **Alternative rejected:** the old canon's two-chapter split (C8 structure; C9 iconography) with the hill Naṭarāja inside the iconography chapter. Merging forces a single argument, and the hill colossus moves to Ch 7 (see decision in 5.4 and the cross-chapter notes).

**Scale:**
- 5.1 is at the scale of the court.
- 5.2 narrows to the sanctum's geometry, then widens briefly to Malwa (Un, Nemawar, Omkareshwar) and to the Deccan (Sinnar, Ambarnath).
- 5.3 and 5.4 work at the scale of a single buttress and niche.
- 5.5 widens to the Paramāra court's Śaivism, held to what Ch 8 will not already do.

**Chronology** is suspended almost throughout. The chapter is synchronic: the building as one design. It runs only in two places: the clause recalling V.S. 1116–1137 (5.1), and the relative sequence of Malwa temples (5.2, "contemporary", "a decade later", "half a century later").

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udayeśvara, as it stands, is a complete and precisely ordered Bhūmija temple with no surviving precursor, whose geometry, craft and image programme are the best evidence for what Udayāditya's foundation commanded, and that the Śaiva meanings read into it are scholars' readings laid over an arrangement that is itself documented.

**Opening move:** Chapter 4 hands over to the building. Open on a plain, checkable fact that carries the chapter's question: the temple faces east, and its main approach was a stair from the east flanked by over-life-size Śaiva door-guardians, but today it is entered from the west (C05-PANDE-002; C05-PANDE-011). The building has to be read against the way it is now met. The first paragraph re-orients the reader to the design's own axis, east to west: stair, porch, hall, vestibule, sanctum. It does not narrate an arrival.

## 3. Sections

### 5.1 The court and the building it holds *(plan, platform, enclosure, attendant shrines, the vedī; what is missing · ~800 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the temple as a designed ensemble and as it survives:
  - a red-sandstone shrine facing east on a high platform: sanctum, vestibule, a covered hall with three porches;
  - a separate, low, flat-roofed *vedī* on the same axis;
  - a square court with a low carved wall lined inside with seats, and stepped, guarded entrances;
  - eight attendant shrines, of which six stand in ruin;
  - and the place at the back where one of the court's structures has been replaced.
- **Purpose:** Give the reader the whole before its parts. Fix the canon's count (eight attendant shrines, Pande) and account for the missing fabric without re-telling the mosque.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The ensemble was a single, axial and symmetrical design. Its plan is documented by observers from the 1870s onward and by Pande, and its losses can be counted. From the court the eye moves inward along the axis, and the section ends at the foot of the sanctum, where the geometry of 5.2 begins.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The design's own axis.** **Point:** Re-orient the reader from today's western entrance to the temple's eastern axis. The temple faces east. It consists of *garbhagṛha*, *antarāla*, a *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* with three *mukhamaṇḍapa*s (east, north, south) and a separate low *vedī* on the same axis, all on an extensive, lofty *jagatī*. Its original approach was a stair flanked by over-life-size Śaiva door-guardians. Material: red sandstone, recalled in a clause from Ch 2 §2.3, not re-quoted. The sanctum has no internal ambulatory (*nirandhāra*), the rule for temples of its class.

### 5.2 A star of seven tiers: the Bhūmija rule and where Udayeśvara stands *(geometry, tower, comparison · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows:
  - how the sanctum's plan is generated: a square rotated about its centre within a circle, with the four cardinal projections laid out square to the sanctum;
  - that this gives a 32-point star (seven projections a side, *saptaratha*);
  - that the wall-buttresses continue upward as chains of miniature spires (*kūṭastambha*), seven tiers of five in each quarter, between four flat bands (*latā*) of arch-lattice;
  - that a great antefix (*śukanāsa*) seals the east face and smaller ones the other three;
  - that the style appears in Malwa with no surviving precursor, so Udayeśvara is already its fullest version;
  - and how its neighbours compare: Un contemporary and simpler; the Amareśvara and Jamli later; Nemawar loftier but judged less well proportioned.
- **Purpose:** Turn "Bhūmija" from a label (Ch 1) into a rule the reader can see, and use the series of Malwa temples to say what the building shows about the workshop and patron behind it.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The design is generated, not accumulated: one rule governs plan and tower together. Because no earlier stage survives, the temple's maturity implies a practised specialist workshop whose earlier work is lost. This is an inference; mark it as such (Hardy: "guilds who were Bhūmija specialists").
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — The rotating square.** **Point:**
     - The plan is not divided as a grid. The square is turned about its centre and stopped at regular intervals; its points describe a circle within which the intermediate buttresses are set.
     - The four main projections face the cardinal points and stay parallel to the sanctum's square.
     - The result is stellate-cum-circular, *saptaratha*: Hardy's "32-point star (equivalent to seven orthogonal projections)". This is the canon's figure.
     - Kramrisch's plan caption may be cited for the plan, but **not** its "1059–1080" span as fact (Ch 1 §1.4).
     - E: C05-PANDE-014, C05-PANDE-002, C05-HARDY-005 · C: C05-KRAM-2-007, C05-KRAM-2-019, C05-KRAM-1-033, C05-BHOJA-TIMES-012 · X: C05-PARTEMPLES-013 (the treatise's *parivartanā* rule, normative) · Note: C05-KRAM-1-034
  2. **P2 — Wall becomes tower.** **Point:**
     - The buttresses read as pillars in the wall and continue in the same vertical lines as *kūṭastambha*s in the spire, so that the building appears to rise from the ground. This is one of Pande's readings of "Bhūmija"; give the others in one sentence: "earth-born", "country-born", "born of storeys".
     - Between the four *latā*s, each laced with *gavākṣa* lattice, are seven *bhūmi*s of five *kūṭastambha*s in every quarter. The shoulder repeats the star, and the *āmalaka*'s cogged rim answers its points.
     - The sources word the counts differently (Pande: "five rows vertically and seven horizontally"; Ganguly: thirty-five in five rows; Krishna Deva: seven vertical and five horizontal rows). All give 35 per quarter. State "seven tiers of five" and put the wording differences in a note.
     - E: C05-PANDE-015, C05-PANDE-017, C05-PANDE-018, C05-PANDE-020, C05-KRAM-2-020, C05-KRAM-2-021, C05-KRAM-2-022 · C: C05-PANDE-012, C05-PANDE-016, C05-KRAM-2-019, C05-HARDY-012, C05-BHOJA-TIMES-015 · X: C05-KRAM-2-108 (the same correspondence of wall and spire on non-stellate temples) · Note: C05-GANGULY-009, C05-TEMPLES-IND-006
  3. **P3 — The antefixes.** **Point:**
     - The east face is sealed by a great *śukanāsa*: a blind arch over the vestibule, its lower part a pillared miniature shrine between dancing goddesses, its upper medallion a dancing Śiva. Name it here; describe its figures in 5.4.
     - On the other three faces smaller *śūrasenaka*s sit at the foot of the *latā*s, closer to the spire's curve.
     - A five-storeyed "celestial mansion" encases each side of the vestibule's superstructure.
     - E: C05-PANDE-003, C05-PANDE-028, C05-PANDE-048, C05-KRAM-2-009, C05-KRAM-2-084 · C: C05-KRAM-2-011, C05-PANDE-045, C05-ARTPAR-007 · X: C05-HARDY-015 · Note: —
  4. **P4 — No precedent, and what follows.** **Point:**
     - Hardy finds no surviving precedent: Udayeśvara is "already the deluxe model". The Bhūmija springs up ready-made, in styles new to the region, worked by specialist guilds, and it was the Paramāras' favoured mode.
     - Where it came from is disputed: born in Malwa (Krishna Deva, from the earliest nucleus at Onkar Mandhata); Deccani in design though Khajuraho-like in mouldings (Sarasvati, Mahesh Singh); possibly the lower Deccan, though Malwa's are the finest (Hardy).
     - The text keeps the question and states the inference with graded confidence: the maturity of the work implies a practised workshop whose earlier buildings do not survive.
     - The treatise attributed to Bhoja lists Bhūmija types (sixteen) and is often taken to show the court's interest. Say it is normative, and that its attribution to Bhoja is doubted. Never say the temple follows it.

  5. **P5 — Neighbours and successors.** **Point:**
     - **Un:** contemporary (an inscription of Udayāditya in the Chaubārā Ḍerā), with the same plan and spire design, simpler, and plain walls except the cardinal niches.
     - **The Amareśvara at Onkar Mandhata:** later, comparable in plan and form, dated 1063 by inscription (era flag; see Verification).
     - **Jamli:** about a decade later, the only non-stellate Bhūmija temple in Malwa.

### 5.3 Surfaces: mouldings, doorways, pillars and ceilings *(craft as evidence · ~600 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the temple's surfaces follow an ordered sequence from base to cornice and from door-frame to ceiling, executed with a precision every observer remarks on, and what that order shows about the workshop: a programme fixed in advance, in which plain surfaces were set against carved ones by design.
- **Purpose:** Give the craft its evidence without an inventory. Choose the four places where the stone shows the most about method: the base, the wall-niches, the doorways and the hall pillars.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The ornament is systematic: five base mouldings, two registers of niches on every buttress, door-frames of many bands, and pillars that move from square to octagon to circle. Its quality is attested from Beglar to Pande. The section moves from outside to inside, ending under the hall ceiling.

### 5.4 Who stands where: the image programme described *(description only, no symbolism · ~950 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the programme as it is placed on the building:
  - dancing Śiva at the head of every face: the great *śukanāsa* on the east and the three *śūrasenaka*s;
  - on the east antefix, a rare dancing Sarasvatī and a composite image of Sūrya, Viṣṇu, Śiva and Brahmā;
  - the eight guardians of the directions, always on the central or second buttress, paired by their mounts;
  - on the sanctum, eight Mothers (*aṣṭamātṛkā*) gathered round Vīrabhadra on the south and west and round Cāmuṇḍā on the north;

### 5.5 What the programme has been made to mean *(interpretation, attributed and bounded · ~750 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows:
  - the principal reading of the programme, Pande's: the temple as an expression of Śaiva Siddhānta, the east antefix as the five "pure" principles and Śakti placed on the left because the feminine is "left";
  - the narrower readings attached to single images (Mṛtyuñjaya and "a temple built for victory"; the deliberate Śaiva Mothers; the flag-bearer as victor, architect or king);
  - the general theories it draws on (Kramrisch's temple as manifestation) and the critique of them (Granoff, through Hardy);
  - what of all this the stone itself supports.
- **Purpose:** Give interpretation its own space, attributed throughout. Decide how much theology Ch 5 needs: only enough to state Pande's reading intelligibly (Śiva, Śakti and the "pure path" in one sentence each). Doctrine, ritual and Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa* are Ch 8's.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The regularities of 5.4 fit a Śaiva Siddhānta reading, and Pande has matched many images to Āgamic and *śilpa* texts. But the reading belongs to one scholar, its key steps are inferences, and the general theory behind it is contested. The documented core is narrower: an overwhelmingly Śaiva programme, Śakti weighted to one side, and images that match prescriptions in texts of the period. The section ends on the one figure whose meaning every reader has argued over, and on the temple's own inscribed name.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — Pande's reading.** **Point:**
     - Pande holds that the temple rests on Śaiva Siddhānta, the Paramāras' Śaivism (Bhoja's authorship of a treatise on it is recalled in a clause and left to Ch 8), and that she decoded the images from the Āgamas and *śilpa* texts of the period.
     - The east antefix shows the five components of the "pure path" (*śuddha adhvā*): Śiva and Śakti in the dance, Īśvara as the dancing creator, Sadvidyā as Sarasvatī, and the composite image as an aid to meditation on Sadāśiva.
     - Śakti dominates the left (north) because the feminine is *vāmā*.

  2. **P2 — Readings of single images.** **Point:** Several readings attach to single images, each a different kind of claim. Each must be marked as Pande's inference:

  3. **P3 — The frame behind the reading, and its critics.** **Point:**
     - Kramrisch's influential view treats the temple as the manifest body of the god: images projected outward from the sanctum, the devotee's circling as comprehension.

## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:**
  - Udayeśvara survives as one design: an axial ensemble of temple, *vedī*, court and eight attendant shrines (six in ruin, two gone).
  - Its sanctum is generated by a single rule, the rotated square, giving a 32-point star of seven tiers.
  - Its surfaces follow a fixed programme of uniform quality.
  - Its images are placed by direction, mount and symmetry, overwhelmingly Śaiva, with Śakti weighted to the north and a dancing Śiva at the head of every face.
  - It stands at the full development of a style with no surviving precursor, ahead of Un and of later Malwa temples.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - where the Bhūmija came from, and whose workshop built Udayeśvara;
  - what the *vedī* was for;
  - the exact original count and placement of the attendant structures;
  - whether the programme was designed to a Śaiva Siddhānta scheme or only fits one;
  - who or what the climbing figure on the spire represents.
- **The tension or question carried forward:** The building names its god, Udayeśvara, and carries a record of a flag-hoisting, but its designers left no statement of intent in the stone. What the inscriptions say, and what they do not, is the next chapter's subject.
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter:** The last paragraph moves from the disputed flag-bearer to the written record of the flag-hoisting and the god's name at the east doorway. Then, in one sentence, it notes that the temple's pillars, seats and parapet carry other writing, from pilgrims' lines to a lettered slab. It must **not**:
  - read or translate any inscription;
  - restate the date debate;
  - mention the mosque's inscriptions;
  - end on a superlative or a moral.
