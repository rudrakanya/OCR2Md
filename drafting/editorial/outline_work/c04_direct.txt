That is, he must have learned and understood the ways-and-manners of statecraft with Raja Bhoj himself; and when Raja Bhoj oversaw his empire from the capital Dhar, Udayaditya must have been sent here, made the feudatory.

From him [came] a famous son, endowed with all virtues — a god-devotee named Gandal (Gondal), a giver (*dana*), an enjoyer (*bhokta*), discerning, and conqueror of all enemies, who became renowned by his fame."
In the third shloka is — "His son Pata (Jnata), by churning the enemy armies (*arivala-mathan*), obtained the kingdom, and won fame in keeping with the ancestors of the lineage. Upon the earthly sphere, going to Madhyadesh Malava, he attained renown; of a movement conforming to [his] branch, a god-worshipper, having performed religious austerity (*dharma-tapasya*), he built temples furnished with water-reservoirs."
The fifth shloka says — "A great giver of gold and grain to the Brahmins, having deemed Udayaditya worthy, he made [him] king, and amassed fame."

But in the writings of Kakanadabot (Sanchi) and Udayagiri, respectively, Amrakardava *[the source reads "आत्रकादवर्द" — garbled]* and Virasena *["शाब" is unclear in source]* — who were the emperor's trusted commanders — were called the *tat-padanudhyata* of Chandragupta II Vikramaditya. From this it was concluded that the literal meaning, "meditating upon the feet," is more apt. This is a eulogy of praise, which has nothing to do with succession. Therefore, here, the claim — from the Shorgadh inscription — that Udayaditya received the succession directly from Bhoj is weakened.
C04-JAGTA-EN-027   ≈ p. 132 (Hindi original)
He says that the mention of this 'Pata' or 'Jnata' as the father of Udayaditya is a point that makes new gestures. One meaning of this is that Udayaditya was not coming down from the lineage-tradition of Sindhuraj, but was related to a junior branch (*avara-shakha*) of the Paramara dynasty. On this basis it may be supposed that Udayaditya possibly ruled in the eastern part of Malwa as a feudatory-lord (*samantadhipati*) under Maharaja Bhoj. Within this region lay Vidisha too. It must have been then that Udaipur began to develop as an important centre under it.
C04-JAGTA-EN-028   ≈ p. 132 (Hindi original)
One more such link joins in, from the Shorgadh inscription of Udayaditya. In its fifth and sixth lines it is said that: "Parama-bhattaraka Maharajadhiraja Parameshvara Shri Bhojdev's *padanudhyata* [one who meditates at his feet], Parama-bhattaraka Maharajadhiraja Parameshvara Shri Udayadityadeva, [is] well (*kushali*)."

It may also be that when the combined armies of the Chalukyas of Kalyani and the Karnatas attacked the capital Dhara and, around 1070 CE, killed in battle Bhojdev's son and successor Jaisingh I — then, in such a situation, in the political emptiness suddenly created, Udayaditya may have come to the throne.

In this, Udayaditya is described as the "*bandhu*" (kinsman) of Bhojdev. This is a word worthy of note.

The Nagpur inscription is that of King Narvarman, son of King Udayaditya, who, after Udayaditya, became the next king of the Paramara dynasty in 1094 and ruled until 1133 CE. In it is the complete information of the Paramara dynasty up to Narvarman — who came to the throne after whom. In the 56th shloka it is clear that this prashasti was composed by King Narvarman himself.

> This means — "He whose father was Udayaditya, and whose *pitrivya* — that is, paternal uncle — was Dev Bhojraj: these two, flourishing, soon by their own splendour attained the sovereignty of the earth and became established (like the sun and the moon, the *pushpavantau*)."

In the Jainad writing, Jagdev wrote King Udayadity as his father and King Bhojdev as [his] uncle (*pitrivya*), whereas in the Dongargaon inscription Jagdev has written his father Udayadity as Bhojdev's 'brother' (*bhrata*).
C04-JAGTA-EN-035   ≈ p. 134 (Hindi original)
Jagdev was the third and youngest prince of his father Udayadity (as his inscriptions tell us). Older than him were two brothers, Lakshmadeva and Narvarman.
Prince Lakshmadeva did not survive even his father's lifetime. Prince Narvarman became the Paramaras' next ruler after [his father] Udayadity's death.
C04-JAGTA-EN-036   ≈ p. 134 (Hindi original)
Now the last knot that remains is this: if King Bhoj and King Udayadity were both "*sahodar*" (uterine brothers, born of the same mother), then Udayadity's birth must have occurred before the death of their father King Sindhuraj, or within just a few months of that death. The exact date of King Sindhuraj's death is not known, but his son King Bhojdev appears as a king in Dhar in 1011 CE. If they were uterine brothers, then Udayadity ought, in the ordinary course, to have become king after King Bhojdev's death in 1055 CE. Why did he have to wait until 1070 CE?
C04-JAGTA-EN-037   ≈ p. 135 (Hindi original)

Ganguly, who brings a detail from the *Ain-i-Akbari* in favour of this same conclusion. Ganguly's opinion was opposed by H.C. Ray in his book *Dynastic History of Northern India*. He does not consider *bandhu* to mean brother.

C04-JAGTA-EN-041   ≈ p. 12 (Hindi original)   also Ch 1
It was a small place situated five hundred kilometres from Dhar, the capital of the Paramaras, on their border — which certainly had strategic importance in the sense that it touched the territory of the Kalachuri dynasty. Udaipur became an important centre situated on the frontier of the two dynasties because there were sandstone quarries here. Being under the Paramaras, Udaipur had seen itself developing like some mini-capital. Its importance lay in this — that a future emperor of the Paramara dynasty had, in his youth, lived in Udaipur. That crown-prince was Udayaditya, whose coronation the city of Dhara once witnessed — that same city of Dhara which was King Bhoj's beloved city; today's Dhar, the magnificent capital of the Paramaras.
C04-JAGTA-EN-042   ≈ p. 17 (Hindi original)   also Ch 2
This place's fame is from the Neelkantheshwar temple, which was built by Maharaj Udayaditya, who came in the generation after King Bhoj. The name of this town, Udaipur, too is connected with him.
C04-JAGTA-EN-043   ≈ p. 18 (Hindi original)   also Ch 5, Ch 1
It is very possible that Crown-Prince Udayaditya was sent, in his father's time, to Udaipur situated on the border of the Paramaras' kingdom. It may be that Udayaditya himself made the first major plan for the development of this frontier town and, according to the tradition of his lineage, began the finest constructions, which must have continued even after he became king. Otherwise, such temples are there village by village, but the layout of a palace encircled by a vast boundary wall and bastions, within a town protected by a square fort, is only there in this region. It must certainly have been a settlement inhabited before Udayaditya, where the Paramaras' military detachment must have been posted for the protection of the borders. Udayaditya's appointment had awakened the fortune of this settlement, where now something was going to happen that would remain alive in memory even after centuries.
C04-JAGTA-EN-044   ≈ p. 19 (Hindi original)   also Ch 1
In the Paramaras' time this was the Sabha Mandal — the city's important Brahmasthan. It was a vast temple precinct, which was also a Sanskrit school — exactly on the lines of the great Saraswati-kanthabharanas of Ujjain, Dhar and Mandu. But the construction of the Sabha Mandal took place after Bhoj, and it was Udayaditya himself who had it done.
C04-JAGTA-EN-045   ≈ p. 80 (Hindi original)
Udaipur was on the geographic border of the Paramara dynasty, which was strategically important. On the hill adjoining Udaipur — encircled on all sides by a fort — the remains of a fortification are visible even from Google Map. Besides the main temple, the presence of the vast precinct of the palace tells that the kin of the Paramara kings themselves must have lived here for the security of this border.

The rulers of the Paramara dynasty were worshippers of Shiva, and in their long rule of three or four hundred years, they had built countless temples, schools and hostels in their vast kingdom. On the border of Bundelkhand, Udaipur was one of the busiest commercial centres of their kingdom, where, in a city encircled on all sides by a fort, a palace, several stepwells, doorways, temples, squares-and-platforms, and a courthouse were built over centuries. The whole town-layout can even today be seen and understood from Google Map, which extends up to the fortification done all the way up the hill.

The inscription then proceeds to trace the genealogy of the Paramāra house of Mālwā. It tells us in vv. 5-6 that a hero who had sprung from the fire-altar of Vasishtha on the Mount Arbuda (Ābū) slew the enemies and brought back the cow of the sage which Viśvāmitra had taken away, and was, in reward of this deed, given the name of Paramāra (slayer of the foes) by Vasishtha who also blessed him with kingship. \( ^{2} \)  This account is purely legendary, a later creation, and can mean nothing more than to invent a high origin for the house, calling the Paramāras as belonging to agni-kula, exactly similar to that of the races born from the Sun and the moon. \( ^{3} \)  The present inscription, as we may note here, is the first to mention this legend, which is echoed in the later epigraphical records of the house, e.g., in the Nagpur Museum inscription and the Mändhātā copper-plate grant of Jayasimha II, as we shall see below.
