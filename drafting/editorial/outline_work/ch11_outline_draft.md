## 1. Chapter Function

- **Central question:** How did Udaypur become something that offices know, list, repair and own, and what did each of those acts do to the fabric, to the way the place is read, and to what is left out of the record?
- **Principal argument / intervention:** Udaypur was turned into an object of knowledge and administration **piece by piece, by different hands, and never as a whole**. The antiquarian tours of the 1870s made the temple and its inscriptions the place's public identity and fixed the first attributions (and the first correction). Gwalior State's archaeology department found, removed, catalogued and reset stones in the 1910s–1920s, and its descriptions passed straight into post-independence scholarship. After 1947 custody divided: the ASI holds the temple (and, per INTACH, the Barah Khambi), the state holds a mosque, the Pisanhārī temple and the Naṭarāja, the forest department holds the hill and its cave, and the revenue department holds the palace ground. Only in 2020–21 did anyone attempt to document the whole town, and that documentation was made by a non-governmental body. The chapter's claim is that the shape of custody explains much of what is known, and much of what has decayed: the protected temple is the best-recorded and best-kept object, while the palace, the fortification and the scattered sculpture appear in the record chiefly as losses. This is **interpretive** and must be argued from the documented pattern of custody, not asserted.
- **What changes over the chapter:** the scale widens from one temple and its inscriptions (11.1–11.2) to a divided set of custodians (11.3) to a whole town counted for the first time (11.4) to the gap between listing and protection (11.5). The reader's sense of the sources changes with it: from published reports read at second hand, to an editor's provenance notes, to one man's memory, to a survey's tables and a journalist's campaign.
- **How it develops Chapters 1–10:** It builds on Ch 1 §1.3 (the brass cover in V.S. 1841; maintenance "under Garde Sahib" in Tiwari's account; custody today) and §1.4 (Beglar 1871–72, Cunningham's correction in the reports of 1874–77, Bühler in *EI* I, Garde's 1925–26 find of the second slab, the restoration-reset slab, the survival legends). **It must not re-tell any of these.** It recalls each in a clause and asks a question Ch 1 did not: what the intervention *did*: what it removed, moved, fixed, named or left out. It also draws on Ch 2 §2.5 (illegal quarrying, Tiwari) only by recall; on Ch 6 for the inscriptions' content (none of it repeated here); on Ch 7 for the structures whose custody is discussed (descriptions stay in Ch 7); and on Ch 10 for the Tughluq mosque and the Aurangzeb tradition as *events* (here only as *attributions made and corrected by the antiquarians*).
- **What it must establish for Chapter 12:** that by 2021 the official map of Udaypur was a patchwork of custodians with a documented record of encroachment, theft, quarrying and decay, and that the most visible interventions of that year (the palace eviction, the temple-lane clearance) came from a campaign and a revenue court, not from the heritage departments. Chapter 12 then takes up the people who live inside that patchwork: the lane-clearing by residents, the encroachments as households, quarrying as work, and the youth groups.

## 2. Chapter Arc

11.1 (the 1870s–1880s: the temple enters print, and the first attribution is corrected) → 11.2 (the 1910s–1920s: a princely department finds, removes, restores and resets, and writes the description later scholars copy) → 11.3 (after 1947: who holds what; the one remembered clearance; what protection does not record) → 11.4 (2020–21: the first count of the whole town, and what kind of document it is) → 11.5 (the record of loss in the same years: encroachment, a revenue order, theft, vandalism, quarrying, a cracked repair) → close on what the archive of protection still lacks.

**Why this order.** Chronology carries 11.1–11.4, because the chapter's argument is cumulative: each layer of custody was laid on the previous one, and the reader must see the temple-first pattern established before seeing the town counted. The alternative, a thematic order (surveys / laws / repairs / listing / threats, as in the map's sub-topics), would put the ASI before the Gwalior department that preceded it and would split the 2021 documentation from the 2021 losses it recorded. Chronology is **suspended in 11.5**, which is a condition record of c. 2015–2021 organised by kind of threat; its sources (GEOHER, Tiwari) are of the same years as 11.4, so 11.5 is a cross-section, not a sequel.

**Where scale shifts.** 11.1–11.2: one building and its stones; Gwalior and Calcutta only as where the reports were printed and the slab went. 11.3: the scale jumps to departments (Centre, state, forest, revenue). 11.4: the whole town and hill, about fifty sites. 11.5: back down to single objects (a slab of wall, an image, a signboard) as instances of the pattern.

**Through-line (one sentence):** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur was made known and protected in pieces: the temple first and best, the rest late, partly and by other hands. The shape of that custody explains both what scholarship says about Udaypur and what has been lost from it.

**Opening move:** Open on the first printed description: Beglar's tour of 1871–72 and what he set down, a paved platform, minor temples "now disappeared or converted into" dwellings, and a mosque he attributed to Aurangzeb (C07-PANDE-005, C01-PANDE-012, C07-PANDE-008). This carries the chapter's question at once: the first official account both *recorded* the place and *mis-attributed* part of it, and the correction came from the next official account. It takes up Chapter 10's close by content: Chapter 10 should end with the town under Maratha (Scindia) rule, and Chapter 11 opens when that town is first written about by a government survey. ## 3. Sections

### 11.1 Into the reports *(the antiquarian tours, 1871–1888 · ~800 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that Udaypur first entered the written record of the state through two survey reports and an epigraphic edition. The first report described the temple's surroundings as already broken up and attributed the mosque to Aurangzeb. The second read the mosque's inscriptions and moved it to Muhammad Tughluq's time. Through these reports the temple became a standard example in later scholarship, so the attributions and plans of the 1870s still shape how Udaypur is read.
- **Purpose:** Ch 1 §1.4 gives one sentence to this ("Beglar … whose account Cunningham corrected … then Bühler's edition"). Turn it into an account of *what the reports did*: the record they made, the error they made and corrected, and the chain by which their plans and judgements passed into Kramrisch and Pande.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** The first official description was also the first official interpretation. The interpretation was corrected by reading the stones, not the tradition. The movement runs from tour report to plan to model, and the temple became the Udaypur of scholarship.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — What Beglar set down.** **Point:** The 1871–72 tour report records the temple on a paved, raised platform. The walls with seats around the platform had long disappeared, except in the mosque at the back. Minor temples once stood around the great one, and "all of them" had "disappeared or been converted into" dwellings. This is the earliest dated condition record in the corpus. It shows the precinct already broken up and lived in, generations before any department protected it. Do not adopt Beglar's count of seven minor temples; Pande's eight is the canon figure and belongs to Ch 5. Say "minor temples" and put the count in a note.

  2. **P2 — An attribution, and its correction.** **Point:** Beglar's report tied the mosque behind the temple to Aurangzeb. This is the village tradition's attribution, told in Ch 1 §1.4. ### 11.2 A princely department at work *(Gwalior State, 1910s–1940s · ~850 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that Gwalior State's archaeology department, under M. B. Garde, was the first agency to act on the fabric:
  - it found a second slab of the *praśasti* in a house in the town and took it to the Gwalior museum;
  - it noticed the town's inscriptions in its annual reports;
  - it ran restoration campaigns at the temple in the 1920s, one of which left its own inscription (V.S. 1985) and probably reset an inscribed slab inside out.

  Garde's descriptions passed, through his successor Patil, into the standard post-independence account. Before the department, care of the temple had been devotional patronage: the brass cover of V.S. 1841, recalled only.

### 11.3 Protected in parts *(after 1947: lists, custodians and one clearance · ~900 w)*

- **Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things.
  - After 1947 Udaypur appeared first on a successor state's list of monuments.
  - The temple came under the ASI at a date no source records. The one post-independence intervention any witness remembers is a clearance of houses and rubble around it, recalled by Narayan Vyas.
  - By 2021 custody had divided among the Centre (the temple; the Barah Khambi), the state archaeology department (a mosque, the Pisanhārī temple, the Naṭarāja), the forest department (the hill and its cave) and the revenue department (the palace ground).
  - Much of what the offices knew, above all the inscriptions, stayed in their files.
- **Purpose:** Replace the old canon's single "becoming protected" claim ("ASI protection covers the temple, not the hill") with the more complicated pattern the survey tables document. State the gap plainly.
- **Core claim / narrative movement:** Protection did not arrive as one act over one place. It came as separate listings by separate offices, each covering a building, never the town. The temple's custody is the oldest and the best kept; the rest is recent, partial or none.
- **Paragraph plan:**
  1. **P1 — On a new state's list.** **Point:** Patil's *Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat* counts 1,783 monuments on the State Archaeology Department's list. The 145 most important had been declared of national importance under the Ancient Monuments Preservation (Declaration of National Monuments) Act. ## 4. Chapter Conclusion

- **What has been demonstrated:** Udaypur was made into an object of knowledge in fragments, in a traceable order:
  - the antiquarian reports chose the temple and fixed its first attributions, one of them wrong and corrected from the inscriptions;
  - a princely department removed, reset and recorded stones and wrote the prose later accounts repeat;
  - after 1947 custody divided among offices by building and land title;
  - only in 2021 did a non-governmental survey list the town.

  The documented losses fall where custody is weakest.
- **What remains unresolved:**
  - when, and by what notification, the temple came under the ASI;
  - what the protected area's boundary is;
  - what repairs, if any, the ASI has made since 1947;
  - whether "Garde Sahib" is M. B. Garde;
  - whether the parapet slab was reset by Gwalior or by the ASI;
  - whether INTACH's notification proposal led anywhere.
- **The tension carried forward:** a protected temple in a town that is listed but not protected, where the quickest remedy came from a revenue court and a campaign.
- **How the last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content:** End on the people the record keeps meeting at its edges: the households cleared from the temple's sides, the occupants who handed over the keys, the residents who report a stolen image. Chapter 12 takes them up as residents, not as encroachers or witnesses. The paragraph must **not** recommend anything (the Afterword's task), must not narrate the residents' lane-clearing of February 2021 (Ch 12, C12-JAGTA-EN-008/-014), and must not end on Tiwari's rhetoric of awakening.
