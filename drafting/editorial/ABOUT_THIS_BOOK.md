# About this book

Udaypur is a village in Ganj Basoda tahsil, Vidisha district, Madhya Pradesh. The 2011 census counted 6,383 people there. Inside its gateways and lanes stand the remains of a medieval town, and at its centre a Śiva temple, the Udayeśvara, still in daily worship. This book asks how an ordinary village came to hold the remains, records and memories of a far more important place, and what that place was.

**The argument.**

- **The land** made the town possible without deciding it. With no river at its door, water had to be stored, and the town was built from the stone of its own hill.
- **The country around it** was old, named and carved in stone long before, but its past reaches the hill only as proposals.
- **The founding.** In the eleventh century the Paramāra king Udayāditya, Bhoja's brother, recovered his kingdom after an attack from three sides and gave his name to a town, a temple and a tank. The record makes the name and the temple certain by 1080. Whether he founded the town or renamed an older place, it does not show, and the book leaves that open.
- **The temple and the town.** The book reads the temple for its own making, the inscriptions for who made them speak, and the town's other works as layers more than a calendar.
- **The later centuries.** Udaypur did not simply fall into ruin. It lost rank in steps under successive masters, the Caulukya kings of Gujarat among the first.
- **The modern record.** From 1871 to 2021 the town was described and protected in pieces, and it lives on as a working village inside an old town.

The Afterword makes one argument, labelled as the book's own: a place that survived by being used is best conserved as a lived-in town and hill.

**The approach.** This is an evidence-based narrative history. Its sources are:
- the edited corpus of Paramāra inscriptions;
- archaeological surveys and the district Gazetteer;
- colonial-era tour reports, as later scholars cite them;
- modern scholarship;
- the testimony of a local writer and of residents.

It keeps their kinds apart:
- An inscription's praise of a king is a claim.
- A tradition is evidence of memory.
- A survey's date is a proposal.
- Testimony never supplies a measurement or a date.

Where sources disagree, the book names the readings and says which is firmer.

**The scope.** Twelve chapters and an Afterword run from the village today, through the land, the country before Udaypur, the founding, the temple, its inscriptions and buildings, ritual, society, the later regimes and modern conservation, to the present village and its future.

**Who it is for.** The book is written for:
- readers of Indian and regional history;
- residents of Udaypur and the Vidisha district;
- heritage professionals and administrators;
- researchers.

For researchers, it ends with the questions it leaves open, set out chapter by chapter.
