# Chapter 1 — Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory — interpretive brief (revised)

**Rule applied throughout:** no claim is stronger than its weakest supporting item. Where the evidence is tentative, the argument says so through its framing (a question, a proposal, a reading set against another), not through hedges in the prose.

## Thesis

Udaypur's memory is organised around a single eleventh-century act of naming: Udayāditya gave one name to a town, a temple and a tank. What the place remembers now (its names, its *praśasti*, its dated markers, its survival stories) is arranged around that act and explained through it. Whether he founded Udaypur or renamed a place already in use is the question the evidence leaves open.
- If he founded it, the town is a royal creation of the 1050s, and its "long memory" is the memory of one man's project.
- If he renamed it, the founding was an act of appropriation, laid over a sixth-to-ninth-century place whose own name and masters survive only as tradition.

*Why this can be disputed:* a reader could argue that the place's memory is continuous rather than organised around one act, or that its identity comes from the later layers (Tughluq, Mandu, Mughal, Scindia) rather than from the foundation. The chapter has to show that those layers were added to the founder's precinct and explained through it, and has to set out the founded-or-renamed question fairly from the evidence on both sides.

*Removed from the earlier draft of this thesis:* "has almost forgotten the place he renamed". No item documents forgetting. The village keeps traditions of earlier masters (Bhringraj, Gond rule), which argues against it.

## 1.1 Arrival

**Claims:**
1. **The village lives inside the medieval town, not beside it.** (C01-GEOHER-003, C01-KB-GEOHER-010, C01-KB-GAZ-002, C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-001, C01-KB-GEOHER-002, C01-KB-INTACH-001, C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-003)
   - Evidence: a census village of 6,383 with schools and a Monday market; houses pressed against Paramāra-style gateways; a house of twelve courtyards still used by its family, most of them ruined; shop rows holding debris near living shops.
   - Reasoning: daily life occupies the old town's gateways and courtyards. The old plan still shapes where people walk and trade. Weakest item: the gateway description is one walker's testimony (Tiwari), so it is presented as what he saw.
2. **Several of the village's memories attach to the founder's works and court.** (C01-JAGTA-HI-009, C01-GEOHER-013, C01-GEOHER-014, C01-JAGTA-EN-033, C01-JAGTA-EN-044, C01-PANDE-006, C01-JAGTA-EN-024, C01-JAGTA-EN-032, C01-GEOHER-012)
   - Evidence: black-soil fields are remembered as Udayāditya's tank (Tiwari's account); one tradition makes the vermilion image a dancer of his court, set against another tradition and a scholar's *yakṣa* identification; the time-keepers' house recalls the temple's bells; a mosque is still called the *kachahari*; residents remember a tally of waterworks.
   - Reasoning: where the memories touch the temple, the lake and the court, they point to the founder and his works. They are stories, not records, and the chapter presents them as traditions.
3. **The monument stays in memory partly because it is still in use.** (C01-GEOHER-005, C01-PANDE-010, C01-JAGTA-EN-025, C01-KB-JAGTA-HI-004, C01-JAGTA-EN-030, C01-PANDE-004, C01-MADHYABHARAT-009)
   - Evidence: morning and evening *pūjā*, daily *darśan*, an annual fair, and a nineteenth-century report that Hindus and Muslims alike reverenced the *liṅga*. Tiwari's counter-testimony is that the lane and its traders show little care for the temple or for history (C01-KB-JAGTA-EN-007, C01-KB-JAGTA-EN-008). The chapter sets the two side by side: worship continues, and attention to the monument as history is thin.
   - Reasoning: worship renews the temple's place in daily life. This is an inference from use, offered as one reason among others, not the cause.

**Significance:** this sets out the book's opening puzzle in concrete terms: a small village whose fabric and many of whose memories are those of a royal foundation.

**Connections:** Chapter 7 (gateways, fort, mosques), Chapter 9 (oral traditions such as Berni Mata), Chapter 12 (the living village).

## 1.2 Long memory

**Claims:**
1. **Later rulers added to the founder's precinct rather than replacing it.** (C07-PANDE-009, C01-MADHYABHARAT-012, C01-PARTEMPLES-005, C10-PARTEMPLES-003, C01-MADHYABHARAT-005, C01-PANDE-005, C07-MADHYABHARAT-012, C07-MADHYABHARAT-011, C01-KB-MADHYABHARAT-002, C01-MADHYABHARAT-013, C01-PANDE-011)
   - Evidence, in order:
     - the Paramāra temple court (work from 1059, the flag in 1080);
     - a mosque of the 1330s set in that court after a corner shrine and the western hall were taken down;
     - a Mandu-style mosque (1488);
     - the Shahi Masjid (1632) beside a mansion, probably the governor's;
     - a brass cover for the *liṅga* (1775).
   - Reasoning: each regime built in or beside the same precinct, so the town's history accumulated in one small compass.
2. **The place was a seat of government under the Paramāras, and governing buildings recur under later regimes.** (C01-GANGULY-005, C01-GANGULY-015, C01-GANGULY-016, C01-KB-MADHYABHARAT-002, C01-KB-GEOHER-011, C01-KB-MADHYABHARAT-006)
   - Evidence: a Paramāra divisional headquarters and Devapāla's residence; later a walled town with gates, a mansion that was *probably* a Mughal governor's residence, and a fortified palace that appears to have served successive rulers, its builder unknown.
   - Reasoning: governing functions recur here across regimes. The later evidence is a probable identification and a survey's reading of the ruins, so the claim is that the pattern recurs, not that it explains every later investment.
3. **The temple survived conquest by losing part of its court.** (C07-PANDE-009, C01-PANDE-005, C01-PANDE-013, C01-GANGULY-013, C01-PANDE-003)
   - Evidence: a shrine and a hall were demolished and a mosque built in their place while the central temple stood. The mosque's inscriptions name Muhammad Tughluq; a later legend names Aurangzeb.
   - Reasoning: the survival recorded in stone was partial. The survival legends taken up in 1.5 dwell on what was saved.
4. **The temple carries a long record of visitors.** (C01-KB-MADHYABHARAT-003, C01-KB-GEOHER-006, C01-GEOHER-010, C01-SERPENT-002)
   - Evidence: pilgrims' records from distant places, and inscriptions in three languages across eight centuries. The three attributed assessments (Hardy, Krishna Deva, Adhikari) are the chapter's only named judgements, kept brief.

**Significance:** Udaypur's "long memory" is a memory of accumulation around one centre. That explains how a village came to hold so large a record.

**Connections:** Chapter 4 (the Paramāra foundation and government), Chapter 7 (the mosques and the palace), Chapter 10 (the regimes after 1305), Chapter 11 (the ASI's custody).

## 1.3 Epistemic discipline: founded or renamed?

This section carries the thesis's open question and argues it from both sides.

**Claims:**
1. **The documented record begins in 1080.** (C01-PARTEMPLES-005, C01-CII-007, C01-CII-008, C01-KB-GANGULY-005, C01-GANGULY-008)
   - Evidence: the in-situ inscription records the flag raised in 1080 and supplies the earliest known date for Udayāditya. The *praśasti* is undated.
   - Reasoning: everything earlier is either tradition or a proposal. That is the firm boundary from which both sides of the question are argued.
2. **The case that he renamed an existing place rests on proposals, not secure dates.** (C01-KB-GEOHER-013, C01-KB-GEOHER-014, C01-KB-GEOHER-012, C01-KB-GEOHER-007, C01-INTACH-006, C01-GAZ-017)
   - Evidence:
     - a two-line hill inscription whose reading is described as tentative, assigned to the eighth century;
     - Bhujariya Talab, which "may belong to the 8th or 9th century";
     - columns provisionally identified as seventh–eighth-century temple fragments, reused in a stepwell;
     - a reading of the village's layers back to the sixth century;
     - an uncemented wall of large blocks.
   - Reasoning: the items agree in direction but each is a proposal. Together they make an earlier place possible, and the chapter presents them as the survey's proposals.
3. **The case that he founded a new town rests on an absence and a reading.** (C01-JAGTA-EN-050, C01-JAGTA-EN-042)
   - Evidence: one local interpretation dates the town's emergence to Udayāditya because no earlier Paramāra inscription is known. The same interpretation allows an earlier frontier post.
   - Reasoning: an argument from the absence of Paramāra inscriptions does not exclude a non-Paramāra place, and even this view allows an earlier post. Its weight is equal to the evidence it rests on.
4. **Memory keeps earlier masters only as people the founder conquered.** (C01-GEOHER-008, C01-GEOHER-011, C01-GEOHER-009, C01-GEOHER-022, C01-GEOHER-024)
   - Evidence: Bhringraj of Bhringrajpur, and Gond territory renamed by Udayāditya. Bhringraj's dynasty is unknown.
   - Reasoning: these traditions favour "renamed", but as stories told from the founder's side. The chapter sets them beside the archaeological proposals as a second, independent kind of evidence. Neither kind is decisive.
5. **What each answer would mean.** No new evidence; this is interpretation of claims 1–4.
   - **Founded:** Udaypur is a royal creation of the 1050s.
   - **Renamed:** the naming was an act of appropriation, and the village's earliest history lies under the founder's name.
   - The chapter states both outcomes and leaves the question open.
6. **The archive is late and partial.** (C01-PANDE-012, C01-KB-PANDE-001, C01-KB-PANDE-002, C01-PANDE-002, C01-CII-009, C01-KB-CII-001, C01-KB-CII-002, C01-GANGULY-007, C01-GANGULY-018, C01-GANGULY-020)
   - Evidence: modern scholarship from the 1870s; the *praśasti* edited in 1888; its second half found in 1925–26 and badly abraded; later medieval mentions that are a possible identification (Udhapuram) or retrospective (the error-strewn 1513 record).
   - Reasoning: the founder is documented mainly through his own panegyric. That is why the question cannot yet be closed.

**Significance:** the thesis's open question is argued here, with the evidence weighed on both sides.

**Connections:** Chapter 3 (the landscape before Udaypur), Chapter 6 (the inscriptions in detail), Chapter 11 (archaeology and the second slab).

## 1.4 Naming the settlement (kasba)

**Claims:**
1. **The single name is the foundation's most lasting trace.** (C01-MADHYABHARAT-001, C01-GANGULY-002, C01-CII-002, C01-PARTEMPLES-001, C01-INTACH-001, C01-GANGULY-004)
   - Evidence: one king's name was given to a town, a Śiva temple and a tank (Udayapura, Udayeśvara, Udayasamudra), and Udayapura survives as Udaypur.
   - Reasoning: the town's name has outlasted the tank (lost) and the temple's own name (displaced, see claim 3). Naming made three works one act.
2. **The dates and the inscription's subordinate house bear on what kind of founder he was.** (C01-KB-CII-006, C01-MADHYABHARAT-012, C01-HARDY-004, C01-SERPENT-001, C01-GANGULY-020, C01-GANGULY-021, C01-CII-002, C01-CII-012)
   - Evidence: work began in 1059 and the flag was raised in 1080. One historian's hypothesis makes Udaypur the base of a paternal territory in 1059. The inscription names Udayapura as the capital of a Nēmaka dynasty subordinate to him.
   - Reasoning: if the 1059 work came before his accession, the town began as a provincial seat. The paternal-territory reading is a hypothesis and is presented as one. The readings go to a `{{note}}`.
3. **The names drifted while the place-name held.** (C01-CII-001, C01-JAGTA-EN-006, C01-CII-006, C01-KB-GANGULY-003, C01-GAZ-004, C01-GEOHER-021, C01-JAGTA-HI-009)
   - Evidence: Udayeśvaradeva gave way to Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, date unknown. The tank is located as ruins nearby, as Udaysagar Talab or as black-soil fields, or not identified at all.
   - Reasoning: the founder's name survives best where it is attached to the place as a whole.
4. **The name now invites confusion and lament.** (C01-GANGULY-011, C01-GANGULY-012, C01-GANGULY-024, C01-JAGTA-EN-001, C01-JAGTA-EN-039, C01-PANDE-002, C01-JAGTA-EN-003)
   - Evidence: a founding legend (snake and fire, dismissed as myth); the overshadowing Udaipur of Rajasthan; the "Udepur" spelling; the "Astpur" (sunset-town) pun.

**Significance:** this links the thesis to the name the reader has been using since the first page.

**Connections:** Chapter 4 (Udayāditya's foundation), Chapter 7 (the tank), Chapter 12 (the village's view of its own name).

## 1.5 Stance

**Claims:**
1. **The dynasty's only complete genealogy survives on Udaypur's slab, and it is panegyric.** (C01-GANGULY-009, C01-GEOHER-006, C01-CII-009, C01-JAGTA-HI-003, C01-CII-008, C01-GANGULY-006, C01-GANGULY-008, C01-KB-CII-004, C01-BHOJA-TIMES-009, C01-BHOJA-TIMES-004)
   - Evidence:
     - two slabs, one found in a Dhimar's house;
     - undated, and assigned to about 1072 or to 1059;
     - the only complete genealogy from Upendra to Bhoja, and the earliest record of the dynasty's mythical origin;
     - its earliest names are absent from another record, and its building claims are uncorroborated.
   - Reasoning: Udaypur's own monument is the fullest witness to the dynasty that named it, so the founder appears vivid in praise and thin in fact.
2. **The accession date decides whether the 1059 work was a king's or a prince's.** (C01-KB-GANGULY-007, C01-KB-GANGULY-008, C04-PANDE-005, C01-KB-PARTEMPLES-001, C01-MADHYABHARAT-011, C01-GANGULY-026, C01-KRAM-2-002, C01-MADHYABHARAT-012)
   - Evidence: the accession is dated 1058/1059 or c. 1070 (the working position), and work began in 1059. One historian notes that the inscription states Udayāditya was "ruling in Sam 1116" (1059), while placing his recovery of the kingdom after Jayasiṃha's death.
   - Reasoning: on the working position, the work began about a decade before the accession. The text states the question and its stakes; who argues what goes to a `{{note}}`.
3. **The legends explain the temple's survival in ways the documents do not.** (C01-GANGULY-013, C01-KB-GANGULY-002, C01-PANDE-003, C01-JAGTA-EN-043, C01-JAGTA-EN-029, C01-JAGTA-EN-034, C01-PARTEMPLES-002, C01-MADHYABHARAT-006)
   - Evidence: wetted gunpowder under Tughluq, Aurangzeb's change of heart, organised local resistance (a modern conjecture), single-night building, buried treasure.
   - Reasoning: each story accounts for what still stands. None accounts for the losses recorded in 1.2.

**Significance:** this closes the argument. The records that carry Udaypur's story (a founder's praise, two dates and a set of survival legends) are all organised around the act of naming, which is why the founded-or-renamed question cannot be closed from them alone.

**Connections:** Chapter 2 opens on the land in which the town (founded or renamed) stands.

## Questions the chapter raises

- Was Udaypur founded or renamed? This is the thesis's own question, argued in 1.3.
- Who held the place before Udayāditya, and under what name? Bhringrajpur and Gond rule are remembered, not documented.
- What was the eighth-century hill inscription: a local lineage's record, a pilgrim's, or a workman's? Its tentative reading names a *sūtradhāra*.
- Did the 1059 work begin under a reigning king or a territorial lord?
- How much of the original precinct was lost when the mosque was fitted in?

## Unsupported claims cut

- That Udaypur has forgotten the place Udayāditya renamed: no item documents forgetting, and the earlier-masters traditions run against it.
- That Udayāditya chose the site because it was already sacred: no item says why the site was chosen.
- That the village's population descends from the medieval town's: no evidence.
- That the temple survived because of local resistance: a modern conjecture, kept only as an attributed conjecture.
- That governing functions explain why later rulers invested here: the later evidence is a probable identification and a survey's reading. The claim is narrowed to a recurring pattern.
