# Chapter 1 — Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory — chapter plan v5

*Approved structure (chapters.md, Plan changes, 14 October 2026). This plan replaces outline v4 and absorbs the interpretive brief, which is kept for the record.*

**Historical question.** How did an ordinary village in Basoda tahsil come to hold the remains, records and memories of a far more important place, and what kind of royal act stands at its beginning?

**Through-line.** By the end, the reader knows that an ordinary village lives inside a Paramāra king's town, whose remains, records and memories all reach back to one eleventh-century act of naming, and that the evidence leaves open whether that act founded the place or took it over.

**Arc.** The present puzzle (1.1) → what the place was: a king's town on the eastern edge of the Paramāra kingdom (1.2) → how it changed across eight centuries (1.3) → how it is known (1.4) → the question the evidence leaves open, ending on the ground both answers stand on (1.5, into Chapter 2).

**Each paragraph's evidence** is marked **E** establishing (carries the claim), **C** corroborating (use where it matters), **X** context (use only if it helps). Not every item needs to appear. Each item's tier is shown in the evidence block.

**Points** name the historical work a paragraph must do. The paragraph as a whole delivers it, directly or cumulatively. No sentence merely repeats a claim already made in the paragraph.

## 1.1 The village under the temple *(The town today · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that today's Udaypur is a large, ordinary village living inside the gateways, lanes and memories of a much greater medieval town. Its residents keep the founder's name and his temple in worship, but hold little of the history.

**Purpose:** Show that today's village lives inside the medieval town, and that its memories reach back to the founder.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The approach.**
   - **Point:** Make the reader meet Udaypur in the order anyone coming from Ganj Basoda does: the hills, then the temple, then the village. By the end of the paragraph the reader should sense a place that once mattered, without being told so twice. Vijay Manohar Tiwari is named once here, as the eyewitness: use the item for Tiwari's sight of the temple, never for a distance (testimony never supplies a measurement). Roads, the railway station and distances are context, at most one clause: this is an approach, not a location list. (Plan note, run 2, 15 October.)

## 1.2 A king's town on Malwa's eastern edge *(The Paramāras and the founding · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the Paramāras as a kingdom, not an adjective. It was ruled from Dhārā, reached Bhilsa on the east, was nearly lost at Bhoja's death and was recovered by Udayāditya, a kinsman of Bhoja whose exact relationship is disputed. That king gave one name to a town, a temple and a tank on the kingdom's eastern edge, and the town served, in his reign, as the seat of a local house under the king, and in the 1160s–70s, under the Caulukyas, as the seat of an eastern division (R4, U1). Chapter 4 tells the full story; this section gives what the reader needs now.

**Purpose:** The Paramāra polity in outline, and the founding as an act within it.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A kingdom ruled from Dhārā.**
   - **Point:** Who the Paramāras were as a polity: lords of Malwa ruling from Dhārā, a kingdom whose eastern reach ran to Bhilsa. Udaypur lies at that eastern edge.
   - E: C01-KB-GANGULY-010, C01-KB-GANGULY-009, C01-KB-GANGULY-004. C: C01-BHOJA-TIMES-006. X: C01-KB-GANGULY-020.
2. **P2 — Bhoja's fall and Udayāditya's recovery.**
   - **Point:** Narrate the crisis. Bhoja died at war and his enemies took Dhārā. Jayasiṃha regained the throne with Chālukya help, then died fighting new invaders. Udayāditya recovered the kingdom. His kinship to Bhoja is disputed: give the canon wording, with one note setting out the readings by tier (the praśasti names him only as successor; the Nagpur inscription calls him *bandhu*, "a relative in general"; the Dongargaon and Jainad inscriptions make him a brother; a sixteenth-century genealogy, rejected by its editor; later "cousin or brother" and "nephew"). The accession is c. 1070, the working position, with the other datings beside it in a note. The Nagpur verse's boar image is the dynasty's own telling.

C: C01-KB-CII-003, C01-KB-CII-017, C04-PANDE-005, C01-KB-PARTEMPLES-002. Lineage note: C04-CII-084, C04-CII-083, C04-CII-086, C01-CII-014, C04-GAZ-003, C04-BHOJA-TIMES-029, C01-KB-JAGTA-EN-013. Accession note: C04-PANDE-005, C01-KB-PARTEMPLES-001, C01-MADHYABHARAT-011, C01-GANGULY-026, C01-KRAM-2-002, C01-KB-JAGTA-EN-012. X: C01-KB-BHOJA-TIMES-002.
3. **P3 — One name for a town, a temple and a tank.**
   - **Point:** The founding was an act of naming as much as of building: Udayapura, Udayeśvara and Udayasamudra under one name, with work read as begun in V.S. 1116 (a reading: R1, R3) and a flag raised in 1080. Whether 1059 falls before his kingship changes what kind of founding it was: a lord's seat in his rise, or a new king's first work. State that once.

4. **P4 — How the east was governed.**
   - **Point:** Explain what "a seat of the eastern division" meant. The kingdom was organised into maṇḍalas, bhogas, viṣayas and pathakas under viceroys and local officers. In a record of the early 1170s Udayapura lies in the Bhāillasvāmi-mahādvādaśaka, and in the 1160s it was a headquarters of the eastern division under the Caulukyas (R4, U1); and its own inscription calls it the capital of the Nēmaka house under the king: a royal name over a local line's seat. Ganguly's reading of a feudatory line at Udayapur is attributed to him.

**Transition:** The kingdom that made the town outlived its founder by two centuries, and every regime after it built inside his precinct.

## 1.3 Eight centuries in one precinct *(Layers of rule · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader can place Udaypur's layers in time:
- later Paramāra governors and kings at the town;
- a Candella king at Bhilsa in 1134;
- Paramāra rule in the east into the 1260s;
- the conquest of Malwa in 1305;
- the 1330s mosque in the temple court;
- Mandu and Mughal building;
- the Scindia brass;
- the state's custody today.

Through all of it the founder's temple stayed in worship.

**Purpose:** Chronological layers inside and beside the founder's precinct.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The east after Udayāditya.**
   - **Point:** The east stayed contested after the founder.
   - Udayāditya's line lost Malwa to Gujarat under Yaśovarman, and a Candella king issued a grant while residing at Bhāillasvāmin in 1134.
   - The Mahākumāra branch held on in the east.
   - Paramāra officials and kings still acted at Udaypur: Lūṇapasāka's grants of 1171–73 and Devapāla residing there. The Lūṇapasāka date variants go in a note.
   - Jayavarman II still held Bhilsa in 1263.

   Narrate it briefly. Chapter 10 tells the full story.

## 1.4 How Udaypur is known *(The record · 750 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows the kinds of evidence behind the story and what each can bear:
- the praśasti, the dynasty's self-portrait, broken and kept in Gwalior;
- the two dated inscriptions;
- eight centuries of pilgrims' writing on the stones;
- scholarship since the 1870s;
- the survival legends, which are memory.

**Purpose:** How Udaypur is known, and what each kind of record can and cannot establish.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A broken self-portrait.**
   - **Point:** The praśasti is the dynasty's only complete genealogy, and it opens with a hero sprung from Vasiṣṭha's fire-altar on Mount Abu. It is also praise: its earliest names are missing elsewhere and its building claims are uncorroborated. Its two halves were found apart and now lie in Gwalior, undated (readings in a note). It shows the founder as the dynasty wished him seen.

   - **Point:** The founding has two fixed points, V.S. 1116 and V.S. 1137. The second is read three ways: the flag-staff erected, the flag hoisted or the flag-staff repaired. ## 1.5 Founded or taken over? *(The open question · 700 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader understands why the founding question is genuine:
- what the case for an older place rests on (proposals that point the same way, each uncertain);
- what the case for a new town rests on (an absence, which its own proponent qualifies, and traditions that remember earlier masters only as people the founder defeated);
- what each answer would mean for the kind of royal act Udaypur began with.

The section ends on the ground both answers stand on, which leads into Chapter 2.

**Purpose:** Argue the founded-or-renamed question honestly and leave it open.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The case for an older place.**
   - **Point:** Present the survey's proposals: the hill inscription read tentatively and assigned to the eighth century; the Bhujariya Talab, which may belong to the eighth or ninth century, its embankment older than the Udaysagar; columns provisionally dated seventh–eighth century and reused; layers read back to the sixth century; the uncemented wall. They point the same way, which gives the case its weight, but each is a proposal, which keeps it a case rather than a finding. Grade confidence on each clause. Who proposes what goes in a note. Never merge the tentatively read two-line inscription with the survey's inscription that "remains to be deciphered".
   - E: C01-KB-GEOHER-013, C01-KB-GEOHER-014, C01-KB-GEOHER-012, C01-KB-GEOHER-007, C01-KB-GEOHER-008. C: C01-GEOHER-021, C01-INTACH-006, C01-KB-INTACH-002. X: C01-GAZ-017, C01-KB-CII-005.
2. **P2 — An absence, and remembered masters.**
   - **Point:** The case for a new town rests on the absence of earlier Paramāra inscriptions. That absence cannot rule out a place under other masters, and its proponent allows an earlier frontier post. The traditions of Bhringraj and Bhringrajpur and of Gond rule remember those masters, but only as people the founder defeated: memory favours renaming while telling it from the founder's side. The Daśārṇa and Udayagiri associations belong to Chapter 3.
   - E: C01-JAGTA-EN-050, C01-GEOHER-008, C01-GEOHER-011, C01-JAGTA-EN-042. C: C01-GEOHER-009, C01-GEOHER-022, C01-GEOHER-024, C01-JAGTA-EN-007, C01-JAGTA-EN-008.
3. **P3 — What each answer would mean.**
   - **Point:** Set out what each answer would mean:
     - If Udayāditya founded Udaypur, it was a royal creation on the recovered kingdom's eastern edge, and its long memory is the memory of one eleventh-century project.
     - If he renamed it, the naming laid a king's name over an older place: appropriation or reorganisation, a political re-inscription.

     The later echoes (Udhapuram, the sixteenth-century praise, the record of 1513) all come after the act and cannot decide between the two. Do not inflate the uncertainty.
   - E: C01-GANGULY-007, C01-GANGULY-018, C01-GANGULY-020, C01-PARTEMPLES-005. C: C01-KB-GANGULY-004.
4. **P4 — The ground both answers stand on.**
   - **Point:** Close briefly on what is common to both answers: the place sits on a hill of stone with tanks around it, older and newer. Whoever first lived here had to make terms with that ground. Lead into Chapter 2 by content; never claim the land caused the founding.
   - E: C01-KB-GEOHER-014, C01-GEOHER-021. C: C01-JAGTA-HI-009.

**Transition:** Chapter 2 turns to that ground: the Betwa valley's water, stone and seasons.
