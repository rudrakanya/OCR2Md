**Historical question.** How did a frontier ridge on Malwa's eastern edge come to carry a king's name? What can the record actually say about whether Udayāditya made Udaypur before he was king, as king, or by putting his name on a place that others held?

**Through-line.** At the start the reader knows Udaypur as a king's town, said to have been begun in 1059. By the end, the reader understands four things:
- the Paramāras reached this frontier as Rāṣṭrakūṭa feudatories who made themselves kings;
- they held it as their eastern limit under Bhoja and nearly lost the kingdom after him;
- the town carries the name of the kinsman who recovered it, and a contemporary record ties a temple there to him by 1080;
- the familiar beginning in 1059, before he was king, is Ganguly's reading of late records, which the dated record cannot confirm.

**Arc.**
1. The frontier and the house that took it (4.1).
2. Bhoja's kingdom, and Bhoja's name on Udaypur's stone (4.2).
3. The broken succession and the recovery: who Udayāditya was (4.3).
4. The dates tested: 1059 against 1080, then the dated reign (4.4).
5. What Udayapura was in his kingdom, and what the record ties to him there (4.5, into Ch 5).

Chronology runs through 4.1–4.3, is suspended in 4.4, and returns in 4.5 P5 (his death).

## 4.1 From feudatories to kings of Malwa *(c. 900–1010; regional, then dynastic · 700 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things:
- the Paramāras first appear as Rāṣṭrakūṭa feudatories in Gujarat;
- Sīyaka's claim to a sovereign title brought war, and the Paramāras sacked Mānyakheṭa in 972–73;
- the first royal charter issued from Malwa itself dates from 975;
- the dynasty's own genealogy, kept at Udaypur, magnifies these kings in ways their editors do not accept.

**Purpose:** Answer Ch 3's open line with the house that took the frontier, briefly, and set up the praśasti as a source of claims.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The claim on the east.**
   - **Point:** Take up 3.5. The district Gazetteer credits Sīyaka II with ousting the Chandellas from Malwa and with a kingdom bounded on the east by Vidisha. Other modern accounts give the same eastern limit at his abdication. This is a frontier, not a holder of Udaypur's hill.
   - E: C04-GAZ-001 (T7), C04-PARTEMPLES-002 (T5). C: C04-GANGULY-035 (T5). Note: the Gazetteer's "known date" of 972 (C04-GAZ-001).
2. **P2 — Feudatories who took a king's title.**
   - **Point:** The earliest grants place the family in Gujarat. At Harsola in 949, Sīyaka is both *Mahārājādhirāja* and "crest-jewel among the feudatories", and the grants name his line back to Bappaiparāja (c. 900).
   - E: C04-CII-002 (T1), C04-CII-003 (T1), C04-CII-006 (T1). C: C04-CII-007 (T1). Note: C04-GANGULY-015, C04-GANGULY-027 (T5) (Ganguly's Rāṣṭrakūṭa-vassal origin, attributed).
3. **P3 — Independence by war.**
   - **Point:** The title probably provoked the Rāṣṭrakūṭa expedition. The Paramāras answered at Khalighaṭṭa on the Narmadā and then sacked Mānyakheṭa in 972–73, a date fixed by Dhanapāla's colophon. Sīyaka was still ruling in 969. Vākpati-Muñja's grant of 975 is the first royal charter of the house issued from Malwa.
   - E: C04-CII-004 (T1), C04-CII-042 (T1), C04-GANGULY-032 (T5), C04-CII-012 (T1). C: C04-CII-009 (T1), C04-GANGULY-033 (T5).
4. **P4 — What Udaypur's stone claims for them.**
   - **Point:** Muñja died a captive of the Chālukya Tailapa II between 993–94 and 997–98. Yet the Udaypur praśasti has the Karṇāṭas, Lāṭas, Keralas and Cholas at his feet and his sword raised in Tripurī. The editor finds no evidence for Tripurī and calls the Karṇāṭa claim "evidently inadmissible". Sindhurāja has half a verse and is known mainly from a poet. *(Evidence pass: C04-CII-129 is the Udaypur praśasti's own claim; it sets up "claim, not event" on Udaypur's stone.)*
   - E: C04-CII-024 (T1), C04-CII-129 (T1), C04-CII-026 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-045 (T5), C04-CII-025 (T1). Note: C04-CII-129 (the editor on the Tripurī claim).
5. **P5 — A genealogy written later.**
   - **Point:** Brief. The praśasti alone gives the early line complete (recall 1.4 in a clause), and its descriptions of those kings are "purely conventional". Its first ruler, Upendra, is the Kṛṣṇarāja of the grants.
   - E: C04-CII-021 (T1), C04-CII-023 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-019 (T5). X: 1.4 (agnikula), clause only.

**Transition:** Within a generation of Sīyaka, the eastern limit was held by a king whose reputation would outrun his kingdom.

## 4.2 Bhoja's kingdom and Bhoja's name *(c. 1010–1055; the eastern frontier in view · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows three things:
- Bhoja's dated reign (1011–1047) and the date by which the Bhopal country is known to have been his (1018);
- the model of royal building and learning his name carried: Bhojpur, the Vāgdevī image, a Śaiva treatise;
- that at Udaypur his fame survives first as praise cut by his successors, which modern writers have extended into memory.

**Purpose:** Bhoja only for the frontier, the model of royal building, and his memory at Udaypur (user's brief).

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The east under Bhoja.**
   - **Point:** Bhoja's dated records run from the Mōdāsā grant (1011) to the Tilakawāḍā grant (1047). The first Paramāra charter in the Bhopal territory (1018) shows that country already in his kingdom. In 1030 Al-Bīrūnī put the kingdom's eastern limit at Bhilsa. Udaypur's country lay inside that edge, but no record of Bhoja's time names it.
   - E: C04-CII-072 (T1), C04-CII-071 (T1), C04-CII-049 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-051 (T5). X: 1.2 (the eastern reach), clause.
2. **P2 — A king who built and wrote.**
   - **Point:** The model his name carried:
     - the Bhojpur temple, begun but never finished, linked to a colossal dam (2.4 told the dam; one clause);
     - the Vāgdevī image of 1033–34, engraved by Manathala;
     - a Śaiva Siddhānta treatise, the *Tattvaprakāśa*, ascribed to him. Doubts about his authorship go in a note.
   - E: C04-HARDY-001 (T5), C04-CII-063 (T1), C04-PANDE-003 (T5). Note: C06-BHOJA-TIMES-046 (T5) (authorship doubts; homed Ch 6).
3. **P3 — Bhoja on Udaypur's stone.**
   - **Point:** At Udaypur, Bhoja's fame survives as praise cut by his successors. The praśasti gives him verses 16–20: his strength, gifts, victories and temples, and a realm "from Kailāsa to Malaya". Verse 20 says he "covered the world all around with temples" to Kedāra, Rāmeśvara, Somanātha and others, which the editor reads as the four quarters. Few remains of such work can now be traced. That this is praise, not an inventory, was said in 1.4 (recall).
   - E: C04-CII-069 (T1), C04-CII-070 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-104 (T5). X: 1.4 (Ray's doubt), clause.
4. **P4 — Bhoja remembered at Udaypur.**
   - **Point:** Modern Udaypur writing draws Bhoja closer than the record does. Tiwari sees a "deep relation" between the town and Bhoja, and imagines Udayāditya preparing for kingship under his protection. Popular books make Bhoja a founder of cities. The record gives no act of Bhoja at Udaypur; it gives only the praśasti's statement that Udayāditya followed him on the throne. Memory, told as memory.
   - E: C04-JAGTA-EN-024 (T8). C: C04-RAJABHOJ-EN-012 (T9). X: C04-CII-084 (T1) (the praśasti's bare succession).
5. **P5 — Bhoja's end, in a clause.**
   - **Point:** Bhoja died some time before 1055 (1.2 told how).
   - E: C04-GANGULY-070 (T5).

   *(Folded into P4's close if short.)*

**Transition:** The praśasti that praises Bhoja passes over the king who came next.

## 4.3 The broken succession and the recovery *(c. 1055–c. 1080; read against rival records · 1,100 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things:
- the dynasty's own praśastis leave Bhoja's successor Jayasiṃha out, though his grants place him between 1047 and 1080;
- a record of V.S. 1116 from Vāgaḍa suggests (the editor's presumption) a struggle in which Udayāditya may have been Jayasiṃha's adversary;
- the coalition that overwhelmed Jayasiṃha was three-sided and is reconstructed differently by the editor, in two lists, and by Ganguly;
- Udayāditya's victory is praised on Udaypur's second slab, and his relation to Bhoja stays disputed, for reasons that matter to Udaypur.

**Purpose:** The evidential core. Deepen 1.2 by showing what each record says and where they part. No new verdicts.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The missing king.**
   - **Point:** Recall in one clause Jayasiṃha's restoration (1.2). New: the Udaypur and Nagpur praśastis, which list almost every king, omit him. His own grant names him Bhoja's immediate successor, and the editor places him "between 1047 and 1080". The panegyric's silence is a choice.
   - E: C04-CII-076 (T1), C04-CII-092 (T1), C04-CII-075 (T1). Note: C04-CII-092 (Kielhorn: the reign was short).
2. **P2 — A rival for the throne?**
   - **Point:** The Pānāheḍā inscription of V.S. 1116 (1059) shows Maṇḍalika of Vāgaḍa "greatly devoted" to Jayasiṃha. The editor presumes that Maṇḍalika's son may have helped Jayasiṃha against "his adversary Udayāditya or some other person". A possibility only, but it bears on 4.4: the year read as Udaypur's beginning is a year of Jayasiṃha's acknowledged rule.
   - E: C04-CII-106 (T1). C: C04-CII-093 (T1).
3. **P3 — Three enemies, and two accounts.**
   - **Point:** New against 1.2: the shape of the second invasion. Udayāditya's son's record counts three enemies. In the editor's fuller account these were:
     - the Kalachuri Karṇa;
     - the Chālukya Someśvara II, whose general, the Gaṅga Udayāditya, came with the Hoysala Ereyaṅga.

     Someśvara II was quarrelling with his brother Vikramāditya, Jayasiṃha's ally. Jayasiṃha was "possibly" killed. Ganguly tells it otherwise: the Caulukyas and Karṇāṭas held Dhārā, and Udayāditya won with cavalry from the Cāhamāna Durlabha III against the "Gurjara" Karṇa. The text keeps the event; the identities go to a note.
   - E: C04-CII-114 (T1), C04-CII-096 (T1), C04-CII-095 (T1), C04-CII-083 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-074 (T5). Note: C04-CII-082 (T1) (the editor's other list, p. 135: Karṇa, Bhīma, Ereyaṅga); C04-GANGULY-076, -078 (T5) (Durlabha III; the "Gurjara Karṇa"); C04-PANDE-006 (T5) (Bhatia: Karṇa's invasion of 1079, defeated by Udayāditya "and his allies").
4. **P4 — Udaypur's second slab, and a war that went on.**
   - **Point:** 1.4 told the second slab's rout of the lord of Chedi (recall in a clause). New:
     - the editor uses it to answer Bühler's doubt about Bhoja's reverse;
     - the slab's last complete verse says that for him who had restored the earth "it was easy to restore the Primeval Boar", which the editor takes as a hint that Udayāditya restored a Boar temple, recorded in the lost portion. This is the editor's inference only.
     - Peace did not last: after Vikramāditya VI took the Chālukya throne in 1076, an inscription of 1077 calls him "a great fever of terror to the king of Dhārā" (Ganguly).
   - E: C04-CII-130 (T1), C04-CII-112 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-096 (T5). X: C04-JAGTA-EN-048 (T8), C04-JAGTA-EN-052 (T8) (Verma's reading via Tiwari, note only).
5. **P5 — Why his descent matters at Udaypur.**
   - **Point:** One sentence with the canon wording: a kinsman of Bhoja; the exact relationship is disputed (1.2's note holds the readings; do not repeat them). New:
     - The question is Udaypur's because the "junior branch" view would give Udayāditya ground of his own in the east before he was king. That view rests on a pedigree from an Udaypur inscription in which Udayāditya, "having obtained the eastern territory", proceeded into Malwa, which his ancestors had once governed.
     - Verma, as Tiwari reports, holds that "meditating at the feet" (*pādānudhyāta*) is a formula of praise, used even of Chandragupta II's commanders, and proves no descent.
     - Tiwari asks why no record of Bhoja's time names a brother.
   - E: C04-GANGULY-079 (T5), C04-CII-084 (T1). C: C04-BHOJA-TIMES-038 (T5), C04-JAGTA-EN-027 (T8), C04-JAGTA-EN-029 (T8), C04-JAGTA-HI-032 (T8). Note: C04-JAGTA-EN-038 (T8) (Kielhorn and Ganguly against Ray, as Tiwari reports); C04-CII-076 (T1) (the editor calls him "definitely" Bhoja's brother).

**Transition:** That eastern territory comes with a date, V.S. 1116, eleven years before the accession.

## 4.4 Before the throne? The dates and what rests on them *(1059 / c. 1070 / 1080–1093; chronology suspended · 1,050 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things:
- that "begun in 1059" is Ganguly's reading of late records: a fragmentary record he himself dates 1513 and calls full of "gross mistakes", and a pedigree record that CII dates to V.S. 1562 and rejects;
- that modern handbooks and surveys repeat the year without adding a witness;
- that 1080 stands on a record on the temple itself;
- that his reign is followed year by year from 1080, with c. 1070 the working accession.

**Purpose:** U6. The text, not only a note, says whose reading 1059 is and why it is weaker than 1080. This is the one section where scholars are named in the running text, because the disagreement is the answer (house voice C).

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The case for 1059, at its strongest.**
   - **Point:** Give it fairly:
     - the district Gazetteer: two temple inscriptions refer to work begun in V.S. 1116, and it calls the building's years "abundantly clear";
     - Patil gives the same span;
     - Hardy writes "founded … in 1059 and consecrated in 1080";
     - Kramrisch's caption gives c. 1059–1087;
     - the survey gives one foundation year for the town, the temple and the lake.

     Behind them stands Ganguly, who found Udayāditya "ruling in Sam 1116" and reconciled this with the accession by a reign in his paternal territory, "apparently from Udayapur". (1.2 noted the reconciliation; here it is the point.)
   - E: C04-GAZ-006 (T7), C04-GAZ-005 (T7), C04-HARDY-003 (T5), C04-GANGULY-094 (T5), C04-GANGULY-092 (T5). C: C04-MADHYABHARAT-009 (T5), C04-KRAM-2-001 (T5), C04-GEOHER-003 (T7), C04-INTACH-003 (T7). Note: C04-JAGTA-EN-044 (T8) (Tiwari's crown prince sent to the frontier: testimony).
2. **P2 — Where the year comes from.**
   - **Point:** U6. Trace each support:
     - **(a)** The statement that Udayāditya built the temple in V.S. 1116 comes from the fragmentary record Ganguly dates 1513 and himself calls "very perfunctorily executed" and full of "gross mistakes" (1.5, recall in a clause).
     - **(b)** The statement that he was ruling in V.S. 1116 comes from the Udaypur pedigree (4.3 P5). The CII editor dates that record V.S. 1562 (1505/06) and rejects its genealogy against the Dōngargāon and Jainad records.
     - **(c)** The two late records are not identified with each other in any item; keep them apart (Part I, C1-B). The handbooks and surveys add no independent witness. The inscription on the temple that the surveys read as V.S. 1116 has no edition in the corpus (note).
   - E: C04-GANGULY-093 (T5), C04-GANGULY-094 (T5), C04-CII-131 (T1), C04-CII-132 (T1). C: C04-GAZ-006 (T7). X: C01-GANGULY-020 (T5) (1.5). Note: C06-JAGTA-EN-011 (T8), C06-INTACH-001 (T7) (the V.S. 1116 text as read by Verma and INTACH; homed Ch 6).
3. **P3 — The other side of the scale.**
   - **Point:**
     - The CII editor: the Udaypur record of V.S. 1137 (1080) "supplies the earliest known date for the king Udayāditya", and names the temple Udayeśvaradeva, "evidently after the name of its constructor".
     - The flag-hoisting record is in situ on the temple.
     - The Pānāheḍā record of V.S. 1116 shows Jayasiṃha acknowledged that very year (4.3 P2), and Ganguly's own argument from Karṇa shows Udayāditya could not then have been sovereign.
     - A lord building on his own ground before his accession is possible, on a late record. It is not shown.
     - Recall 1.4's three readings of the 1080 record in a clause.
   - E: C04-CII-108 (T1), C04-CII-119 (T1), C04-CII-106 (T1). C: C04-PARTEMPLES-007 (T5), C04-GANGULY-092 (T5). X: 1.4 (three readings). Note: C04-JAGTA-EN-061 (T8) (Verma's 30 March 1080: his calculation; not used in text).
4. **P4 — The dated reign.**
   - **Point:**
     - **The dated records:**
       - 1080, Udaypur;
       - 1081, an image consecrated at Dhārā by Jasahara;
       - a gift by Naravarman, made while Udayāditya was encamped at Dhavalī near Ujjain, for a perpetual lamp for his dead brother Lakṣmadeva;
       - 1086–87, a Śambhu temple at Jhālrapāṭan built by the *paṭṭakila* Jannaka of an oilman's lineage "when Udayāditya was prosperous in the region";
       - a royal endowment to Somanātha on the fort of Kōśavardhana.
     - **The span:** the reign runs to his death in 1093 (4.5).
     - **The accession:** c. 1070 is the working position. The earlier starts (1058, 1059) rest on the same 1059 reading (interpretive, said once; the list of datings was in 1.2's note: recall, do not list).
   - E: C04-CII-109 (T1), C04-CII-078 (T1), C04-CII-120 (T1), C04-CII-110 (T1), C04-CII-121 (T1), C04-CII-089 (T1). C: C04-GANGULY-112 (T5), C04-PANDE-005 (T5), C04-PARTEMPLES-005 (T5). Note: C04-GANGULY-098 (T5) (Ganguly: reign closed shortly after 1086; CII's 1093 preferred); C04-MADHYABHARAT-005 (T5) (Patil's 1058–87, recalled).

**Transition:** If the record makes him king first and patron at Udaypur second, the question becomes what Udaypur was to that king.

## 4.5 A town with a king's name *(Udayāditya's reign and after; Udaypur in focus · 1,300 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows five things:
- what the name proves (a king's name on a temple, and on a town and tank by a later report) and what it does not (a founding);
- that one damaged record makes Udayapura the seat of a subordinate Nēmaka house under the king;
- that the provincial geography often attached to his Udaypur belongs to the 1170s;
- how later writers have pictured his town;
- that the reign ended in 1093, with Naravarman succeeding and a memorial gift in 1095.

The one work a contemporary record ties to him at Udaypur is the temple that bears his name.

**Purpose:** Change scale to the town. Udaypur is the subject throughout.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — What the name proves.**
   - **Point:** Recall the triad (1.2) in a clause. New:
     - the 1080 record names the temple after its constructor;
     - Patil and Ganguly report another inscription in the temple that credits him with town, temple and tank;
     - Ganguly also cites "a contemporary inscription" that the city was built by Udayāditya, though the item does not name it;
     - later writers turn the name into a founding outright: he "laid the foundation of the city" (Ganguly), "founded the city" (Pande); INTACH and Adhikari follow.

     A royal temple was a royal gift (*dāna*), bringing merit to the giver: a king who named a temple after himself gave in his own name. Name ≠ foundation: 1.5's question is sharpened, not closed.
   - E: C04-CII-119 (T1), C04-MADHYABHARAT-006 (T5), C04-GANGULY-106 (T5), C04-GANGULY-101 (T5). C: C04-GANGULY-105 (T5), C04-PANDE-007 (T5), C04-INTACH-002 (T7), C04-PARTEMPLES-006 (T5), C04-HARDY-002 (T5). Note: C04-GANGULY-101 (which inscription is not stated); C04-JAGTA-EN-049 (T8) (Tiwari's argument from the absence of earlier Paramāra inscriptions: 1.5 told the absence; note only).
2. **P2 — A seat for a subordinate house.**
   - **Point:** 1.2 gave the reading in one sentence; here it is the subject:
     - the damaged slab calls Udayapura the capital town of the Nēmaka dynasty, "which was subordinate to Udayāditya";
     - its object appears to be that the place was put in charge of a Nēmaka king, by whom, or in whose reign, a temple was built;
     - his father Śūdraka is said to have defeated a king of Gujarat, possibly the Caulukya Karṇa, which sets the house in Udayāditya's time.

     Which temple is meant is not stated. Tiwari, reporting readers of the script, gives the Nēmakas as Śiva worshippers and surmises that they administered the place when the temple was begun. That surmise goes in a note.
   - E: C04-CII-113 (T1), C04-CII-122 (T1). C: C04-JAGTA-EN-062 (T8). Note: C04-JAGTA-HI-059 (T8), C04-JAGTA-EN-062 (T8) (Tiwari's surmise).
3. **P3 — A province described eighty years later.**
   - **Point:**
     - the district Gazetteer attributes to Udayāditya a record of 1173 that describes Udayapur's province (the Bhilsa *maṇḍala*, the Bhṛṅgārikā *pāṭhaka* of sixty-four villages, a *daṇḍanāyaka* probably seated at Udayapur);
     - Udayāditya died in 1093, and in the 1170s the province was governed for the Caulukyas (1.3, as corrected);
     - the Mahādvādaśaka-maṇḍala is a twelfth-century name.

     The province is evidence for the town's later rank, not for his founding.
   - E: C01-GAZ-010 (T7), C04-GAZ-007 (T7), C04-CII-089 (T1). C: C04-BHOJDEV-HI-011 (T9). X: C01-GANGULY-010 (T5) (1.3). Note: the Lūṇapasāka date is "early in the 1170s" (home 6.4).
4. **P4 — The town as it has been pictured.**
   - **Point:** In Tiwari's telling, the town was planned:
     - square, walled, with the king's palace inside;
     - a "mini-capital" on the Kalachuri frontier, made important by its sandstone;
     - with a great hall and Sanskrit school "on the lines of" Bhoja's foundations.

     Ganguly lists Udayapura among the towns that rose to importance under the dynasty. The walls, the palace and the tanks are dated by no record of this reign (Ch 7 treats their fabric). The land is setting, not cause (no "because of the quarries").
   - E: C04-JAGTA-EN-056 (T8), C04-JAGTA-EN-042 (T8), C04-JAGTA-EN-045 (T8). C: C04-GANGULY-108 (T5), C04-JAGTA-EN-058 (T8). X: C07-GEOHER-003 (T7) (homed Ch 7, clause).
5. **P5 — The king's death, and what Udaypur keeps.**
   - **Point:**
     - Lakṣmadeva died before his father; Naravarman's lamp gift shows it.
     - A further son, Jagaddeva, is praised for refusing the throne out of regard for an elder brother.
     - Udayāditya died in the latter part of 1093. Naravarman succeeded in V.S. 1151 (1094), and in V.S. 1152 (1095) made a gift at his father's annual funeral rite.
     - Close: of all that later writers give him at Udaypur, a contemporary record ties one work to his reign, the temple that bears his name. This hands the chapter to Ch 5.
   - E: C04-CII-077 (T1), C04-CII-085 (T1), C04-CII-089 (T1), C04-CII-087 (T1), C04-CII-091 (T1). C: C04-CII-079 (T1), C04-GANGULY-085 (T5). Note: C04-GANGULY-088 (T5) (tradition and Ganguly have Jagaddeva or Lakṣmadeva succeed; the CII line preferred).
