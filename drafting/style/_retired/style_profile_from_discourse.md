# STYLE PROFILE

## 1. Stance
This is a spiritual-polemical address using historical figures, not evidence-led historical narrative. The speaker occupies the position of a confident teacher: the reader asks about contemporary relevance; the answer supplies an overarching interpretation of religion and humanity.

Argument precedes illustration. Krishna is declared exceptional, then contrasted with an undifferentiated religious past. Brief narrative elements—dancing, a legendary birth, an imagined gardener—serve the thesis rather than develop independent scenes. Inclusive first-person language makes the audience share responsibility for failing to understand or absorb the teaching. The speaker’s authority comes chiefly from conviction, repetition, and moral contrast, not demonstrated research.

## 2. Paragraph architecture
Four recurring patterns organize the address, sometimes across the transcription’s paragraph breaks:

- **Rule and exception:** sweeping judgment about religion → Krishna as counterexample → amplified claim of wholeness → implication for humanity. This structures discussions of sorrow, incarnation, bodily acceptance, and repression.
- **Assertion and amplification:** short governing proposition → several parallel reformulations → emphatic restatement or instruction. The Freud discussion repeatedly announces a transformation before insisting that it must be understood.
- **Diagnosis made physical:** abstract division → causal account of its damage → bodily or natural image → consequence for followers. The body–soul opposition becomes anemia, lifelessness, and a fallen leaf.
- **Analogy and reversal:** familiar hypothetical situation → apparently favorable result → reversal of its interpretation → application to religion. The gardener’s one flowering tree becomes evidence against, rather than for, his method. The fighting-hands comparison likewise turns apparent struggle into shared destruction, though its concluding application is truncated.

These are recursive rather than steadily additive structures: the prose often revisits one proposition through another contrast or image.

## 3. Evidence handling
Names enter the main text as intellectual and religious reference points. Schweitzer receives an attributed paraphrase; Freud supplies a turning point; Jesus, Buddha, Mahavira, Rama, and Zarathustra furnish contrasts or reported traditions. No works, dates, inscriptions, archival documents, or notes are supplied.

Disagreement is handled through controlled concession followed by decisive exclusion. Schweitzer’s criticism is accepted broadly, then rejected wherever Krishna is concerned. The speaker does not compare competing scholarship or establish the criticism’s original context.

Reported traditions are introduced with formulas such as “It is said,” without investigation of provenance. The gardener’s numerical example is hypothetical, not empirical evidence. Distinguish these persuasive devices from historical substantiation: an illustrative story makes the argument intelligible but does not verify its generalizations.

## 4. Rhythm
The supplied sentence mean is **16.4 words**, close to the **16-word median**. This suggests a medium-length declarative base rather than prose dominated by very long sentences. The **9.8-word standard deviation**—approximately **96 words² of variance**—indicates substantial movement around that base.

The mixture matters: **16.9%** of sentences contain eight words or fewer, while **9%** reach thirty words. Short assertions establish or seal a judgment; longer sentences accumulate causes, attributes, qualifications, and consequences. Repeated conjunctions and parallel clauses give these expansions the momentum of speech.

Paragraphs average **56.3 words**, with a **33.9-word standard deviation** and a **16–151-word range**. Compact verdicts alternate with developed comparisons. The low question rate fits the opening question-and-answer frame rather than a continuously interrogative method. First-person language sustains audience involvement.

Treat these as statistics of the supplied extraction. Mid-sentence breaks, incomplete wording, and an intrusive running header mean that not every apparent boundary represents an authorial rhythmic choice. Do not imitate transcription damage.

## 5. Indic terms
The supplied text uses plain spellings such as Krishna, Mahavira, and Rama, without diacritics. No italics are visible in the transcription; that does not establish the PDF’s original typography.

Names are assumed familiar rather than formally glossed on first appearance. Their significance emerges through comparison—for example, different degrees of divine incarnation—not through dictionary-style definitions. The conceptual vocabulary is predominantly English: body, soul, liberation, religion, and incarnation.

There are no displayed Indic-language passages or identifiable translated quotations. The effect is accessibility through ordinary religious vocabulary, not philological precision.

## 6. Time and scale
Time is organized into an ancient past, an inadequate present, and a promised future. Krishna’s antiquity is made paradoxically compatible with future relevance: humanity, rather than the ancient figure, has yet to catch up.

Freud functions as an epochal hinge between repression and a new understanding of human life. References to thousands of years and multitudes of unsuccessful seekers enlarge the indictment, but do not establish a chronology. There is no dynastic time, archaeological deep time, or dated sequence.

Scale repeatedly contracts from humanity to the body, a leaf, a tree, or two hands, then expands back to civilization. These small physical models make universal claims easier to apprehend.

## 7. Openings and closings
The chapter title promises future significance. An explicit questioner then asks why Krishna matters now. The answer opens with a superlative judgment, not a setting, incident, or documentary discovery.

Internal units often begin with a compact assertion or a connective that resumes the argument: “And,” “But,” or “So.” Such openings preserve the feeling of continuous spoken explanation. A renewed claim about Krishna’s future launches the turn to Freud.

Local endings tend to deliver verdicts, assign responsibility, or complete a reversal. They tighten the preceding development rather than summarize it neutrally. No complete chapter ending is available: the supplied extract ends mid-sentence, so its final cutoff cannot be treated as a crafted closing.

## 8. Register and vocabulary
The register is exhortative, accessible, repetitive, and absolute. Evaluative words organize the argument: whole against fragmented, joyful against sorrowful, accepting against suppressive. Superlatives and exclusives repeatedly elevate one figure above the rest.

Abstract claims receive sensory reinforcement through dancing, bodily vitality, barrenness, flowering, and illness. Lists often work by near-synonymous accumulation rather than by introducing sharply distinct categories. Generic references to man and humanity universalize the audience.

Within this extract, the author does not build documentary suspense, reconstruct detailed historical scenes, or sustain scholarly qualification. Occasional hedges and concessions appear, but do not alter the dominant certainty.

Do not describe the prose as cliché-free or restrained. Island, oasis, desert, watershed, and warfare imagery are familiar; “worth his salt” is openly idiomatic. Dramatic declarations and sweeping contrasts are central techniques, not things this writer avoids. Their persuasive force should not be mistaken for evidential reliability.

## 9. Rules for imitation
1. **Open with significance:** pose a question of present relevance or announce a temporal paradox before supplying background.
2. **State the governing judgment early:** let subsequent paragraphs explain and intensify a clear proposition.
3. **Organize contrasts consistently:** establish opposed values, then show how the focal figure changes or escapes that opposition.
4. **Repeat strategically:** return to the central name and governing concepts instead of disguising every recurrence with synonyms.
5. **Alternate assertion and expansion:** follow compact declarations with cumulative explanation, then land on a short verdict.
6. **Give abstractions a body:** translate conceptual damage or flourishing into a physical process or natural image.
7. **Develop analogies into arguments:** establish the comparison, test its apparent lesson, and reverse the attribution where useful.
8. **Implicate speaker and reader together:** use inclusive first-person language for shared habits and responsibility, reserving the singular for an embodied thought experiment.
9. **Make temporal shifts argumentative:** move between past, present, and future to explain relevance, rather than inserting chronology without a purpose.
10. **Keep terminology accessible:** introduce unfamiliar concepts through their function or contrast; do not manufacture scholarly apparatus absent from the material.
11. **Separate rhetorical confidence from verification:** in historical nonfiction, substantiate generalizations and identify traditions or hypotheticals as such rather than borrowing the sample’s unsupported certainty.
12. **Close local units decisively:** end on an implication, reversal, or forward hinge; preserve the spoken momentum without reproducing extraction errors.

## 10. Adaptation for this book (binding; where this section and rules 1–12 above conflict, this section wins)

The sample is a spiritual-polemical address, not evidence-led history. Only its craft transfers. Its certainty does not.

**What transfers:**
- **A governing judgment stated early** (rule 2). Each section opens on its claim about Udaypur and its land, then grounds that claim at once in cited fact.
- **Assertion, then expansion, then verdict** (rule 5). A compact sentence states the point, longer sentences carry the cited particulars, and a short sentence lands the implication. This is the model for the paragraph's `[INT]` sentences.
- **Rhythm.** Mean sentence about 16 words with a wide spread (SD about 10); about one sentence in six of 8 words or fewer; paragraphs that genuinely vary in length, within the book's 120–200-word band.
- **Abstractions given a body** (rule 6). An interpretation is carried by a physical thing in the evidence: a dry well in May, a stepwell's steps, a slab found in a house. The images must come from the cited facts.
- **Analogy that is tested and reversed** (rule 7). Use this only where the comparison stays inside cited facts, for example two dated works set against each other. It is never a hypothetical scenario presented as evidence.
- **Contrast as a structuring device** (rule 3). Founded against renamed, river against store, seam against valley.
- **Decisive local closings** (rule 12). Each paragraph and section ends on an implication or a forward hinge.
- **Time moved for argument's sake** (rule 9). Move between geological time, the eleventh century and the present only to make a point.

**What is overridden by the book's rules:**
- **Absolutes and superlatives** ("alone", "utterly", "the whole of"): banned in the narrator's voice. Use calibrated, specific claims no stronger than the weakest cited item.
- **Sweeping claims about all religions, all of humanity, "man"**: banned. Every interpretation names something concrete at Udaypur.
- **First-person "we"/"I" and implicating the reader:** not used. The voice is authorial, but in the third person.
- **Unsourced assertion:** every fact carries its evidence IDs. Traditions are marked as traditions ("is said to", "local tradition holds"), as the sample itself sometimes does.
- **Plain spellings without diacritics:** the book keeps consistent diacritics and italicises Indic terms on first use with a brief gloss (Udayāditya, *praśasti*, *talav*).
- **Repetition of the central name** (rule 4): limited by the book's monotony check. Vary how the place is named (the temple, the hill, the village, the tank).
- **Familiar idioms and stock imagery** (island, oasis, "worth his salt"): avoided. The banned-phrase list and generic-significance list still apply.
- **The question-and-answer frame:** not used. Openings are a concrete scene or object, as the writer brief requires.
