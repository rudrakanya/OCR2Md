# Style brief: narrative history of India

*A craft brief, not a sample. No exemplar passages are given to any drafting call. Where this brief and the writer brief differ, the stricter rule applies.*

## 1. Stance

The author stands between the evidence and the reader as an interpreter, not a reporter.
- Each paragraph makes an argument about Udaypur and its country. The facts are there to carry that argument.
- The author commits to readings the evidence supports, and shows where it stops: by framing ("the case for an older place rests on proposals"), not by stacking hedges.
- Narrative and argument alternate within the paragraph. An event or object is told plainly, then its meaning is drawn out.
- The voice is third-person throughout: no "we", no "I", no addressing the reader.

## 2. Paragraph architecture: each paragraph advances one idea

The governing claim comes early, usually the first sentence. The paragraph closes on its consequence. Four recurring patterns:
- **Claim → evidence → consequence.** State what the paragraph argues about the place. Bring in the cited facts that bear on it, combined where they serve the same point. Close on what follows: an unstated consequence, a forward link or a turn in the thesis.
- **Object → context → meaning.** Begin with a concrete thing (a slab, a tank, a dry well, a gateway), set it in its time or setting with cited facts, then say what it shows that the facts alone do not.
- **Two pieces of evidence → the tension → what turns on it.** Set a dated record beside a tradition, or one measurement beside another kind of evidence. Name the tension. Say what each answer would mean. The who-says-what detail goes to a note.
- **Then and now.** Join a fact from one period to a fact from another, both cited (the eleventh-century quarry and today's; Bhoja's dam and Udaypur's tanks). The comparison is the interpretation.

## 3. Facts combined into sentences

- Facts that serve one point go into one sentence, with the evidence IDs at the end of the clause or sentence they support. A sentence may carry several IDs.
  - Not: "The village lies 13 km from Basoda. [ID] Bareth lies to the west. [ID] A road runs east. [ID]"
  - But: "The village lies some 13–16 km from the market town and is reached by a metalled road from the nearest station, on the line between two district towns. [IDs]"
  - The interpretive sentence that follows then says what that position means. It does not repeat the facts.
- Never line facts up one per sentence. A paragraph in which most sentences are short single-ID statements fails.
- Settled ranges stay ranges (13–16 km, 55–70 km), with both items cited. A range is never narrowed to one source's figure.

## 4. Rhythm: sentence length varies with purpose

- **Long sentences** (25–45 words) carry evidence and qualification: several cited particulars, a subordinate clause that limits the claim, a "while" or "although" that holds two facts in tension.
- **Short sentences** (6–12 words) land a point: a verdict, a consequence, a turn. Use one or two per paragraph, never several in a row.
- Mid-length sentences carry narrative.
- Paragraphs run 120–200 words. Their length follows the idea they advance.
- Avoid opening consecutive sentences with the same word, and avoid repeating the place-name as the subject of every sentence.

## 5. Interpretation that adds insight

Every paragraph has at least one interpretive sentence, and each does at least one of these, labelled at its end:
- `[INT:cause]` explains a cause or reason;
- `[INT:consequence]` draws a consequence the facts imply but do not state;
- `[INT:comparison]` compares or contrasts across time or place, using cited facts;
- `[INT:tension]` names the tension between two pieces of evidence;
- `[INT:thesis]` connects the point to the chapter's thesis.

**The restatement test:** if a reader who had just read the cited facts would already know what the sentence says, cut it. An interpretive sentence names something concrete (this river, this slab, this distance, this ruler) and adds no new fact.

**Each paragraph has a written point.** The outline gives every paragraph a Point: the specific claim it exists to make. Build the paragraph so its facts lead there, and state the point as its interpretive sentence(s). Sharpen the wording if needed; never change the claim.

**Vary where and how the interpretation lands.** Put the interpretive sentence where the argument turns: early as the governing claim, between two pieces of evidence, or last as the consequence. A paragraph that turns on a tension may carry two. Not every paragraph ends on a one-line consequence.

## 6. Evidence handling

- Sources live in the notes.
- The text names a source only when its identity matters: competing datings, a tradition against an inscription, an eyewitness. At most once per paragraph, briefly.
- Inscriptions, the *praśasti*, slabs and grants are objects of the place. "The inscription records …" is narrative, not source-reporting.
- Disagreement: the text states the historical question and what hangs on it. Who argues what goes into a `{{note: … [IDs]}}`.
- Traditions are marked as traditions ("a local tradition holds", "is said to"). Panegyric is read as praise. Testimony is attributed to the witness.

## 7. Indic terms

- Italicise on first use, with a half-clause gloss: *praśasti*, a royal eulogy in verse; *talav*, a tank; *tekro*, an inhabited mound; *kharif*, the monsoon crop.
- After that, leave them roman and unglossed.
- Keep consistent diacritics: Udayāditya, Paramāra, Udayeśvara, Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, Vētravatī (in inscriptions), *liṅga*, *darśan*, *pūjā*, Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra.
- Translate quoted Sanskrit or Hindi into English, marked "(translation)" if quoted; never quote old-OCR items.

## 8. Time and scale

- Move between scales only to make a point:
  - geological time: the trap worn back from the sandstone; a mesa wearing down to a butte;
  - dynastic time: 1059, 1080, c. 1070;
  - the present: the census village, the working temple, today's quarries.
- Every jump in time carries a comparison or a consequence; none is decoration.
- Dates are given as the evidence gives them. Where items differ, give the range around the event, with a note.

## 9. Openings and closings

- A chapter opens on a concrete scene or object that carries its thesis.
- A section opens on its governing claim and closes by moving the argument forward.
- The chapter's last paragraph leads into the next chapter.
- No summaries. No rhetorical questions. No "this chapter".

## 10. Register and vocabulary, and what the author never does

- Exact, plain, concrete English (British spelling); verbs that do the work.
- **Never:**
  - absolutes or superlatives in the narrator's voice ("alone", "utterly", "the greatest", "unique");
  - sweeping claims about all of India, all religion or "man";
  - stock imagery or idiom (oasis, island, tapestry, "worth his salt");
  - generic significance ("rich heritage", "stands as a reminder", "speaks volumes", "a testament", "shaped the destiny");
  - false drama;
  - stacked hedges;
  - meta-commentary about the book or its method;
  - "we";
  - "[source] says / writes / records / gives / places", "according to [title]" or "the author".
