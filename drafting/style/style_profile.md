# House voice: the Udaypur history

*Approved with the editor-in-chief audit, 14 October 2026. This brief replaces the round-3 craft brief (archived in `_retired/`). It holds principles, not templates. The worked examples in H show a principle once. Never reuse their wording.*

The narrator is a historian standing in Udaypur and looking outward. The narrator knows the kingdom, reads stone and paper with equal care, and tells the reader what happened, to whom, and how we know, in that order of emphasis. Udaypur is the centre of gravity. The Paramāra world is the frame, and it enters when it changes what Udaypur means.

## A. What the narrator does

- **Tells history.** Events, changes and decisions are told in sequence. Where the evidence names people (a king, a governor, a feudatory house, a general, a pilgrim, an archaeologist, today's priests), they are the ones who act. A person or group is the subject only where a cited item describes that group or its action. Anonymous labour (builders, quarrymen, masons) is never invented from a passive mention: where a source says only that stone was quarried, the history says that.
- **Starts from a thing in a place.** Most sections begin with a hill, a slab, a dam, a gateway or a named person, and the argument grows out of it.
- **Gives context before the event, briefly and materially.** For example, who ruled at Dhārā and what had just happened to the kingdom, then the act at Udaypur. Then it returns to Udaypur.
- **Interprets where the history turns.** Interpretation is short, comes after the evidence, and is said once. Often it lives in the construction of the paragraph (what is set beside what, in what order), with no separate sentence announcing it.
- **Keeps time visible.** The reader always knows which layer is meant: before Udayāditya, his lifetime, the later Paramāras, the Sultanate, the Mughals, the Scindias, colonial archaeology, the modern village. The place changes its meaning across these; that change is one of the book's subjects.
- **Gives a mental map.** Selective distance, direction and height, used when they explain something: the village, the hill, the temple, the tanks, the road from Ganj Basoda, Vidisha and the Betwa.
- **Writes plain, exact English** (British spelling), with concrete nouns and working verbs. Sentence and paragraph length follow the job.

## B. Failures to avoid

- **Fiction.** Never invent thoughts, motives, conversations, scenes, weather, emotions, gestures, sensory detail, journeys, chronology or causation. Historical imagination chooses what to explain; it never supplies what happened.
- **Source-led prose.** "[Source] says/writes/records/gives/places …", "according to [title]", "the author", or paragraphs organised source by source. The text is about the past, and the notes say where it comes from.
- **Repeating a point.** No sentence restates, in interpretive dress, what the paragraph has already established (INT-TRIVIAL). One sentence that makes the point is enough.
- **A house rhythm.** "Not X but Y", "both X and Y", "the same X that … also …", neat binaries, triplets, colon reversals, closing aphorisms, "This mattered because …", "The significance lies …". None is banned. None may become the habit. Most paragraphs should end on content, not on a quip.
- **Uniform shape.** Every paragraph the same length; every paragraph evidence → evidence → verdict.
- **Abstraction as subject.** Avoid "the evidence", "the landscape", "the settlement" as the subject when a person or a place could act. They are fine where they are the true subject.
- **Determinism.** The land offers and withholds; people decide. Never write that geography caused a founding.
- **Generic significance and superlatives in the narrator's voice:** "rich heritage", "a testament", "stands as", "unique", "the greatest". Meta-commentary: "this chapter", "the reader", "we".

## C. Sources

- **One historical intelligence.** The book must not sound like whichever source supplied the most evidence. A survey report, a Gazetteer, an inscription edition, a monograph and a villager's memory are absorbed into one voice.
- **Evidence tiers** (strongest first for medieval events):
  1. contemporary inscriptions, read critically;
  2. the standing fabric and archaeology;
  3. later inscriptions;
  4. documents and normative texts;
  5. modern scholarship;
  6. colonial observation;
  7. modern survey and the 1979 Gazetteer;
  8. tradition and Tiwari's testimony;
  9. heritage and popular narratives;
  10. the book's own inference.

  Each evidence item is labelled with its tier.
- **The tiers in practice.**
  - For dynastic facts, the CII VII.2 edition outranks Ganguly. Where Ganguly interprets rather than reports, the note attributes it to him as his reading.
  - A tradition is evidence for **memory**, not for the event it narrates.
  - A praśasti is evidence of what the dynasty claimed. That the inscription claims something and that it happened are different propositions.
- **Naming a scholar in the text.** Only when the scholar is an actor in the story (the first readers of the slabs) or when the disagreement changes the answer. Otherwise the history is told and the note carries the sources.
- **Compatibility is not identity.** Never merge two inscriptions, buildings, tanks, traditions, rulers, places, layers or hypotheses because they fit together. They become one only if an item says so.
- **Disagreements.** The text keeps the reader's question and what hangs on it. Who argues what goes in a `{{note: … [IDs]}}`.

## D. Uncertainty

- Grade confidence on the clause it qualifies:
  - established: "was";
  - probable: "probably";
  - plausible or possible: "may", "could";
  - proposed: "has been read as", "is assigned to";
  - traditional: "the village tells", "is said to";
  - unknown: say so once.
- State secure history cleanly. Add a qualification only where it changes the interpretation. Never stack hedges, and never append "perhaps" by reflex.
- Treat the founding question as a real problem. Foundation, appropriation, reorganisation and renaming imply different kinds of royal action, so say what each would mean. Do not inflate the uncertainty into mystery.

## E. Narrative

- Chronology, people, place and change generate movement. A thematic section still moves through time where its material allows.
- Scale shifts on purpose: from the village to Malwa and back, from geological time to the eleventh century to today. Each shift earns its place by changing what the reader understands about Udaypur.
- Architecture is evidence. It answers questions about patronage, resources, craft, conquest, reuse, worship and memory, never an inventory of parts.
- Landscape is a working place: water held or lost, stone cut, fields sown, roads travelled.

## F. Paragraphs

- A paragraph has a job: to narrate, set context, explain, compare, reconstruct, qualify, describe a place, introduce a ruler, open or resolve a question, trace a consequence, separate memory from history, shift scale, or prepare a turn. The plan's **Point** names the historical work the paragraph must do. The paragraph as a whole delivers it, directly or cumulatively. It is not a sentence to be paraphrased, placed first or placed last.
- Paragraphs vary: some 60 words, some 250. Sentences vary with purpose: a long one carries evidence and qualification, a short one turns or lands. Avoid opening consecutive sentences the same way.
- Facts that serve one point go together; never line facts up one per sentence.
- Not every supplied item needs to appear. Use the establishing items, corroborate where it matters, and leave the rest.

## G. Chapters

- Each chapter has a historical question and an arc. Each section has a statement of what the reader understands at its end that they did not understand at its beginning. Sections hand over to one another.
- A chapter opens on something concrete that carries its question. Its last paragraph leads into the next chapter by content, not by announcement.
- No summaries, no rhetorical questions, no "this chapter".

## H. Worked examples from our own material

*Each shows one principle. Do not reuse the wording.*

**1. Interpretation by construction, not by announcement** (the approach to Udaypur)
- *Before:* "Udaypur was a place of considerable medieval importance, but its road and rail approaches now pass through neighbouring centres. […] The approach reveals a former centre now dependent on its neighbours."
- *After:* "Ten kilometres out on the road from Ganj Basoda, two of Udaypur's hills stand out among the hillocks on the horizon, and some five kilometres from the village the temple comes into view. The village itself is a large one in Basoda tahsil, 13–16 kilometres from the market town and its station, reached by a metalled road from Bareth on the line between Bina and Bhilsa. In 1979 the district's compilers could still call it 'once a place of considerable importance'."
- *Why:* the order of the facts (hill, temple, village) and the Gazetteer's "once" carry the puzzle. The before version announced it, then announced it again.

**2. People as agents; a praśasti read as a claim; a once-disputed kinship kept in its place** *(example updated 16 Oct for D2)*
- *Before:* "Udayāditya's relationship to Bhoja is described differently in the records. The inscriptions present him as restoring the kingdom."
- *After:* "When Bhoja died, his kingdom sank under its enemies' attacks, and the man who recovered it was his brother Udayāditya. {{note: … the inscriptions that state it, and the dissent … [IDs]}} A later Paramāra inscription remembers the recovery in the dynasty's preferred image: Udayāditya, 'like the holy Boar', lifting an earth that Karṇa had seized."
- *Why:* the king acts. The kinship dispute is one clause and one note. The boar image is presented as how the dynasty told the event, not as a report of it.

**3. Graded confidence instead of stacked hedges** (the case for an older place)
- *Before:* "The Bhujariya Talab may possibly belong to the eighth or ninth century, which might perhaps suggest earlier settlement."
- *After:* "The survey assigns the Bhujariya Talab, on the basis of its embankment, to the eighth or ninth century, older than the Udaysagar."
- *Why:* one hedge, attached to the claim that needs it ("assigns"). The inference about settlement is drawn once, where the proposals are weighed together.

**4. INT-TRIVIAL**
- "The roads define a village through its neighbours; the temple gives arrival a focus of its own." This only repackages the route facts before it. Cut it; let the next paragraph move on.
