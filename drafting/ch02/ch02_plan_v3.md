# Chapter 2 — The Betwa Valley: Geography, Geology and Natural Environment — chapter plan v3

*Approved structure (chapters.md, Plan changes, 14 October 2026). This plan replaces outline v2 and absorbs the interpretive brief, which is kept for the record. Moved out: wildlife (Chapter 12) and lichen dating (Chapter 7).*

**Historical question.** What could this country give, and what did it withhold, to the people who settled and built at Udaypur, and what did kings and villagers make of it?

**Through-line.** By the end, the reader knows the land the founder found: a country with no river at its door and no dependable groundwater, one wet season, black soil and a hill of building stone, so that its water had to be made and its town was built of its own rock. The land is the setting, never the cause.

**Arc.**
- 2.1–2.2: what the land withheld (a river that does not reach the town, wells that fail in summer, one wet season).
- 2.2–2.3: what it offered (black soil, a seam of building stone, forest on the slopes).
- 2.4–2.5: what people made of it (reservoirs by kings and tanks by villagers, a town built of its own hill).

It ends on the setting a founder found, without claiming that the land caused the founding.

**Each paragraph's evidence** is marked **E** establishing, **C** corroborating or **X** context. Not every item needs to appear. Each item's tier is shown in the evidence block. Modern climate and flow data describe the present regime only.

**Points** name the historical work a paragraph must do. The paragraph as a whole delivers it. No sentence merely repeats a claim already made in the paragraph.

## 2.1 Where the water runs, and where it does not *(Rivers and groundwater · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows four things about the Betwa:
- Udaypur belongs to the Betwa's country, the river of the inscriptions and of Bhilsa, the eastern centre of the Paramāra kingdom, but not to its banks;
- the nearest stream is a tributary a kilometre off;
- the upper river runs mainly in the rains;
- the sandstone that gives the town its stone gives it wells that fail in summer.

So Udaypur's water had to be held.

**Purpose:** Show that Udaypur belongs to the Betwa's country but not to its banks, and that neither river nor groundwater could supply it.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The river of the inscriptions.**
   - **Point:** Introduce the Vētravatī as the inscriptions' river, flowing by Vidisha/Bhilsa with the ancient city in the fork, and Bhilsa as the eastern limit and later the name of the province (Bhāillasvāmi-mahādvādaśaka) in which a record of the early 1170s, under Caulukya rule, places Udayapura (R4, U1). Political geography is setting only. The river served the great city, not this village.
   - E: C02-CII-001, C02-CII-002, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001, C02-KB-GANGULY-002, C01-KB-GANGULY-016. C: C02-GAZ-001, C01-GANGULY-005, C02-KB-PARTEMPLES-001.
2. **P2 — Udaypur's margin.**
   - **Point:** Place the village in the drainage:
     - the Betwa's course from Raisen;
     - the district's northward slope;
     - the Kewtan, 1.8 km from Shiva Pindi and 1 km from the village.

     The survey nevertheless counts Udaypur's as a landscape without rivers. What mattered here was whether a stream could be relied on, not how far away it was.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-002, C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GAZ-001. C: C02-GAZ-002, C02-GAZ-003, C02-KB-GAZ-001, C02-GAZ-005, C02-BETWA-CLIM-001.
3. **P3 — A river of the rains.**
   - **Point:** In its present regime the upper Betwa is intermittent until its last 85 km, rising in June and falling after September, with most of its runoff in the monsoon. Near its source it cannot carry a settlement through the dry months. Keep it brief; the rains themselves are 2.2's subject.
   - E: C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001, C02-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-BETWA-FLOW-003. C: C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-014.
4. **P4 — Wells that fail in summer.**
   - **Point:** In the Vindhyan sandstone around Udaypur, wells generally dry up in summer with water more than 15 m down. Only Vidisha draws a piped supply from the Betwa. The rock that gives the town its building stone withholds dependable groundwater.
   - E: C02-KB-GAZ-004, C02-GAZ-053, C02-GEOHER-003. C: C02-GAZ-043, C02-KB-GAZ-010, C02-KB-GAZ-011.
5. **P5 — Water held in the hollows.**
   - **Point:** Udaypur's water lies in the depressions around it, and its streets drain towards the fields, so village and farmland depend on water held from one monsoon to the next. This is the hinge to the rains (2.2) and to the tanks (2.4). Deliver it once.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GEOHER-028.

**Transition:** What the river would not give, the monsoon gave once a year.

## 2.2 The rains and the farming year *(Climate, soil and harvest · 850 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that the year's water arrives in four months, that bad years come in runs, and that the black soil, the wells and the tanks carry the winter crop. Udaypur's farming year is built on water held from the rains. The modern climate trend appears only in a note.

**Purpose:** The farming year as an arithmetic of stored rain.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Four wet months.**
   - **Point:** About 92 per cent of the rain falls in June–September and evaporation exceeds rainfall, so the year's supply is decided in one season. The heat of May, the end of the dry season, is a clause, not a paragraph. Date-stamp the 1979 Gazetteer here.
   - E: C02-GAZ-015, C02-GAZ-016, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004. C: C02-GAZ-014, C02-GAZ-021. X: C02-BETWA-FLOW-010.
2. **P2 — Bad years in runs.**
   - **Point:** Low rainfall has come in runs at Vidisha and Basoda, and the famine of 1899–1900 shows how far one season can fall short. A store has to outlast more than one weak monsoon.
   - E: C02-GAZ-017, C02-GAZ-018, C02-GAZ-019. C: C02-GAZ-023, C02-GAZ-022.
3. **P3 — Black soil and two crops.**
   - **Point:** Udaypur's ground is three-quarters black cotton soil and a quarter red-yellow soil from sandstone and shale. The black soil holds moisture and is "very good for wheat". The kharif is 90 per cent rain-fed but the rabi only 34 per cent, so the winter crop leans on held water: in the soil, the wells and the tanks.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-027, C02-GAZ-040, C02-KB-GAZ-005, C02-KB-GAZ-006, C02-KB-GAZ-007. C: C02-GAZ-009, C02-GAZ-013, C02-KB-GAZ-013, C02-KB-GAZ-014, C02-GAZ-011.
4. **P4 — Without a doab.**
   - **Point:** The district's best land lies where two rivers enclose it, in the doab of the Bah and the Betwa. Udaypur shares the black soil but not that advantage, so its harvest depends on the water it holds. The modern rainfall trend and the upper catchment's drying go in one note: what the present regime implies, never a claim about the eleventh century.
   - E: C02-GAZ-045, C02-GAZ-046, C02-GAZ-047. C: C02-GAZ-052, C02-GEOHER-027. Note: C02-BETWA-CLIM-007, C02-BETWA-CLIM-011, C02-BETWA-FLOW-006, C02-BETWA-FLOW-004, C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-002.

**Transition:** Where the rain gathers or runs off is decided by the rock beneath it.

## 2.3 A seam of stone *(Geology, landform and forest · 900 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader sees the hill as geology turned into history. Udaypur sits where erosion stripped the trap from bedded sandstone. The temple's sandstone was quarried in the hill close by. The hills are remnants of a wasting tableland, dry on top and hollowed by caves. The thin forest on the slopes has long been grazed and cut by the village.

**Purpose:** The rock that gave Udaypur its stone and its landmarks, and the forest that grows on it.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A village on the tableland.**
   - **Point:** Place Udaypur on the uneven, north-sloping tableland of the Vindhyan spurs, in the western foothills of the Garhi–Teonda range, at 340–480 m (mean 410 m). It sits above the river valleys that drain it. Describe the setting without saying it determined anything.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-005, C02-GAZ-007, C02-KB-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-009. C: C02-GEOHER-006, C02-GAZ-006.
2. **P2 — The seam and the temple's stone.**
   - **Point:**
     - Erosion stripped the Deccan trap, which covers most of the district, from this range and left sandstone and limestone at the surface around Udaypur.
     - The temple is built of hard red sandstone quarried in the hill close by (the item is passive: no builders or quarrymen as subject).
     - The same Vindhyan sandstone built Vidisha and Gyaraspur and makes the northern region rich in monuments.

     The geology that sets the village apart from the trap country put the founder's building stone within reach. People chose the stone; the rock did not choose for them.
   - E: C02-GAZ-010, C02-GAZ-008, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-PANDE-001. C: C02-GEOHER-013, C02-GAZ-042, C02-GAZ-050, C02-MADHYABHARAT-003. X: C02-GAZ-044.
3. **P3 — Remnants of a tableland.**
   - **Point:** In the survey's model, the hills on Udaypur's skyline are stages in the decay of a sandstone tableland: a two-storey mesa wearing down to a butte, as hard caprock breaks back over weaker beds. Water carved the caves and gullies but does not stay on the heights, whose valleys are dry and without channels. The hill offers stone and shelter, not supply. Give one figure per height; the rises above the plain go in a note.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-008, C02-GEOHER-009, C02-GEOHER-010, C02-GEOHER-016. C: C02-GEOHER-011, C02-GEOHER-015, C02-GEOHER-014, C02-GEOHER-007, C02-GEOHER-012, C02-GEOHER-004, C02-KB-GEOHER-010. X: C02-GEOHER-020, C02-KB-GEOHER-008, C02-KB-GEOHER-009, C02-KB-GEOHER-011.
4. **P4 — Forest on the slopes.**
   - **Point:** Woodland in the district keeps to the hill ranges and slopes, and on sandstone it is poor and shallow-soiled. The hill's present cover is disturbed by grazing and illegal felling: partly the village's own work, a forest used for timber, fuel and pasture. Leave wildlife out (Chapter 12). Tiwari's description of the fort hill in teak and grass may be used, naming Vijay Manohar Tiwari in full if this is the chapter's first mention.
   - E: C02-GAZ-027, C02-GAZ-012, C02-GEOHER-021. C: C02-GAZ-030, C02-GAZ-028, C02-GAZ-037, C02-GEOHER-019, C02-JAGTA-HI-001. X: C02-GAZ-038, C02-KB-GEOHER-012, C02-GAZ-031, C02-GAZ-032.

**Transition:** Of all this land offered, the town took its stone; its water it had to make.

## 2.4 Water made by kings and villagers *(Storage · 900 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader knows that storing water against the dry months was Paramāra practice. Bhoja's dam on the Betwa and his temple at Bhojpur are brief comparative evidence of it, as is the dynasty's habit of pairing temple and reservoir. The treatise the dynasty is credited with (its attribution doubted) prescribes such water as a norm. At Udaypur, town, temple and tank shared one name, and tanks, kunds and stepwells were sorted by use. The tanks are treated at landscape level; their detail is in Chapter 7.

**Purpose:** What kings and villagers made of a land without dependable water.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Bhoja's lake.**
   - **Point:** Bhoja's dam on the Betwa near Bhojpur held a lake of about 250 square miles, fed through the dry months from an auxiliary tank, and he built other tanks at Dhārā and elsewhere. Storing water against the dry season was already Paramāra practice on a vast scale. Keep it brief and comparative. The breach dispute goes in a note; popular accounts are attributed.
   - E: C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-003, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-GANGULY-001. C: C02-KB-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-002, C02-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-003. Note: C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-005, C02-KB-JAGTA-EN-001. X: C02-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-004, C02-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-005.
2. **P2 — What the treatise asks of a site.**
   - **Point:** The treatise credited to Bhoja (an attribution doubted: he may have been a general editor at most, per the note) classes dry country as *jāṅgala* and asks for stable sweet water, tanks and stepwells. These are norms, not a record of why Udaypur was chosen. A place without a perennial river could meet them only if its water was made.
   - E: C02-BHOJDEV-HI-002, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-004, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-005. Note: C02-KB-SAMAR-1-001, C02-KB-RAJABHOJ-EN-002.
3. **P3 — Udaypur's made water.**
   - **Point:** At landscape level, set out the made water:
     - the tank below;
     - the inhabited mound and temple above;
     - kunds on the hilltop;
     - stepwells and baolis filled in the rains for year-round use;
     - the Bhujariya Talab, older than the Udaysagar, beside a live quarry.

     The water was sorted by use: tank water for fields and washing, groundwater for drinking. Several structures are undated, so the point is the pattern, not its first date. Detail is left to Chapter 7.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-028, C02-GEOHER-029, C02-KB-GEOHER-005, C02-KB-GEOHER-001. C: C02-KB-GEOHER-002, C02-KB-GEOHER-003, C02-KB-INTACH-001, C02-KB-GAZ-012, C02-KB-GEOHER-007, C02-KB-GEOHER-017, C02-KB-GEOHER-018, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-003. X: C02-KB-GEOHER-019, C02-KB-GEOHER-020, C02-KB-JAGTA-EN-002.

**Transition:** The water was made; the town that held it was cut from its own hill.

## 2.5 A town of its own stone *(Stone, then and now · 500 w)*

**Section statement:** By the end, the reader sees the continuity of stone. The temple, the dry-masonry houses, the shops built without cement and today's quarries all come from the hill, and the quarrying that made the town now presses on the land around it. The chapter closes on the setting a founder found: what it allowed, and what it did not decide. Others had used this country long before (Chapter 3).

**Purpose:** Stone from the founding to today's quarries; the setting, not the cause.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Built of its own hill.**
   - **Point:** From the temple to dry-masonry houses, cement-less shops and today's quarries, much of them illegal on forest land, Udaypur has been built from its own stone. The stone that made the town is now the pressure on its land. People act only where items describe them (a ground report on illegal quarrying; the stone cut and sent abroad); no invented builders or quarrymen.
   - E: C02-KB-GEOHER-006, C02-JAGTA-EN-001, C02-JAGTA-EN-002. C: C02-JAGTA-HI-004, C02-JAGTA-EN-003, C02-PANDE-001.
2. **P2 — The setting a founder found.**
   - **Point:** Close the chapter on what the land made possible (stone at hand, rain to be held, black soil for wheat) and what it did not decide: who came, when, and why. Lead into Chapter 3 by content: this country was used long before Udaypur had a name. No new facts; recall cited ones briefly.
   - E: C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-GEOHER-027.

**Transition:** Chapter 3 turns to the people who used this country before Udaypur.
