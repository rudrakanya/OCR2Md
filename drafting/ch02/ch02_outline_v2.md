# Chapter 2 — The Betwa Valley: Geography, Geology and Natural Environment — outline v2 (authored history)

**Thesis:** see `ch02_interpretive_brief.md`. Udaypur stands on a seam where the trap has worn away from Vindhyan sandstone, on a tableland with no perennial river and wells that generally fail in summer. From its founding it has depended on stored water and stone cut from its own hill.

## 2.1 The Vetravatī: a river that does not reach the town *(Hydrology · 800 w)*

**Purpose:** Show that Udaypur belongs to the Betwa's country but not to its banks, and that neither the river nor groundwater could supply it.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Opening scene: the dry hill above the valley.**
   - **Point** *(comparison)*: The river that gives the Paramāra inscriptions their geography, the Vētravatī flowing by Vidisha with the ancient city in its fork, does not come to Udaypur, where the surveyed landscape has no rivers: the valley's namesake served the great city, not this village.
   - Role: carry the thesis from the first line. The river named in the inscriptions, and the town it does not reach.
   - The Vētravatī of the records; Vidisha in the fork of two rivers; "rivers in this landscape are absent" at Udaypur.

   (C02-CII-001, C02-CII-002, C02-GAZ-001, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001, C02-GEOHER-001)
2. **P2 — The river's course and Udaypur's margin.**
   - **Point** *(tension)*: Even with a Betwa tributary, the Kewtan, about a kilometre from the village, the survey counts Udaypur's as a landscape without rivers, so what mattered here was not a stream's distance but whether it could be relied on.
   - Role: place the town in the drainage.
   - Rise in Raisen; 590 km to the Yamuna; the district's south-to-north slope; tributaries; the Kewtan 1.8 km from the hill and 1 km from the village.

   (C02-GAZ-001, C02-BETWA-CLIM-001, C02-GAZ-002, C02-GAZ-003, C02-KB-GAZ-001, C02-GAZ-005, C02-GEOHER-002)
3. **P3 — A seasonal river.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Because the upper Betwa runs mainly in the monsoon months (intermittent until its last 85 km, rising in June and falling after September), in its present regime the river near its source cannot carry a settlement through the dry season; that water has to come from the ground or from storage.
   - Role: claim 2.
   - Intermittent above and perennial only in the last 85 km; flow from June to September; 80 per cent of runoff in the monsoon; water bodies 2 per cent of the upper catchment.

   (C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001, C02-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-BETWA-FLOW-003, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-014)
4. **P4 — Wells that fail in summer.**
   - **Point** *(tension)*: The sandstone around Udaypur that gives it building stone gives it wells that generally dry up in summer, with water more than 15 m down, while only Vidisha draws a piped supply from the Betwa: the same rock supplies the stone and withholds dependable groundwater.
   - Role: claim 3, where both sides of the thesis meet in the same rock.
   - Vindhyan wells dry, water deeper than 15 m; the district's reliance on wells; Vidisha's Betwa-fed scheme as the contrast.

   (C02-KB-GAZ-004, C02-GAZ-043, C02-GAZ-053, C02-KB-GAZ-010, C02-KB-GAZ-011, C02-GEOHER-003)
5. **P5 — The consequence.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Udaypur's water lies not in a river but in the hollows around it, and its streets drain towards the fields beyond, so the village depends on what lies outside its houses: water held in the surrounding depressions from one monsoon to the next, and the farmland that this drainage feeds.
   - Role: close claim 1 and move the argument on. Water had to be held from one monsoon to the next.
   - Lead into the calendar of 2.2.

   (C02-GEOHER-028, C02-GEOHER-001)

**Conflicts:** none to set out. Rival figures go in `{{note}}`s.

**Transition:** What the river would not give, the monsoon gave once a year.

## 2.2 Monsoon and the working year *(Climate · 650 w)*

**Purpose:** Show that the year is an arithmetic of scarcity, and that the farming year is built on stored rain.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Four wet months.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: With about 92 per cent of the rain falling in June–September and the basin's evaporation exceeding its rainfall, water not caught in those four months is largely lost to the air, so the year's supply is decided in one season.
   - Role: claim 1.
   - A dry climate with one monsoon; 92 per cent of rain in June–September; evaporation exceeding rainfall.
   - The 1979 Gazetteer is date-stamped here.

   (C02-GAZ-015, C02-GAZ-016, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-GAZ-014)
2. **P2 — Heat and cold.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: The hottest month, May, comes at the end of the long dry season and just before the rains, so heat and scarcity peak together, at the point where any store must last longest.
   - Role: the stakes of the dry season.
   - May about 40 °C, January cold waves; the upper catchment's 40–45 °C and 2 °C.

   (C02-GAZ-021, C02-BETWA-FLOW-010)
3. **P3 — Bad years in runs.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Because low rainfall has come in runs at nearby Vidisha and Basoda, dependence on stored rain carries a risk the averages hide: a store has to outlast more than one weak monsoon, and the famine of 1899–1900 marks how far a single season can fall short.
   - Role: claim 2.
   - 1905 and 1948; consecutive low years; the famine of 1899–00; rainy days and the 360.2 mm day at Basoda.

   (C02-GAZ-017, C02-GAZ-018, C02-GAZ-019, C02-GAZ-023, C02-GAZ-022)
4. **P4 — The farming calendar.**
   - **Point** *(comparison)*: At Udaypur 90 per cent of the kharif area is rain-fed but only 34 per cent of the rabi, so the winter crop that predominates in the district leans on water held over from the rains: in the black soil, in wells and in tanks.
   - Role: claim 3.
   - *Shialu* and *unhalu*; ploughing and sowing; rabi predominance; Udaypur's 90 against 34 per cent rain-fed.

   (C02-KB-GAZ-007, C02-KB-GAZ-006, C02-KB-GAZ-005, C02-GEOHER-027, C02-KB-GAZ-013, C02-KB-GAZ-014)
5. **P5 — A shifting pattern.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Basin rainfall shows no significant century-long trend, yet minimum temperatures are rising and the upper catchment's flow has fallen since 2000; a place that lives on one season's stored water feels such shifts first, because its reserve is only what that season leaves.
   - Role: claim 4, and the close.
   - Rising minimum temperatures; the upper catchment drying, with the two studies' difference in a `{{note}}`.
   - Lead into the rock on which the rain falls.

   (C02-BETWA-CLIM-007, C02-BETWA-CLIM-011, C02-BETWA-FLOW-006, C02-BETWA-FLOW-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-016, C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-003)

**Conflicts:**
- **District against basin rainfall** (1,229.9 mm vs 1,138 mm), and **the long-term trend:** `{{note}}`s, with the question stated in the text.

**Transition:** Where the rain gathers or runs off is decided by the rock.

## 2.3 The plateau and its rock *(Geology and landform · 950 w)*

**Purpose:** Show that Udaypur sits where the trap has worn away from bedded sandstone, on a decaying tableland whose heights are dry, and where two soils meet.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The tableland.**
   - **Point** *(thesis)*: Udaypur occupies the uneven, north-sloping tableland of the Vindhyan spurs rather than the river valleys that drain it, so its setting is a matter of rock and height before it is one of water.
   - Role: the setting.
   - The Vindhyachal plateau and its spurs; the Betwa valley between two ranges; Udaypur's 1,966 ha at 340–480 m, mean 410 m.

   (C02-GEOHER-005, C02-GAZ-007, C02-GEOHER-006, C02-GAZ-006)
2. **P2 — The seam.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Erosion stripped the trap from this range and left sandstone at the surface around Udaypur, the stone of which the temple was built, 'quarried in the hill close by': the geology that sets the village apart from the district's trap country also put its building stone within reach.
   - Role: claim 1.
   - Deccan trap across most of the district; erosion removing it along the Garhi-Teonda range; sandstone and limestone exposed; Udaypur among the western foothills.

   (C02-GAZ-010, C02-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-009, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-GEOHER-013, C02-PANDE-001)
3. **P3 — A tableland in decay.**
   - **Point** *(cause)*: In the survey's model, the hills that form the region's skyline are stages in the decay of a sandstone tableland, a two-storey mesa wearing down to a classic butte, because hard caprock over weaker cross-bedded horizons breaks back unevenly: Udaypur's landmarks are remnants of a vanished plateau.
   - Role: claim 2.
   - Cross-bedded sandstones and weak horizons; mesa decay; a two-storey mesa; the classic butte; mesa 567.66 m and butte 582.31 m.
   - The 270 m and 100 m rises go in a `{{note}}`.

   (C02-GEOHER-008, C02-GEOHER-009, C02-GEOHER-010, C02-GEOHER-011, C02-GEOHER-015, C02-GEOHER-014, C02-GEOHER-007, C02-GEOHER-020, C02-KB-GEOHER-008, C02-KB-GEOHER-009, C02-KB-GEOHER-011)
4. **P4 — Dry heights and hollow rock.**
   - **Point** *(tension)*: Water made the hill's caves and gullies but does not stay on it: the mesa's valleys are dry and without channels, so the heights could offer stone and shelter, not supply.
   - Role: claim 3.
   - Dry, channel-less valleys on the mesa; natural and man-made caves; gully erosion.

   (C02-GEOHER-010, C02-GEOHER-016, C02-GEOHER-012, C02-GEOHER-004, C02-KB-GEOHER-010)
5. **P5 — Two soils.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Udaypur's ground is three-quarters black cotton soil and a quarter red-yellow soil from sandstone and shale, so the seam between trap and sandstone runs through its fields too, and the moisture-holding black soil, 'very good for wheat', is the farmer's partial answer to the dry year.
   - Role: claim 4, and the close.
   - Alluvium, laterite and trap; black soil "very good for wheat"; Udaypur's 75 and 25 per cent; the catchment's elevations, labelled.

   (C02-GAZ-009, C02-GAZ-040, C02-GAZ-013, C02-GEOHER-027, C02-BETWA-FLOW-007, C02-GAZ-011)

**Conflicts:**
- **The hill's rise above the plain:** in a `{{note}}`. One figure per measured thing in the text.

**Transition:** The thin-soiled heights that resist the plough hold the woods.

## 2.4 Forest, flora and fauna *(Natural environment · 600 w)*

**Purpose:** Show that the forest follows the rock, that the hill's cover bears the village's pressure, and that wildlife gathers at the hollows and waters the land and people made.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Forest on the slopes.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Woodland in the district keeps to the hill ranges and steep slopes, and on sandstone it grows poor and shallow-soiled; the hill that gave Udaypur its stone gave little to the plough, and its cover follows the rock rather than the village's fields.
   - Role: claim 1.
   - Two-fifths of the district under forest, on ranges and slopes; poor mixed forest on sandstone; teak and khair; timber and fuel.
   - The hill fort in teak and grass, in Tiwari's account. Vijay Manohar Tiwari is named in full here if this is his first mention in the chapter; otherwise "Tiwari".

   (C02-GAZ-027, C02-GAZ-012, C02-GAZ-030, C02-GAZ-028, C02-GAZ-037, C02-JAGTA-HI-001, C02-GAZ-038, C02-KB-GEOHER-012)
2. **P2 — The hill's plants.**
   - **Point** *(cause)*: The hill's flora is disturbed by grazing and illegal felling, so its present cover is partly the village's own work, and the district's loss of game follows the same spread of cultivation and clearing.
   - Role: claim 2.
   - Van Tulsi, neem, bel, palash; grazing and illegal felling; the wildlife decline recorded in the district.

   (C02-GEOHER-019, C02-GEOHER-021, C02-GAZ-031, C02-GAZ-032)
3. **P3 — Animals and birds.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Migratory birds winter at Udaypur's water bodies and thousands of bats roost in its caves, so the water bodies around the village and the hollows the rock left are also habitats: the land's held water and its broken hill support a wildlife of their own.
   - Role: claim 3.
   - The 1968 Udaipura block; the district's mammals; 58 bird species, vultures, migratory birds at the water bodies; bats and guano in the caves.

   (C02-GAZ-034, C02-GAZ-035, C02-GEOHER-023, C02-GAZ-033, C02-GEOHER-022, C02-GEOHER-017, C02-KB-GEOHER-013, C02-KB-GEOHER-014, C02-KB-GEOHER-015, C02-KB-GEOHER-016)
4. **P4 — A slow clock.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Lichens that take hundreds of years to grow on the fortification wall make the hill a possible clock for its own building, a natural record that might date the human work it grows on, though it has yet to be studied systematically.
   - Role: claim 4, and the close.
   - Lichens on the fort wall, centuries in growth; their dating potential.
   - Lead into the works people made from this ground.

   (C02-GEOHER-024, C02-GEOHER-025)

**Conflicts:** none.

**Transition:** Of all that this land offered, the town took its stone and made its water.

## 2.5 Soil, stone and site *(Settlement and materials · 1,000 w)*

**Purpose:** Show how the environment shaped the settlement: stored water organised it, and local stone built it, from the temple to today's quarries.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — What a site should have.**
   - **Point** *(tension)*: The treatise's ideal site has stable sweet water, tanks and stepwells, and it classes dry country as jāṅgala; a place without a perennial river could still meet that ideal if its water was made. These are norms, not a record of why Udaypur was chosen.
   - Role: claim 1.
   - *Jāṅgala*, *anūpa* and *sādhāraṇa*; sweet stable water, tanks and stepwells, safety. Norms, attributed to the text.

   (C02-BHOJDEV-HI-002, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-004, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-005)
2. **P2 — Made water.**
   - **Point** *(consequence)*: Tank below, inhabited mound and temple above, kunds on the hilltop and stepwells and baolis filled in the rains for year-round use: Udaypur's water was held in tiers sorted by use (tank water for fields and washing, groundwater for drinking), so the place's layout follows how its water was stored. Several structures are undated, so the point concerns the pattern, not its first date.
   - Role: claim 2.
   - *Talav* and *tekro*, with the temple on the high point; hilltop kunds; the three-sided stepwell; Bhujariya Talab beside a live quarry; baolis still in use.

   (C02-GEOHER-028, C02-GEOHER-029, C02-KB-GEOHER-005, C02-KB-GEOHER-001, C02-KB-GEOHER-002, C02-KB-GEOHER-003, C02-KB-INTACH-001, C02-KB-GAZ-012, C02-KB-GEOHER-007, C02-KB-GEOHER-017, C02-KB-GEOHER-018, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-003, C02-KB-GEOHER-019, C02-KB-GEOHER-020, C02-KB-JAGTA-EN-002)
3. **P3 — The dynasty's lake.**
   - **Point** *(comparison)*: Bhoja's dam on the Betwa near Bhojpur, its lake of about 250 square miles fed in the three dry months from an auxiliary tank, shows that storing water against the dry season was already Paramāra practice on a vast scale; Udaysagar answered the same season at the scale of one town.
   - Role: claim 3.
   - Bhoja's dam on the Betwa near Bhojpur; the diverted Kaliasot; the tank for the dry months; the breach dispute in a `{{note}}`.

   (C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-003, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-KB-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-GANGULY-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-002, C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-005, C02-KB-JAGTA-EN-001)
4. **P4 — Soil and harvest.**
   - **Point** *(comparison)*: The district's richest cultivated land lies where two rivers enclose it, in the doab of the Bah and the Betwa; Udaypur, with no river, shares the black soil but not that advantage, so its harvest depends on the water it holds.
   - Role: claim 4, part 1. The black soil's yield.
   - Quick recuperation; the doab; the trade in gram and wheat.

   (C02-GAZ-045, C02-GAZ-046, C02-GAZ-047, C02-GAZ-052, C02-GEOHER-027, C02-GEOHER-001)
5. **P5 — Stone at hand.**
   - **Point** *(thesis)*: Sandstone is excellent building material and explains the northern region's wealth of monuments; at Udaypur the temple's red sandstone came from the hill close by, so the foundation was built from its own site, and the rock that withholds water supplied the stone.
   - Role: claim 4, part 2.
   - Vindhyan sandstone as building material; early buildings at Vidisha and Gyaraspur; ready sandstone and the region's wealth of monuments; laterite once tapped as iron ore.
   - The temple's red sandstone "quarried in the hill close by".

   (C02-GAZ-042, C02-GAZ-050, C02-MADHYABHARAT-003, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-PANDE-001, C02-GAZ-044)
6. **P6 — A town of its own stone.**
   - **Point** *(tension)*: From the temple to dry-masonry houses, cement-less shops and today's quarries, Udaypur has been built from its own stone, and the quarrying that made it now runs, much of it illegally, on the forest land around it: the stone that made the town is now the pressure on its land.
   - Role: the close.
   - Dry-masonry houses; shops without cement; quarries then and now, including the illegal ones.
   - The final paragraph turns to who used this country before Udaypur (Chapter 3).

   (C02-KB-GEOHER-006, C02-JAGTA-EN-001, C02-JAGTA-EN-002, C02-JAGTA-HI-004, C02-JAGTA-EN-003)

**Conflicts:**
- **The Bhojpur breach:** in a `{{note}}`.

**Transition:** Chapter 3 turns to the landscape before Udaypur.
