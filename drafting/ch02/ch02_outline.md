## 2.1 The Vetravatī *(Gazetteer + epigraphy · 900 w)*

**Purpose:** Introduce the river that names the valley. Cover its old name in the records, its rise in Raisen, and its south-to-north course past Vidisha to the Yamuna. Then the tributaries of Vidisha district, and the Kewtan, the stream nearest Udaypur.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — The name in the records.**
   - The Paramāra inscriptions name the Vētravatī, which their editor identifies with the Betwa "flowing by Vidishā" and by the city of Bhilsā.
   - The Gazetteer calls the Betwa or Betrawati "a river of great antiquity".
   - Ancient Vidisha (Besnagar, the Vessanagar of the Pali books) stood in the fork of the Betwa and the Bes.

   (C02-CII-001, C02-CII-002, C02-GAZ-001, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001)
2. **P2 — Rise and length.**
   - The river rises from the main Vindhyan range in the extreme south-west of Raisen district and joins the Yamuna in Hamirpur district.
   - It runs 590 km in all, 232 km of them in Madhya Pradesh and 358 km in Uttar Pradesh. The basin covers about 43,500 km².
   - The Gazetteer ranks it the third largest river of Malwa; attribute the ranking.

   (C02-GAZ-001, C02-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-BETWA-CLIM-001)
3. **P3 — Through Vidisha district.**
   - The whole district lies in the Yamuna basin and slopes from south to north. The Betwa basin proper lies between two sub-water divides.
   - After 96 km the river enters the district 6 km south of Vidisha. It leaves after about 112 km and then forms district boundaries.
   - The Bes joins it 3 km north of Vidisha. Besnagar lies along this river.

   (C02-GAZ-002, C02-GAZ-001, C02-GAZ-003, C02-KB-GAZ-002)
4. **P4 — Tributaries.**
   - The left-bank and right-bank tributaries. Name only those that matter here; do not list them all.
   - The Bina and Nion rise in Raisen on either side of the Garhi-Teonda spur. The Kethan is the last significant left-bank tributary and is tapped for an irrigation tank.
   - The Dhasan is "a very significant stream of Bundelkhand region": the valley drains towards Bundelkhand as well as Malwa.

   (C02-GAZ-003, C02-KB-GAZ-001, C02-GAZ-005, C02-GAZ-004)
5. **P5 — The Kewtan.**
   - In Geo-Heritage the Kewtan, a Betwa tributary, flows south to north and joins the Betwa at the Kotha Barrage. It runs 1.8 km from the site of Shiva Pindi and 1 km from the village settlement.
   - The Gazetteer describes a Keotan rising on Mahuakhera hill in the Eastern (Teonda) range and joining the Betwa on its right bank, its whole length inside the district.
   - Give each description under its own source. Do not assert that the two names are one river.

   (C02-GEOHER-002, C02-GAZ-005)
6. **P6 — A river for the town of Vidisha.**
   - The modern contrast: Vidisha's water-supply scheme draws on the Betwa, while elsewhere in the district people use wells.
   - In Madhya Bharat the north and north-east follow the Betwa's course.
   - Close on the distance between the river that names the valley and Udaypur's own ground.

   (C02-GAZ-053, C02-MADHYABHARAT-001)

**Conflicts:** none to set out. Keep each source's figures under its own name.

**Transition:** The Betwa names the valley and gives Vidisha its water, but no river runs through Udaypur. How Udaypur's water was found follows in 2.2.

## 2.2 An intermittent river *(Survey + hydrology · 1,150 w)*

**Purpose:** Establish the premise. Udaypur lies in the Betwa basin but has no river of its own. The upper Betwa is largely intermittent and perennial only in its lower 85 km. Stored water was therefore a necessity, not ornament: tanks, wells, stepwells and kunds. The river's best-known medieval intervention, Bhoja's dam at Bhojpur, frames the point regionally.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A basin that does not water the town.**
   - Geo-Heritage: "rivers in this landscape are absent". The region depends on surface water bodies, lakes and ponds formed in natural depressions.
   - Water attained "paramount importance" in semi-arid Central India.
   - Note in one clause that the Kewtan passes 1 km from the village.

   (C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GEOHER-028, C02-GEOHER-002)
2. **P2 — Intermittent above, perennial below.**
   - The upstream Betwa is largely intermittent and becomes perennial only in the downstream 85 km before the Yamuna.
   - The upper Betwa runs as a single-thread channel of 127 km, with a 9,322 km² catchment, in semi-arid to dry sub-humid country.
   - The hydrograph rises in June and falls after September. Nearly 80 per cent of the basin's runoff comes in the monsoon.

   (C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001, C02-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-BETWA-FLOW-003, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004)
3. **P3 — Water underground.**
   - Groundwater in the Vindhyan rocks is "mostly far from being satisfactory": wells dry up in summer and the water level lies deeper than 15 m.
   - In the trap country the supply is "somewhat problematical".
   - Most places outside Vidisha depend on wells.

   (C02-KB-GAZ-004, C02-GAZ-043, C02-GAZ-053)
4. **P4 — Talav and tekro.**
   - Settlement follows a pattern: a talav in a depression and an inhabited mound (tekro), with a temple at the highest point and streets draining towards the fields.
   - Drinking water came from dug wells and stepwells set between the settlement and the talav.
   - Baolis caught monsoon water for use through the year.
   - Stepwells marked the hidden aquifer and drew settlement around them.

   (C02-GEOHER-028, C02-KB-GEOHER-002, C02-KB-GEOHER-003, C02-GEOHER-029)
5. **P5 — Udaypur's waters today.**
   - Bhujariya Talab is a natural water body of 8.04 acres south-east of the village, near a live stone quarry.
   - Residents' formula: "chappan baoli, bavan kuae and saadhe baarah talab". Udaysagar, the lake the praśasti credits to Udayāditya, gets one clause only.
   - The INTACH inventory lists named baolis assigned to the eleventh century and still in use.

   (C02-KB-GEOHER-001, C02-KB-INTACH-001)
6. **P6 — Bhoja's dam on the Betwa.**
   - Bhoja dammed the Betwa near Bhojpur, and the lake is said to have spread over 250 square miles.
   - The Kaliasot was turned into the Betwa valley. A storage tank fed the lake through the dry months.
   - The lake held the water so that "the river Betwa was never in floods". Rajpurohit adds that Bhoja then founded a new town on lower ground near Vidisha.
   - Irrigation: one source says the lake irrigated; another says no information affirms its irrigational capacity. Set both down.

   (C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-003, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-002, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-KB-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-004, C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-004, C02-GANGULY-001)
7. **P7 — Who broke the dam.**
   - **Hoshang Shah:** Rajpurohit, the Hindi *Bhojadeva*, and Tiwari. Name Vijay Manohar Tiwari in full here, at his first mention in the chapter.
   - **Shah Hussain of Mandu, "the greatest of the Mandu Sultans":** *Bhoja Paramara and His Times* and Ganguly's quotation of Kincaid. Hussain coveted the fertile land of the lake bed. In this account one dam was breached and the other still turns the Kaliasot into the Betwa.
   - Close on what the breach exchanged: stored water for cultivable silt.

   (C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-001, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-001, C02-KB-JAGTA-EN-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-005, C02-KB-GANGULY-001)

**Conflicts:**
- **The Bhojpur breach:** Hoshang Shah (Rajpurohit; *Bhojadeva*; Tiwari) vs Shah Hussain (*Bhoja Paramara and His Times*; Kincaid via Ganguly). Present both; resolve neither.
- **Irrigation from the lake:** affirmed by Rajpurohit and *Bhojadeva*; doubted by *Bhoja Paramara and His Times*.
- **"Rivers … absent" vs the Kewtan 1 km from the village:** both are Geo-Heritage statements. Set them side by side without inference.

**Transition:** Every tank, well and lake described here fills in one season. The monsoon calendar follows in 2.3.

## 2.3 Monsoon and the working year *(Gazetteer, the 1979 compilation · 1,100 w)*

**Purpose:** The climate and the agricultural year, taken from the Gazetteer (date-stamped) and the basin studies.
- The four seasons, the concentration of rain, its variability, and the kharif/rabi cycle.
- Village-level crops at Udaypur.
- Long-term change as measured, with the two studies' differing findings set out fairly.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — A dry country with one wet season.**
   - The Gazetteer finds the climate "generally dry except during the south-west monsoon season". Nights are pleasant even in summer, which justified the Mughals' praise of Shab-i-Malwa.
   - The summer is less intense, and the winter less cold, than elsewhere in North India.
   - The Betwa basin is "moderate, mostly dry except during the southwest monsoons".
   - Date the Gazetteer once, as the 1979 compilation.

   (C02-GAZ-015, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004)
2. **P2 — Four seasons.**
   - The cold season runs December–February and the hot season to mid-June. The south-west monsoon lasts from mid-June to about the end of September; October–November is the retreating monsoon.
   - May is the hottest month (mean maximum about 40 °C, sometimes above 44 °C) and January the coldest (about 25 °C/10 °C), with cold waves and occasional frost.
   - The upper Betwa catchment reaches 40–45 °C in summer and falls to 2 °C in winter.

   (C02-GAZ-014, C02-GAZ-021, C02-BETWA-FLOW-010)
3. **P3 — How much rain, and when.**
   - The district averages 1,229.9 mm, rising from north-west to south-east. About 92 per cent falls June–September, and July is the rainiest month.
   - The basin study gives 700–1,200 mm, averaging 1,138 mm.
   - Rainy days number 47 at Basoda. The heaviest fall in 24 hours was 360.2 mm at Basoda on 31 July 1951.
   - Monsoon depressions arrive from the Bay of Bengal.

   (C02-GAZ-016, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-GAZ-019, C02-GAZ-022)
4. **P4 — The uncertain year.**
   - In 1901–50 the wettest year brought 149 per cent of normal (1948) and the driest 50 per cent (1905).
   - Low rainfall came in consecutive years at Vidisha and Basoda.
   - The famine of 1899–1900: about 9 inches of rain instead of over 30.

   (C02-GAZ-017, C02-GAZ-018, C02-GAZ-023)
5. **P5 — Kharif and rabi.**
   - Two seasons: kharif (*shialu*, autumn crops) and rabi (*unhalu*, spring crops).
   - The bakhar breaks the fields, and ploughing for the autumn crops begins about the end of May. Sowing follows once the rain has moistened the soil, and spring crops are sown till October. Mixed sowings are common.
   - The district is predominantly rabi (65:35 in 1971–72), and wheat is the most important crop.
   - Udaypur village grows soybean, pigeon pea and maize in kharif and wheat, gram and lentil in rabi. The kharif area is 90 per cent rain-fed; the rabi area is 34 per cent rain-fed.

   (C02-KB-GAZ-007, C02-KB-GAZ-006, C02-KB-GAZ-005, C02-GAZ-051, C02-GEOHER-027)
6. **P6 — A changing pattern.**
   - **1901–2001 study:** the basin-average statistics show no significant trend in seasonal or yearly rainfall. Winter and summer minimum temperatures rise significantly. A delayed monsoon onset at Tikamgarh and Shivpuri could affect kharif planning.
   - **Upper Betwa study:** finds "a transition from wetter to drier hydro-climatic conditions". Mean streamflow fell by 16 per cent in 2001–2020 against 1982–2000. Agriculture there is primarily rain-fed.
   - Different periods and areas: set the findings side by side.

   (C02-BETWA-CLIM-007, C02-BETWA-CLIM-011, C02-BETWA-CLIM-020, C02-BETWA-FLOW-006, C02-BETWA-FLOW-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-016)

**Conflicts:**
- **Average rainfall:** 1,229.9 mm (district, Gazetteer) vs 1,138 mm (basin study). These cover different areas and periods; explain, do not choose.
- **Long-term rainfall:** no significant basin trend (1901–2001 study) vs wetter-to-drier in the upper catchment (later study).

**Rule:** no claims about the timing of building or festivals unless an item states it.

**Transition:** The rain falls on a tableland, and the tableland's shape decides where the water gathers and where people live. The plateau follows in 2.4.

## 2.4 The plateau *(Survey + Gazetteer · 1,000 w)*

**Purpose:** Profile the land: the Vindhyan tableland (Udaypur at 340–480 m, mean 410 m), the ranges and valleys of Vidisha district, the trap and sandstone terrains with their soils, and the position between Malwa proper and the country to the east that makes this a frontier. No hill heights and no sandstone petrology (Chapter 3).

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — Udaypur on the tableland.**
   - Udaypur lies "amidst the eastern part of the Vindhyanchal range": 1,966 ha, with elevation between 340 and 480 m above MSL and an average of 410 m.
   - The tableland owes its existence to "flat-lying sedimentary rocks". Use the first sentence of C02-GEOHER-009 only.

   (C02-GEOHER-005, C02-GEOHER-009)
2. **P2 — Spurs and valleys.**
   - Vidisha lies on the Vindhyachal plateau, whose spurs run north and north-east. The plateau slopes north and is drained by rivers of Malwa and Bundelkhand, which cut valleys between the spurs.
   - The Betwa valley is bordered by the Garhi-Teonda range to the east and the Ganari-Raghoghar range to the west.
   - Undulating topography in an agricultural district.

   (C02-GAZ-007, C02-GEOHER-006)
3. **P3 — The eastern range.**
   - The Garhi-Teonda range runs between the Betwa and Bina valleys and is the district's most extensive and most prominent range; give its highest peak.
   - Here erosion has removed the trap cover, leaving sandstone and limestone.
   - Gyaraspur and Udaipur stand among the western foothills, Basoda and Pathari near the eastern slopes.

   (C02-KB-GAZ-008, C02-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-009)
4. **P4 — Trap country and black soil.**
   - The Deccan (Malwa) Trap is the most widespread formation: horizontal lava flows give "a terraced appearance".
   - The valleys hold rich black soil from basalt silt, and the tract is one of Malwa's major wheat-growing tracts.
   - Udaypur itself: 75 per cent black cotton soil, 25 per cent red-yellow soils derived from sandstone and shale.
   - The upper Betwa catchment ranges from 376 to 669 m, higher in the west.

   (C02-GAZ-010, C02-GAZ-040, C02-GEOHER-027, C02-BETWA-FLOW-007)
5. **P5 — Between Malwa and the east.**
   - Patil's "North-Eastern plateau": a narrow out-stretch of the central plateau of Malwa, encircled by wooded Vindhyan ranges, with fertile Malwa proper to the west and the Gangetic plains near to the east. Attribute this; do not adopt its "second to none".
   - The district's rivers drain to both Malwa and Bundelkhand.

   (C02-MADHYABHARAT-002, C02-GAZ-007, C02-GAZ-003)
6. **P6 — A frontier place.**
   - Tiwari's view, attributed: Udaypur became an important centre on the border of the two dynasties because it had sandstone quarries.
   - Access as Patil recorded it: 4 miles by metalled road east of Bareth station; about 8 miles by fair-weather road from Basoda.
   - Close on stone and site.

   (C02-JAGTA-HI-002, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-002)

**Conflicts:** none to set out. The committed elevation (340–480 m, mean 410 m) is the only figure for Udaypur's ground. The catchment range (376–669 m) is a different area; label it as such.

**Transition:** A tableland of black soil and bedded sandstone, short of running water, sets the terms on which a town could be sited. Those terms follow in 2.5.

## 2.5 Soil, stone and site *(Survey · 850 w)*

**Purpose:** How the environment shaped settlement:
- the water-and-height logic of the site;
- soils for wheat and gram;
- building stone at hand.

Hand on to Chapter 3, which turns from water to rock.

**Paragraph plan:**
1. **P1 — What the treatises asked of a site.**
   - The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, as summarised in the Hindi *Bhojadeva*, distinguishes three kinds of country: *jāṅgala* (dry), *anūpa* (watery) and *sādhāraṇa* (middling).
   - It praises land with fresh, stable water, tanks and stepwells; land with cool, sweet water that raiders cannot easily reach suits the founding of villages and towns.
   - These are norms, attributed to the text, not descriptions of Udaypur. Paraphrase only. Never quote ⚠ items.

   (C02-BHOJDEV-HI-002, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-004, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-005)
2. **P2 — Water on the hill.**
   - Kunds on the hilltop sustained those living inside the fortress wall.
   - A three-sided stepwell on the western slope of Pindi Hill held water for the hill's users.
   - The 1,400 m wall encircling the hillock: one clause only (Chapter 11).

   (C02-GEOHER-029, C02-KB-GEOHER-005, C02-KB-GEOHER-004)
3. **P3 — Soil and harvest.**
   - Black cotton soil, *mar* in the local dialect, is "very good for wheat", though kans grass reduces its value.
   - The district has "considerable power of quick recuperation".
   - The Gazetteer attributes the Vidisha cultivator's comparative richness to rich soil, in a tract sparsely cultivated not long before.
   - Vidisha is a trading centre for quality gram and wheat.

   (C02-GAZ-013, C02-GAZ-045, C02-GAZ-046, C02-GAZ-052)
4. **P4 — Stone at hand.**
   - The upper Vindhyan sandstones are "excellent building material" and extensively quarried, mostly Kaimur or Rewa sandstone.
   - Buildings at Vidisha and Gyaraspur testify to the use of fine sandstone "from an early period".
   - Patil explains northern Madhya Bharat's wealth of monuments by "the ready availability of the sandstone from its hills"; attribute this.
   - The terrain around Udaypur is sandstone, widely quarried. Cunningham, quoted by Pande: the temple is "built entirely of a hard fine-grained red sandstone, which is quarried in the hill close by".

   (C02-GAZ-042, C02-GAZ-050, C02-MADHYABHARAT-003, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-PANDE-001)
5. **P5 — A town of its own stone.**
   - Dry-masonry dwellings built of locally sourced material.
   - Tiwari's observations, attributed: shops of Udaypur's own stone with next to no cement or brick, and quarries that supplied building material in abundance.
   - Udaypur and Patari are made rich by quarries, and the stone is exported. Govind Saxena's ground report on illegal quarrying on forest land is attributed.
   - Close by handing on to the rock itself.

   (C02-KB-GEOHER-006, C02-JAGTA-EN-001, C02-JAGTA-EN-002, C02-JAGTA-HI-004, C02-JAGTA-EN-003)

**Conflicts:** none to set out.

**Transition:** The stone that built the village and the temple was cut from the same hill. Chapter 3 turns from water to that rock.
