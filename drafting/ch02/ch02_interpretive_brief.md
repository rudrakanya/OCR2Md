# Chapter 2 — The Betwa Valley: Geography, Geology and Natural Environment — interpretive brief (revised)

**Rule applied throughout:** no claim is stronger than its weakest supporting item. Where the evidence is tentative, the argument says so through its framing, not through hedges in the prose.

## Thesis

Udaypur stands on a seam in the land, at the foot of a range where erosion has stripped the Deccan trap from Vindhyan sandstone. It sits on a tableland in the Betwa's basin with no perennial river: the nearest stream, the Kewtan, a seasonal tributary, passes about a kilometre from the village (C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GEOHER-002, C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001, C02-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-009). The land offered black soil for wheat and bedded stone for building, but no water that lasts through the dry months: wells in its rocks generally dry up in summer (C02-GEOHER-027, C02-GAZ-042, C02-KB-GAZ-004). From its founding the town has depended on stored water and cut stone. The foundation itself included a tank, and the temple was built of red sandstone quarried in the hill close by (C02-KB-GEOHER-001, C02-PANDE-001). Its tanks, its quarried hill and its temple are the record of both.

*Why this can be disputed:* the river and its roads, or the fertility of the black soil, could be argued to matter more. The chapter must show, from the hydrology, the geology and the built works, that storage and stone are what distinguish this site from the valley floor.

*Wording checked against the evidence:*
- "No river crosses" → **no perennial river**: the Kewtan is a Betwa tributary 1 km from the village, and the upper Betwa is "largely intermittent".
- "No running water" → **no water that lasts through the dry months**: wells in the Vindhyan rocks "generally dry up in summer".
- "Always" → **from its founding**: the tank built at the foundation, and the temple's locally quarried stone.
- Today's dependence is shown by the stepwells still in use and the 90 / 34 per cent rain-fed shares.

*Not adopted:* the suggested claim that the Betwa explains a cycle of settlement, abandonment and resettlement. The Chapter 2 evidence documents no abandonment at Udaypur. Besnagar's desertion belongs to Chapter 3.

## 2.1 The Vetravatī: a river that does not reach the town

**Claims:**
1. **Udaypur belongs to the Betwa's country but not to its banks: no perennial river reaches it.** (C02-CII-001, C02-CII-002, C02-GAZ-001, C02-KB-MADHYABHARAT-001, C02-GAZ-002, C02-GEOHER-001, C02-GEOHER-002)
   - Evidence:
     - the Vētravatī is named in the Paramāra inscriptions as the river of Vidisha;
     - ancient Vidisha stood in the fork of the Betwa and the Bes;
     - the whole district drains to the Yamuna;
     - at Udaypur, "rivers in this landscape are absent", and the nearest stream, the Kewtan, runs 1.8 km from the hill and 1 km from the village.
   - Reasoning: the river that named the valley and served its great city leaves Udaypur beside a seasonal tributary, 1 km from the village.
2. **The upper Betwa could not be relied on.** (C02-KB-BETWA-FLOW-001, C02-BETWA-FLOW-002, C02-BETWA-FLOW-003, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-014)
   - Evidence: the upper course is largely intermittent and perennial only in its last 85 km. Its flow rises in June and falls after September. About 80 per cent of the basin's runoff comes in the monsoon. Water bodies cover 2 per cent of the upper catchment.
   - Reasoning: in its upper course the river runs mainly for a season. A settlement on this plateau needed water held from one monsoon to the next. The streamflow figures are modern (1982–2020), so the claim concerns the river's present regime.
3. **Groundwater does not close the dry-season gap.** (C02-KB-GAZ-004, C02-GAZ-043, C02-GAZ-053)
   - Evidence: wells in the Vindhyan rocks dry up in summer, with water deeper than 15 m. Outside Vidisha's Betwa-fed scheme, the district relies on wells.
   - Reasoning: the Vindhyan rock that gives Udaypur its stone gives it wells that generally fail in summer. The two sides of the thesis meet in the same rock.

**Significance:** this establishes the absence that the rest of the chapter answers.

**Connections:** Chapter 3 (Vidisha at the fork of the rivers), Chapter 7 (Udaypur's tanks and stepwells), Chapter 8 (agriculture).

## 2.2 Monsoon and the working year

**Claims:**
1. **The year is an arithmetic of scarcity.** (C02-GAZ-015, C02-GAZ-016, C02-BETWA-CLIM-004, C02-GAZ-014, C02-GAZ-021, C02-BETWA-FLOW-010)
   - Evidence: the climate is "generally dry except during the south-west monsoon season". About 92 per cent of the district's rain falls in June–September. Evaporation in the basin (about 1,830 mm) exceeds its rainfall (1,138 mm on average). May reaches about 40 °C.
   - Reasoning: in the basin's annual balance, evaporation exceeds rainfall. Water not caught in the four wet months is largely lost to the air.
2. **Bad years can come in runs.** (C02-GAZ-017, C02-GAZ-018, C02-GAZ-019, C02-GAZ-023)
   - Evidence: rainfall ranged from 50 to 149 per cent of normal (1905, 1948), with consecutive dry years at Vidisha and Basoda and a famine of 1899–1900 with about 9 inches of rain against over 30.
   - Reasoning: the Gazetteer records consecutive low-rainfall years at Vidisha and Basoda. A store has to carry over more than one season. Nothing records how Udaypur's tanks were sized, so the tanks' size is not interpreted.
3. **The farming year is built on the rains.** (C02-KB-GAZ-007, C02-KB-GAZ-006, C02-KB-GAZ-005, C02-GEOHER-027)
   - Evidence: kharif (*shialu*) and rabi (*unhalu*); ploughing at the end of May and sowing once rain has wetted the soil. At Udaypur the kharif area is 90 per cent rain-fed and the rabi area 34 per cent.
   - Reasoning: about two-thirds of the rabi area does not rely on rain alone. Water held in the moisture-retaining black soil, and water drawn from wells and tanks, carries the winter crop. The figures are modern.
4. **The pattern is shifting at the margins.** (C02-BETWA-CLIM-007, C02-BETWA-CLIM-011, C02-BETWA-FLOW-006, C02-BETWA-FLOW-004, C02-BETWA-FLOW-016)
   - Evidence: there is no significant trend in basin rainfall over 1901–2001, but rising minimum temperatures. In the upper catchment, "a transition from wetter to drier" conditions, with monsoon flow 16 per cent lower in 2001–2020.
   - Reasoning: the same dependence makes the place sensitive to small changes. The difference between the two studies goes in a `{{note}}`.

**Significance:** this turns the absence in 2.1 into a calendar, the seasonal rhythm the chapters on agriculture and village life return to.

**Connections:** Chapter 8 (the agrarian economy), Chapter 9 (festivals and the fair), Chapter 12 (the present-day village).

## 2.3 The plateau and its rock

**Claims:**
1. **Udaypur sits where the trap has worn away.** (C02-GEOHER-005, C02-GAZ-007, C02-GEOHER-006, C02-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-008, C02-KB-GAZ-009, C02-GAZ-010)
   - Evidence:
     - the district is mostly Deccan trap, with terraced lava flows;
     - along the Garhi-Teonda range "the trap cover has been removed by the erosion", exposing sandstone and limestone;
     - Udaypur lies among this range's western foothills at 340–480 m.
   - Reasoning: Udaypur stands where the district's trap gives way to exposed sandstone. That geology came before any political choice, though nothing records whether it shaped that choice.
2. **The hill is a remnant of a vanished tableland.** (C02-GEOHER-008, C02-GEOHER-009, C02-GEOHER-010, C02-GEOHER-011, C02-GEOHER-015, C02-GEOHER-014, C02-GEOHER-007)
   - Evidence:
     - the Udaypur Sandstone Tableland is a test case of mesa decay;
     - its cross-bedded sandstones are separated by weak horizons;
     - the mesa is two-storeyed;
     - Udaypur Hill is a "classic butte", and the next stage is a pinnacle;
     - the mesa top is at 567.66 m and the butte at 582.31 m.
   - Reasoning: the survey reads the hills that make Udaypur visible from far off as stages in the decay of a sandstone tableland: a mesa wearing down to a butte. The reading is the survey's model and is presented as such.
3. **The rock is dry on top and hollow inside.** (C02-GEOHER-010, C02-GEOHER-016, C02-GEOHER-017, C02-GEOHER-012)
   - Evidence: valleys on the mesa "are invariably dry and lack channels"; there are natural and man-made caves; gully erosion has carved a pathway.
   - Reasoning: the mesa's valleys hold no channels, which repeats on a small scale the absence in 2.1. The heights offered stone and elevation rather than water.
4. **Two soils meet at the village.** (C02-GAZ-009, C02-GAZ-040, C02-GEOHER-027, C02-GAZ-013)
   - Evidence: there is black soil from basalt silt in the valleys, "very good for wheat", with "extraordinary power of retaining moisture". Udaypur is 75 per cent black cotton soil and 25 per cent red-yellow soil from sandstone and shale.
   - Reasoning: the seam of claim 1 runs through the fields too. The moisture-holding black soil is the farmer's partial answer to the dry year.
5. The village's hill rises "almost 270 m" from the village in one account and "some 100 m above the surrounding flatland" in another. This goes in a `{{note}}`; the text uses the committed heights above sea level.

**Significance:** this gives the thesis its ground, the material reason why stone and storage, not the river, define the place.

**Connections:** Chapter 4 (why a fort and town on this hill), Chapter 5 (the temple's sandstone), Chapter 7 (the fort on the butte).

## 2.4 Forest, flora and fauna

**Claims:**
1. **The district's forests lie mostly on the slopes the plough avoids.** (C02-GAZ-027, C02-GAZ-012, C02-GAZ-030, C02-GAZ-028, C02-JAGTA-HI-001, C02-GAZ-037)
   - Evidence: forests cover about two-fifths of the district and occupy hill ranges and steep slopes. Mixed forests on Vindhyan sandstone are poor and shallow-soiled. A hill fort near Udaypur stands in teak forest and monsoon grass. Most forest produce is timber and fuel.
   - Reasoning: forests occupy the hill ranges and steep slopes, and on sandstone they grow on shallow sandy loam. The forest pattern follows the relief and rock of 2.3.
2. **The hill's natural cover is under human pressure.** (C02-GEOHER-019, C02-GEOHER-021, C02-GAZ-031)
   - Evidence: Van Tulsi, neem, bel, palash, tamarind on the hill; "intensive human intervention by way of grazing and illegal tree cutting"; game reduced by cultivation, forest clearance and poaching.
   - Reasoning: the survey attributes the hill's disturbed flora to grazing and felling, so its present cover is partly the village's doing.
3. **Some of the hill's wildlife depends on the places people made and the rock left hollow.** (C02-GAZ-034, C02-GAZ-035, C02-GEOHER-023, C02-GEOHER-022, C02-GEOHER-017, C02-GAZ-033)
   - Evidence: in 1968 the Udaipura shooting block recorded 3 tigers and 10 sambhar. Udaypur hosts over 58 bird species, including vultures; migratory birds such as the Sarus Crane visit "its water bodies"; thousands of bats roost in the caves on 500–600 mm of guano.
   - Reasoning: migratory birds come to Udaypur's water bodies and bats roost in its caves, so the tanks of 2.5 and the caves of 2.3 are also habitats.
4. **The hill keeps a slow clock.** (C02-GEOHER-024, C02-GEOHER-025)
   - Evidence: lichens on the fortification wall take "hundreds of years" to grow and could help date the structures.
   - Reasoning: this is one of the few natural records that might measure the age of human building here. It is noted as a possibility, not a result.

**Significance:** this widens the thesis from water and stone to the living landscape that grew around them.

**Connections:** Chapter 7 (the fort wall and lichen dating), Chapter 11 (conservation), Chapter 12 (the village and its hill today).

## 2.5 Soil, stone and site

**Claims:**
1. **Contemporary norms valued exactly what Udaypur had to build.** (C02-BHOJDEV-HI-002, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-004, C02-BHOJDEV-HI-005)
   - Evidence: the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* praises sites with stable sweet water, tanks and stepwells, safe from raiders, and classifies dry *jāṅgala* country.
   - Reasoning: a site without a perennial river could still meet the treatise's ideal, if the water was made. These are norms, not a record of why Udaypur was chosen.
2. **Water was made on the hill and around it.** (C02-GEOHER-028, C02-GEOHER-029, C02-KB-GEOHER-005, C02-KB-GEOHER-001, C02-KB-GEOHER-002, C02-KB-GEOHER-003, C02-KB-INTACH-001, C02-KB-GAZ-012, C02-KB-GEOHER-007, C02-KB-GEOHER-017, C02-KB-GEOHER-018)
   - Evidence:
     - the *talav* and *tekro* pattern, with the temple on the highest point;
     - kunds on the hilltop;
     - a three-sided stepwell on Pindi Hill;
     - Bhujariya Talab (8.04 acres) beside a live quarry;
     - baolis that fill in the rains for year-round use, some recorded as still in use.
     - Udaysagar, built by Udayāditya in 1059, and Bhujariya Talab, which in the survey's reading "existed before his time";
     - Udaipur among the 38 tanks remembered "in olden days" in the district;
     - tank water used for fields and washing but "not considered fit for drinking", which is why the wells and stepwells mattered.
   - Reasoning: the survey reads the settlement's layout as organised by its water: tank, mound, temple and streets draining to the fields. Storage is the answer to 2.1–2.2. Several of the structures are undated, so the claim is about the pattern, not its first date.
3. **The dynasty that founded Udaypur had already dammed the Betwa itself.** (C02-RAJABHOJ-EN-003, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-KB-BHOJA-TIMES-001, C02-GANGULY-001, C02-BHOJA-TIMES-002)
   - Evidence: Bhoja's dam on the Betwa near Bhojpur held a lake whose area is given as about 250 square miles. The Kaliasot was diverted into it, with an auxiliary tank for the dry months.
   - Reasoning: storing water on a large scale was within Paramāra practice before Udayāditya's foundation. Bhoja's reign precedes Udayāditya's, per the canon timeline. Who breached the dam (Hoshang Shah or Shah Hussain) goes in a `{{note}}`.
4. **Stone was the land's other gift, and the town is made of it.** (C02-GAZ-042, C02-GAZ-050, C02-MADHYABHARAT-003, C02-GEOHER-003, C02-PANDE-001, C02-KB-GEOHER-006, C02-JAGTA-EN-001, C02-JAGTA-HI-004, C02-JAGTA-EN-003, C02-GAZ-044)
   - Evidence:
     - upper Vindhyan sandstone is "excellent building material";
     - buildings at Vidisha and Gyaraspur use it "from an early period";
     - the temple is "built entirely of a hard fine-grained red sandstone, which is quarried in the hill close by";
     - dry-masonry houses are built of local stone;
     - quarrying continues, much of it illegal.
   - Reasoning: the sandstone exposed in 2.3 supplied the temple (in Cunningham's description, quoted by Pande), the houses and today's quarries. Cutting stone runs from the founding to the present.

**Significance:** this closes the argument. The town is the sum of stored water and cut stone on a riverless seam, and the closing paragraph turns to who used this country before Udaypur (Chapter 3).

**Connections:** Chapter 3 (the landscape before Udaypur), Chapter 5 (the temple's material), Chapter 7 (the water structures), Chapter 8 (stone and agriculture in the economy), Chapter 12 (quarrying today).

## Questions the chapter raises

- When were Udaypur's first tanks and kunds made? Bhujariya Talab has only a proposed 8th–9th-century date, and the hilltop kunds are undated.
- Was the site chosen for its stone, its height or its soil? No source records the founders' reasons.
- What did the forest on the hill look like in the eleventh century? The evidence describes only its recent, disturbed state.
- Could the lichens on the fort wall date it, as the survey suggests?

## Unsupported claims cut

- That the Betwa caused cycles of settlement and abandonment at Udaypur: no evidence. The idea is kept for Chapter 3's Besnagar.
- That the founders chose the site for defence: plausible, but no item states it. It is phrased only as what the heights allowed.
- That the climate was the same in the eleventh century: no evidence. The chapter describes the modern climate, date-stamped.
