*Rules. The inscriptions are evidence for a history of intellectual life, not an inventory. The praśasti is composition and claim. A few inscriptions carry the argument: the 1080 record, the praśasti, the serpent, the porch archive, the Persian records. Overlap: Chapter 1 (§1.7, §1.10–1.12) told the 1080 readings, the praśasti's finding and contents, the serpent's slab and the survival legends; Chapter 4 (§4.1, §4.9, §4.13, §4.19) used the praśasti for events and set out Records A–C. These are recalled in clauses; Ch 6 reads the texts as texts.*

**Historical question.** What do Udaypur's inscriptions reveal about literate royal culture, the languages of authority, who wrote and commissioned the texts, and the limits of what stone can tell?

**Through-line.** By the end, the reader knows the temple's first dated words and their poet; the faulty long inscription behind the 1059 reading; the nearest dated verse at Amērā; the praśasti as an object, as royal self-portrait, as a genealogy and as a list of conquests, with its tests; the makers of Paramāra praśastis; the serpent of letters at Udaypur and its fellows, and the learning of Dhārā, Bhoja's authorship and the poets of the courts, early and late; how letter forms date the stones; the porch archive, how a grant is read, its records under Caulukya and Paramāra masters, and the last Paramāra traces; Persian on the temple's stones; writing on the hill; and what remains unread.

**Arc.** Three dated texts (6.1–6.3) → the praśasti (6.4–6.8) → the serpent and the learning it displays (6.9–6.14) → script (6.15) → the porch archive (6.16–6.19) → other languages and places (6.20–6.21) → limits (6.22), into Chapter 7.
