## Inscriptions as history, not catalogue

A few inscriptions carry the argument:
- **the 1080 record**, a signed text in which the king appears only in a conventional wish (6.1);
- **the praśasti**, read as an object (6.4), a self-portrait of learned and warlike kings (6.5), a claim to continuity tested by its critics (6.6), and a list of conquests sorted into those other records corroborate and those that rest on the poem alone (6.7);
- **the serpent of letters**, a grammar turned into the king's emblem, with the schoolroom and emblem readings side by side (6.9–6.10);
- **the porch archive**, registering Caulukya and then Paramāra masters through the forms of gift and title (6.16–6.18);
- **the Persian doorways**, the town's second language of record (6.20).

The literary units (6.11–6.14) set out the culture the stones draw on, and each returns to Udaypur.
