# Chapter 7 — An Epigraphic Archive: Eight Centuries of Inscriptions

A great temple is never only a building. It is also a surface — vast acres of cut stone that, the moment a kingdom resolves that this place matters, become a kind of public ledger held open to the sky, and onto which the centuries lean down to write. Kings record their gifts there. Pilgrims scratch their names, hoping, perhaps, to be remembered a little past their own brief breath. Conquerors carve their victories; restorers note their patient repairs. And so, year upon year, the stone keeps gathering writing the way a riverbed gathers silt, slowly, almost tenderly, until the monument is no longer merely a thing seen but a thing *read* — a continuous text composed by hundreds of hands that never once touched one another, in languages that shared no alphabet, professing faiths that warred over the very ground beneath their feet.

The Udayeśvara is such a surface. We have come to it, so far, as architecture and as theology — as the bhūmija masterpiece lifted over the Malwa plateau in the late eleventh century, and as the seat of the Rising Lord whose name it carries like a vow. But the building keeps a second, quieter distinction, one that has only lately begun to receive the wonder it is owed. It is among the most remarkable epigraphic archives in all of central India: a single monument bearing a continuous sequence of inscriptions in Sanskrit, Persian, and Hindi that runs across roughly eight centuries — from the temple's consecration in the year *saṃvat* 1137 (1080 CE) to its archaeological restoration in *saṃvat* 1985 (1928 CE).[1] Nearly the whole span of north India's medieval and early-modern history can be read off these walls, if one only knows where to look, and which scripts to carry to the stone like lamps into a dark room.

This chapter listens to the inscriptions not one by one — Chapter 3 has already given the foundation eulogy, the *Udayapur Praśasti*, its proper hearing — but as a single accumulating corpus across time. We will watch the archive build itself, layer by patient layer: first the dense Sanskrit stratum of the Paramāra and post-Paramāra centuries, then one extraordinary inscription that has slipped almost unnoticed past the scholarly eye — the serpentine "scimitar of letters," a magico-political device unique to a pair of eleventh-century kings — and at last the Persian records that mark the moment the conquerors arrived and a part of the temple became a mosque. The chapter closes, as the book closes, with the temple's long afterlife: defacement, repurposing, restoration, and a worship that has never quite ceased.

## The shape of the corpus

Begin, if you will, with the plainest fact of all — the almost bureaucratic fact of *quantity*. The Udayeśvara is not a temple with an inscription or two; it is a temple swarming with them, alive with them, the way a hive is alive. The Gwalior State archaeology department, in a campaign of survey and copying that ran patiently through the 1920s, transcribed and listed inscription after inscription from the complex, and the labour was carried forward, decades on, by the epigraphy branch of the Archaeological Survey of India.[2] Only a fraction of all this has ever been formally edited and published — chiefly in the corpus volumes of H. V. Trivedi and in the studies of A. C. Mittal — which means that the temple's walls still hold a reserve of unread and half-read text, water in a well not yet drawn: a working archive rather than a closed book.[3]

What survives, gathered together, sketches a biography — the life-story of a place. The Paramāra inventory of inscriptions drawn from Udayapur alone shows the site returning again and again as a place of royal record across the long arc of the dynasty. The founder Udayāditya is named on the great stone inscription dated *saṃvat* 1137 / 1080 CE — the consecration year, the year of beginnings — and on a second undated stone bound to the same moment.[4] His son and successor Naravarman left a record here dated *saṃvat* 1151 / 1094 CE, the year after his accession.[5] More than a century onward, the temple was still drawing kings to its walls: the Paramāra king Devapāla is recorded at Udayapur in *saṃvat* 1286 / 1229 CE, and again a few years later, around 1232.[6] And remarkably late, in the dynasty's very twilight, Jayasiṃha II appears on a stone here dated *saṃvat* 1366 / 1309 CE — only a handful of years before the Paramāra line itself dissolved into the rising tide of the Delhi Sultanate.[7]

Set those dates down in a row — 1080, 1094, 1229, 1232, 1309 — and something rises off the list that is far more than antiquarian. For well over two centuries, through the dynasty's high noon and down into its long decline, the Udayeśvara remained a *living* royal foundation: a place where king after king chose to be seen, to record his gifts and grants, to renew the bond between the throne and the Rising Lord. This was no monument the Paramāras raised once and then forgot. It was an instrument they kept in their hands and kept playing. And when we read these inscriptions as a corpus rather than as scattered curiosities, this is the first thing the stone confides to us — that the Udayeśvara was, for the whole life of the dynasty, the dynastic shrine *par excellence*, the Paramāra answer to the oldest question a Śaiva king can ask: where does one go to make one's kingship visible?

The languages and the scripts deepen the picture still. The earliest layer is Sanskrit, written in the rectilinear Nāgarī of the late eleventh century — characters described by those who have bent close to them as well-formed and inch-high, cut in a rigidly upright style, their verticals finishing in a sharp bend to the right: the local hand of Udayāditya's own masons, the very gesture of their wrists preserved in stone.[8] Over this medieval Sanskrit stratum lie the far later additions: Persian, in the Arabic script, recording the arrival of a wholly different polity; and Hindi, marking the temple's modern caretaking. Three languages, three scripts, three religious and political worlds, all incised on one building. There is the archive in miniature — not a harmony but a stratigraphy, each layer pressing its weight down upon the one before, the way years press down upon a heart.

## A serpentine scimitar of letters

Among all this writing there is one inscription that deserves to be lifted out and held up to the light, for it is unlike anything most of us will ever have imagined a temple could carry. It is not a praise-poem; it is not a grant; it is not a date. It is a *diagram* — a serpent, drawn out of letters.

The stone itself is unassuming, even humble: a rectangular slab of pinkish sandstone, some seventy centimetres high, built into the outer parapet wall — the *prākāra* — at the point where the temple's quadrangular courtyard projects southward.[9] Anyone who has studied that wall closely can read at once that the slab is no longer where it began its life. Its flat, inscribed face is turned *inward*, pressed blind against the masonry, while its decorative side — carved with an ornamental balustrade, a *vedikā* — looks outward, the exact reverse of every well-preserved stretch of courtyard wall around it. The block plainly belonged to this parapet, yet it once looked *into* the courtyard; and at some point it was prised out, flipped, and reset the wrong way round — almost certainly during one of the twentieth-century restoration campaigns, when masons rebuilding the wall did not recognize the treasure in their hands and set it back facing the wrong way.[10] There is a small parable of the whole archive in that accident of survival: a precious text saved precisely by being mortared, sightless, into a wall — refuge granted, all unknowing, in the very act of concealment.

Turn the stone over now in the mind's eye, to its inscribed face, and here is what you find waiting. The centrepiece is a snake — but a snake whose entire body is built from the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, each letter resting in its own little compartment along the serpent's coiling length.[11] Read from the top down, the creature's geometrical "head" carries the fourteen vowels, marshalled in the classical order of short-and-long pairs — *a, ā; i, ī; u, ū; ṛ, ṝ; ḷ, ḹ* — rising to the complex vowels *e, ai* and the compounds *o, au*. Down the tubular "body" run the consonants: the four semivowels at the neck, then the five classes of stops gathered into a rhomboidal knot at the torso, then the three sibilants trailing down one leg. The whole series opens and closes with the voiced aspirate *ha*, exactly as the alphabet is arranged in the Śivasūtras that open Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, the foundational grammar of Sanskrit.[12] At the diagram's base sit the rarer breathed sounds; and at the very tail, looping up and down upon itself, are the grammatical *endings* — twenty-one terminations for nouns across all seven of their cases, and eighteen for verbs in the present tense, in both the active and the middle voice.[13] Which is to say: a complete machine of the language, a living engine of speech. Hold this one serpent in view and you hold, all at once, the very sounds of Sanskrit and the rules for inflecting them, coiled together into a single image — the breath of a whole tongue caught and quieted on a stone.

And what is it *for*? The answer is given by two short lines of writing on the slab — a damaged but partly legible verse — and confirmed, crucially, by an identical diagram surviving elsewhere. The verse on the Udayapur stone calls the diagram, in so many words, a *varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā*: a "serpentine scimitar of letters."[14] The compound is built to startle, and it does. *Varṇa* means both a *letter* of the alphabet and, in another register, the *order of society*; *nāga* is the serpent; *kṛpāṇikā* is a small curved sword, a scimitar. The grammar of the surviving verse runs as a command in the causative — roughly, *"may the protector cause this serpentine scimitar of letters to protect…"* — though precisely what is to be protected has flaked away with the stone, lost to time as so much is lost.[15] The image, all the same, is unmistakable: the vowels form the snake's flaring hood, the consonants its coiled body, the grammatical endings its tail; and that same shape reads, in the selfsame instant, as a weapon — the hood become the curved blade, the knotted torso the ornamented hilt, the winding tail the sword-strap. A snake, an alphabet, and a sword, fused into one device. As the inscription's modern student so finely puts it, the object presents "an unusual synthesis of aurality, visuality and efficacy" — sound, sight, and power braided together into a single bright cord.[16]

For the meaning of that power we must journey west, to the great Mahākāla temple of Ujjain, the sacred heart of the Paramāra realm, where an identical diagram bearing the same name survives in a far better-preserved setting, embedded in a longer dedicatory inscription.[17] There the verses speak plainly. They call the diagram a "serpent-scimitar of letters marked with Udayāditya's name" — *udayāditya-nāmāṅka-varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā* — and say it was composed, along with other "poetic gems," by a figure described as "the friend of talented poets," apparently King Naravarman himself.[18] The diagram, the Ujjain text declares, is an "ornament to be affixed on the chest of both poets and kings," and — most tellingly of all — a "unique magical sword," a *siddhāsiputrikā*, belonging to those two royal devotees of Śiva, Udayāditya and his son Naravarman, and intended *varṇasthityai*: "for the preservation of language and social order."[19]

That phrase is the key that turns in the lock of the whole strange object. Scholars long supposed these letter-serpents to be schoolroom aids — clever mnemonics for drilling Sanskrit into the heads of young boys.[20] The Ujjain verses show us otherwise, and show us something stranger and grander. The serpent-scimitar was a *device of kingship*. The pun in *varṇa* is doing real and weighty work: to "preserve the letters" is, in the very same breath, to "preserve the social order," so that the king who guards the alphabet thereby guards the proper arrangement of the world entire. Śiva, the Ujjain inscription elsewhere declares, is language itself personified; and the Paramāra kings, who held their crowns as Śiva's lieutenants, claimed the alphabet — the very substance of the god — as a magical weapon of their own.[21] To inscribe the serpent-scimitar on the wall of a royal Śiva temple was thus to forge a single object out of grammar, magic, and sovereignty: a talisman that protected language, dynasty, and cosmic order in one coiled and gleaming stroke.

This is why the device travelled. Versions of the serpentine scimitar attributed to Udayāditya are known not from Ujjain alone but from Dhar, the Paramāra capital, and from the temple cluster at Un — and now, with this slab, from Udaypur itself.[22] (At Un the diagram is built into the Chaubārā Ḍerā temple, a stone's throw from another inscription that names the great king Bhoja, Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor.)[23] Together these copies form a small family of "serpentine scimitars of letters," all radiating outward from the same two kings in the last decades of the eleventh century, like sparks struck from one flint. That Udaypur should possess one is exactly what we ought to have expected. The town and its tank were founded and named for Udayāditya himself, around 1080; its temple was his temple, his name made stone. Of all the shrines in Malwa, this was precisely the place to plant the dynasty's grammatical sword — and so it was planted, and here it stayed, flipped and forgotten, until a fieldworker turned the stone over in his imagination and read the snake.

## When the conquerors wrote

The Sanskrit archive is the record of a single civilization talking quietly to itself across two centuries. The next layer is something harsher, and we should not soften it: the record of that civilization being overtaken.

By the early fourteenth century the Paramāras were gone, swallowed — as that very late Jayasiṃha II inscription of 1309 had all but foretold — by the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate across Malwa. And the conquest left its mark on the Udayeśvara in the most literal way imaginable. At the rear of the temple, on the western side of the platform, a mosque was raised — built, in the standard practice of the age, partly out of the temple's own dismantled stone, the body of one faith taken up to house another.[24] Tradition and the local record place this work in the Tughluq period; the relevant order to dismantle and repurpose part of the complex is set around 1325 CE, in the years of the early Delhi Sultanate, and the construction of the rear mosque in the late 1330s.[25] Sources differ on the exact attribution and date — the Madhya Bharat site directory assigns the Tughluq mosque to the years 1336–1338 CE (A.H. 737–739), under Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, while Pande's study frames the dismantling order more broadly within the early Sultanate's reach into Malwa.[26] What is not in dispute is the physical fact, the wound itself: a corner of a Śaiva temple converted into a Muslim house of prayer, its rear hall fitted with a *miḥrāb* — the niche that turns the faithful toward Mecca — cut into a building that had been raised for the Rising Lord.[27]

And here the archive changes its very language. Set into this converted portion of the complex are two Persian inscriptions, in the Arabic script, recording the new dispensation.[28] They are the third great stratum of the Udayeśvara's text: after the Sanskrit of the founders and the grammatical magic of their grammarians, the Persian of the conquerors. We must be candid about the limits of our sources here, as honesty requires. The reference materials available for this chapter confirm the *existence* and the *placement* of the two Persian inscriptions — and confirm that they belong to the mosque-conversion of the rear of the temple — but they do not preserve a full translation of their contents. [GAP — the precise wording and full reading of the two Persian inscriptions are not given in the sources consulted; they are listed and located but not transcribed.] What we can say with confidence is structural rather than verbal: that at the very moment the temple ceased to be purely a Paramāra monument and became contested ground, the writing on its walls switched scripts, and a new sovereignty signed its name in a new hand.

There is something almost unbearably eloquent in that switch of script alone. One does not need to read the Persian to grasp what it means. For two and a half centuries the only writing the Udayeśvara had ever carried was Sanskrit — the god's own language, set down in the kings' own hand. Then, abruptly, a second alphabet appears, running right to left, professing a different god and a different king. The serpent-scimitar had promised to *preserve the letters and the social order*; the Persian inscriptions are the documentary proof that the order it guarded had, in the end, given way. The two layers face one another across the same building like the two halves of a single, painful sentence — and we, latecomers, read it whole.

## Afterlife: a permanent sunrise

What happened next is the part of the story that monuments are so rarely granted to tell, because so few of them survive to tell it. The Udayeśvara did.

The fourteenth-century mosque was not the last violence visited upon it. A persistent local tradition holds that the temple's carvings were defaced again in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, late in the seventeenth century — the same emperor whose 1682 demolition of the Vijay Mandir at nearby Bhilsa stands on firmer record.[29] The tradition at Udaypur is exactly that — a tradition, carried in local memory and repeated in the descriptive literature rather than fixed by a dated inscription — and it should be reported as such, honestly; but the mutilation of the temple's images is plain enough upon the stone, whatever the precise hand that wrought it.[30] For a long stretch, then, the Rising Lord rose over a wounded shrine: images battered, a corner surrendered to another faith, the great red-sandstone spire presiding over a complex that had passed out of the hands that built it. A god keeping vigil over his own injury.

Then the wheel turned once more. In the eighteenth century the Marathas rose to power across Malwa, and with them came the Scindia (Sindhia) house of Gwalior — Hindu rulers who restored Hindu worship to the great shrines their predecessors' enemies had repurposed. At the Udayeśvara the restoration left a precise and touching memorial. In 1775 CE a general of Mahādjī Scindia named Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave the temple a new face for its god: a *liṅga* sheathed in a fitted cover of brass — a *mukhaliṅga*, the brass mask that gives the aniconic stone of Śiva a human face — installed in the sanctum, where it abides to this very day.[31] One ought to pause, and pause long, over the modesty and the meaning of that gift. After the dismantling order, after the mosque, after the defacement, the act that restored the temple to worship was not some grand rebuilding but a single, careful, gleaming face of brass, fitted by a soldier-donor over the ancient stone. The Rising Lord was given back his features. (Sources record the donor's name and rank in slightly varying forms — a general of Mahādjī Scindia, named in the Pande study as Khaṇḍerāo Appājī — and agree on the date of 1775 and the fact of the brass face; the minor variation in how the donor is styled is worth noting honestly.)[32]

The final stratum of the archive is the one that makes this whole chapter possible at all: the modern campaigns of survey, conservation, and repair. Under the Gwalior State, the archaeology department mounted a sustained effort through the 1920s — the very campaign, recorded in its annual reports for the years 1923–24 through 1928–29, that copied and listed the temple's inscriptions and reset its displaced stones, including, as we have seen, the serpent-scimitar slab now mortared the wrong way round.[33] The work passed in time to the Archaeological Survey of India, whose own epigraphy branch and field surveys returned to the temple across the following decades, into the 1980s, to record and to conserve.[34] These restorers, too, wrote upon the building — in Hindi, the third living language of the archive — closing the eight-century sequence that had opened in *saṃvat* 1137 with a final layer dated to *saṃvat* 1985.[35] The men who saved the temple thereby became its last inscribers, their workmanlike notices the most recent silt to settle in the riverbed.

And so the corpus stands complete — or as complete as a living thing can ever be, which is to say never wholly, never finally — for the Udayeśvara is not a ruin, not a museum, not a cleared archaeological site fenced off against its own past. It is a temple still in worship. The brass-faced liṅga that Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave it in 1775 is still attended; the doors still open at morning; the Rising Lord still rises. Eight centuries of writing — Sanskrit praise and Sanskrit magic, Persian conquest, Hindi repair — run across the red sandstone like the rings of a great tree, each marking a year the monument lived through and outlived.

It is fitting to end the whole book on the image its title has promised us throughout. King Udayāditya took for his temple the name of the *Rising Lord* — Udayeśvara — and the foundation eulogy that opens this archive compared the king himself, with a poet's beautiful audacity, to "another sun, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his foes."[36] The metaphor was meant to last a reign. It has lasted, instead, the better part of a millennium. Conquerors came and signed their names in another script; emperors are said to have hacked at the god's face; dynasties fell and rose and fell again. And through all of it the great bhūmija spire has stood upon the Malwa plateau and caught the first light of every single morning on its seven storeys of carved sandstone — a permanent sunrise fixed in stone above a temple that has never quite stopped being a temple. The kings who built it are dust now, and the empires that fought over it are only names in a gazetteer. The Rising Lord is still rising. That, in the end, is what the archive on his walls records — not the triumph of any one of the powers that wrote there, but the endurance of the stone they all wrote on.
