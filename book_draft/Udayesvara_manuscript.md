# Chapter 1 — The Rising Lord

## I. A Temple in the Betwa Country

Set out east from the railway halt at Bareth, and walk — for there is no honest way to know this land but to cross it — some six kilometres over the broad, dry-season plain of central Malwa, and the country will at last yield up to you a walled town, the way a river, after a long thirst, yields water. Its ramparts are not the tidy brick-and-mortar of the centuries that came after, but giant blocks of stone, laid up dry — uncemented, fitted by weight and skill alone, trusting to gravity and the mason's eye where lesser builders would trust to lime — so that the wall does not so much stand upon the plateau as seem quarried out of it, as though the earth had decided, in one slow act of will, to rise.[1] This is Udayapur, today a modest municipal village in the Basoda tahsil of Vidisha district, Madhya Pradesh: a small place, easily overlooked, the sort of place the maps name in their faintest type. Yet nine hundred years ago it was a new royal city, fresh-minted and full of intent, and at its heart its founder raised a building that scholars have not, in all the long generations since, stopped praising.

The building is a Śiva temple, and — like all things that truly belong to themselves — it announces itself from a distance, before you are near enough to know its name. Its tower, the *śikhara*, that soaring superstructure which crowns a North Indian temple's sanctum, climbs in red sandstone above the rooftops, ribbed and faceted, dense with carving from base to summit, catching the low light as a lit thing catches light. Come closer, and the surface resolves into something almost vertiginous, something that will not let the eye rest: hundreds of miniature spires, stacked in tidy diminishing rows like an orchard of stone planted upward into the air, climbing each face of the great tower toward the heavy ribbed disc that caps it. And there — look, on one shoulder of the spire — a small human figure is shown clambering upward, a flag-bearer frozen forever in mid-ascent, carrying the victory banner he means to plant at the very summit.[2] He has been climbing for nine centuries and is climbing still; he will never arrive, and that, perhaps, is the point. The whole monument is a study in rising. And how fitting that is, for it carries its patron's name three times folded into stone. The king was Udayāditya — *udaya* meaning "rising," as of the sun over a dark land — and the god he installed here he called **Udayeśvara**, the Rising Lord.[3]

The setting moves me as much as the stone, for the two cannot be parted. This is the Betwa country. The river that the ancients called Vetravatī rises in the hills of Raisen district to the west and runs broadly south to north across the Vidisha plateau, patient and unhurried, before it surrenders at last its water to the Yamuna, far away in the plains of the north — a small life poured into a greater one, the way all our small lives are.[4] It is not, for most of its length, a grand perennial flood; in its upper reaches, the very reaches that drain this region, the Betwa is largely intermittent, running hard and brown in the rains and thin or wholly dry between them.[5] The climate it waters is semi-arid central India, a land of fierce and beautiful extremes — summer heat of forty degrees and more, cool nights in the winter, and a monsoon that arrives in June and, in the brief mercy of four months, delivers some nine-tenths of the whole year's rain.[6] This is a country that has learned, as the patient always learn, to store water against the long dry season; and Udayāditya was working squarely within that ancient logic when, beside his new city and his new temple, he had a great reservoir excavated — the **Udayasamudra**, the "sea of Udaya," a tank to cup the monsoon in its hands and feed the town through the parched and waiting months.[7] City, temple, and lake were dreamed together, conceived as a single royal foundation and stamped, three times over, with one man's name.

The stone itself is of this place; it could be of nowhere else. The Malwa plateau here is built of Deccan trap and Vindhyan sandstone, and it is the sandstone — quarried red and pink and tender beneath the chisel — that gave the region its temples, as a body gives the soul a face.[8] You can read the very geology in the carving, if you are willing to read slowly. Where Khajuraho's celebrated temples rise a few hundred kilometres to the north-east, the masons of Malwa worked their own red rock into something the early surveyors judged the equal of anything in all of India. And the *Vidisha District Gazetteer*, surveying the monument with the cool, dry prose of a government handbook, allows itself one unguarded breath of wonder, one verdict it cannot quite withhold: the sculpture here, it says, is "finer than Khajuraho."[9]

## II. Why This Temple Holds Us

It is a large claim, this, for a building that most travellers have never so much as heard named, and it deserves to be unfolded slowly. Why should an eleventh-century Śiva temple, in a half-forgotten Malwa town, hold our attention across the whole arc of a book?

The first answer is art-historical, and it is the plainest. To the archaeologists who catalogued the monuments of this region in the middle of the twentieth century, the Udayeśvara was, quite simply, the best thing they had. D. R. Patil, surveying the cultural heritage of the old Madhya Bharat state in 1952, returned to it again and again, the way one returns to a beloved face, and rendered his judgement without a single hedge: "In the long array of these sacred structures, the Udayeshwar temple stands supreme."[10] It was, in his telling, the very apogee of the Indo-Aryan — the Nāgara — temple tradition in central India, a building against which all the others were measured and found, gently, wanting. When he wished to praise the Siddheśvara temple at Nemawar in the Narmada valley, the highest compliment he could find was to liken its tower to Udaypur's; when he reached the temples at Un, he called them work "matching that of Udaypur."[11] The Udayeśvara had become the yardstick — the one true note against which the others were tuned.

The second answer is that the temple is a particularly pure and mature flowering of a specific architectural manner — the **Bhūmija** style — and one of the few monuments that lets us understand that style at all. The word *bhūmija* means "born from the earth," and it is a name with several layers folded quietly into it, the way a seed folds the whole of a tree. It marks the style as native, a regional manner rooted in the very soil of Malwa rather than carried in from elsewhere. It hints at a "terrestrial" character, an earthly evolution set against styles imagined to have a divine origin. And, most concretely of all, it describes exactly what the eye sees: those rows of miniature spires (*bhūmis* can mean "stories" or "levels") climbing the tower so that the whole thing appears to grow, living and rooted, straight up out of the ground.[12] The Bhūmija manner was no provincial accident, no happy stumble. One of the earliest texts to name it is the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, that great treatise on architecture compiled under the Paramāra king Bhoja — Udayāditya's illustrious predecessor — and by the time of our temple the style was so fully formed, so sure of itself, that it must have had a long and patient history behind it. It was the Paramāras of Malwa who brought it to its maturity; and the scattering of Bhūmija temples across Malwa, Rajasthan, and northern Maharashtra traces, like footprints, the far reach of their cultural influence.[13]

The third answer is that the Udayeśvara is not merely beautiful but *legible* — that it speaks. A great temple is never only an art object set down to be admired; it is a religious building, a machine, if you will, for making the invisible visible, and its design is a coded statement of belief, patient and waiting to be read. This one is unusually rich in such coding. It belongs to the world of **Śaiva Siddhānta**, the dominant theological school of medieval Śaivism, a systematic philosophy of Śiva as the supreme and final reality — and its sculpture reads almost as that philosophy set, line by line, into stone.[14] The walls carry a deliberate, ordered population of gods, each in his appointed place: the eight **Dikpālas**, the guardians of the directions, each set upon the wall that faces the quarter of the world he rules — Indra to the east with his thunderbolt, Agni to the south-east upon his ram, Yama the lord of death to the south, Varuṇa of the cosmic waters to the west upon his crocodile mount.[15] Among them and above them, Śiva himself appears in form after form, restless and inexhaustible: as the cosmic dancer **Naṭeśa**, caught and held in the violent *tāṇḍava* with one knee flung high; as **Tripurāntaka**, the warrior who undid three demon-cities with a single arrow; and — pointedly, achingly, for a king who fought hard to keep his throne — as **Mṛtyuñjaya**, the "Conqueror of Death," seated in meditation, wreathed in living serpents, invoked to grant victory and to overcome the cold danger of death upon the battlefield.[16] To walk around this temple, then, is not to look at a theology. It is to be walked through one.

And the fourth answer is human, and historical, and it is the one that quickens the heart. The Udayeśvara has survived nine hundred turbulent years, and the marks of that survival are not blemishes upon its meaning but a part of it. It was begun, by the testimony of its own inscriptions, in 1059, and its victory flag was raised in 1080 — twenty-one years of labour by a royal workshop at the very height of its powers, a whole generation bending itself to one rising thing.[17] It outlived the dynasty that built it, as the made thing so often outlives the maker. In the fourteenth century, when the armies of the Delhi Sultanate took Malwa, the rear of the complex was converted into a mosque, built partly from the stones of the temple's own dismantled outbuildings — the building, in a sense, made to give up its body for another faith — and two Persian inscriptions still record the deed.[18] Sculptures were defaced; later legend laid further mutilation at the door of the emperor Aurangzeb.[19] And yet the main building came through, in the early surveyors' spare phrase, "without serious damage"; and in the eighteenth century the Maratha Scindias of Gwalior restored Hindu worship, sheathing the ancient stone *liṅga* in a brass cover with a carved face — a gift recorded for a general of Mahadji Scindia in 1775.[20] The temple we walk into today is therefore a palimpsest, a thing written and overwritten and written again: an eleventh-century Paramāra masterpiece, scarred by the fourteenth century, mended in the eighteenth, and worshipped, even now, still.

## III. The Patron and His Moment

Behind the building, as behind every made and lasting thing, there stands a man, and a dynasty. The **Paramāras** ruled Malwa from their capital at Dhar through the tenth, the eleventh, the twelfth centuries, and they were among the great cultured houses of medieval India — a line that prized the lamp as much as the sword. It ran through able and ambitious kings: Sīyaka II, Mūñja, and above all **Bhoja** (who reigned, roughly, in the first half of the eleventh century), that scholar-king whose name became a byword for learning across the land, who endowed a temple of Sarasvatī at Dhara and under whose patronage that very treatise on architecture, the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, was compiled.[21]

Udayāditya came after Bhoja, and — this is the heart of it — he came to a kingdom in trouble, to a house whose light had guttered. He ascended the throne around 1070 and inherited the hard, unglamorous work of consolidation, of holding the Paramāra realm together against its enemies with both hands.[22] The temple's foundation inscription — the *Udayapur Praśasti* — remembers him in the heroic register of that age, casting the king as a second sun risen over a dark dominion: "another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword, and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."[23] The sun-imagery is not idle court flattery, not a poet's reflex. It is the same image that runs, like a single bright thread, through the whole foundation — *udaya*, the rising sun, in the king's name, the city's name, the god's name, the reservoir's name, sounding and re-sounding like a bell struck four times. To build the Udayeśvara was to make a permanent claim, to carve a hope into something that could not easily be unsaid: that under this king, after a long and bitter season, Malwa had risen again into light and stability. During his reign, the sources tell us, the region knew peace and flourished as a leading centre of religion and culture; and Udayāditya marked that flourishing by founding a city and crowning it with this temple, as a man plants a tree he knows will shade those he will never meet.[24]

This is among the things that make the Udayeśvara so rewarding to dwell upon: it is a monument whose patron, date, dynasty, faith, and even psychological motive are all, astonishingly, within our reach. So many great temples come to us anonymous, mute, their makers swallowed by time. This one comes to us with a name, a praise-poem, a pair of dates, and a king who needed, with his whole heart, his god to be a conqueror of death.

## IV. How to Read the Building

Before we set out together, it helps to carry a rough map of the temple in the mind, for the chapters ahead will return again and again to its parts by their proper names, and a thing named is a thing we may begin to love.

A North Indian temple of this kind is, at its core, a small dark room beneath a tall tower — a held breath beneath an upward cry. The room is the **garbhagṛha** — literally the "womb-chamber," the sanctum, the holy of holies — and within it stands the *liṅga* of Śiva.[25] Over it rises the **śikhara**, the mountain-like tower, reaching where the room cannot. In front of the sanctum, here at Udaypur, comes a vestibule, the **antarāla**, and then a large pillared hall for the worshippers, the **gūḍhamaṇḍapa** ("closed hall"), which throws out three projecting balcony-porches to the east, the north, and the south, like arms opened to the day.[26] The whole temple — *prāsāda* is the old and lordly word for such a stone edifice — stands upon a lofty platform, the **jagati**, which was once ringed about by eight subsidiary shrines, several of them now broken, their absence its own quiet kind of speech.[27] On the same axis, in front, stands a separate low, flat-roofed hall, the **Vedī**, a stone pavilion enclosed by a balustrade and pierced with latticed windows that strain the light — built, by old tradition and the report of the surveyor Alexander Cunningham, as a public hall for the recitation of the Vedas and the performance of sacrifices.[28]

The original way in was from the east, up a grand stairway flanked by over-life-size doorkeepers, the *pratīhāras* of Śiva, standing their long watch; today, by the small accidents of history, visitors enter from the west, against the grain of the builders' intent.[29] And the plan beneath all of this is itself a small marvel, a piece of geometry that feels less designed than discovered. Rather than divide a square like a grid, the Bhūmija designer rotated a square around its own still centre, pausing at fixed intervals as a dancer pauses; the spinning corners map out a circle, and that circle governs the line of the outer walls. The result is a plan at once star-shaped and circular — *saptaratha*, seven-faceted, upon the ground, and *saptabhūmi*, seven-storeyed, in the air.[30] The faceted buttresses of the walls run unbroken up into the tower, where they become those climbing rows of miniature spires; the surveyors, reaching for the right image, compared them to long strings of pearls hung down from the summit.[31] It is a building conceived as a single, unhesitating upward gesture, from earth to sky — which is, of course, precisely what the name had promised all along.

## V. The Journey of This Book

This book is a single temple, examined slowly, and lovingly, and from many sides. That is itself a deliberate choice, and one with a distinguished pedigree: the scholar whose decade-long study of the Udayeśvara underpins so much of what follows, Anupa Pande, argued that a temple is precisely the kind of object where art, religion, and architecture meet and intersect, and that its meaning does not sit in any one of these but lives in the crossings between them — so that to understand it truly you must be willing to read it as art object, as house of God, and as engineered building, all at once, holding the three together in a single gaze.[32] That is the spirit of the chapters ahead; and they are arranged as a journey, inward and downward, from the wide world the temple stood within to the still vision it was raised to express.

We begin, in the next chapter, with **history** — the long, slow story of the Betwa country and the Paramāra dynasty, the rise of Udayāditya, and the moment of crisis and recovery into which the temple was born. From there we turn to the **patron** himself: the king of the praise-poem, the founder of cities, the man who named a god after the rising sun and then asked that god, with what we must imagine was real fear, to conquer death.

Then we come to the stone. A chapter on **architecture** will take the Bhūmija style seriously, on its own terms and in its own language — the rotated plan, the seven facets and seven storeys, the soaring stacked spires, the mouldings of base and wall that the old craftsmen named with such loving precision that the names alone are a kind of prayer. A chapter on **sculpture** will walk the outer walls deity by deity, reading the ordered crowd of guardians and goddesses and dancing Śivas, and pausing — for we must pause — over the rare and the strange: a dancing Sarasvatī, a goddess seated upon a corpse, a syncretic god who is Śiva and Viṣṇu and Sūrya and Brahmā at once, all the divine faces gathered into one.

With the imagery in hand, we may at last ask what it all *means*; and so a chapter on **philosophy** will set the temple within Śaiva Siddhānta, that metaphysics of Śiva which gives the whole programme its deep logic — the very system the eleventh century itself laboured to capture in texts like Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*. And finally we will turn to the **inscriptions** — the *Udayapur Praśasti* and the later Persian records of conquest and conversion — the written voices, Hindu and Muslim, royal and humble, that the building has gathered patiently to itself across nine hundred years, and that murmur to us still.

It is a great deal to ask of a single temple. But the Udayeśvara, as we shall come to see, can carry it, and carry it lightly. It was built by a king who meant his stone to outlast him — to stand upon the Malwa plateau as a permanent sunrise, a dawn that would not set — and so, against every odd, it has.

# Chapter 2

## The Paramāras of Malwa — From the Noon of Bhoja to the Rising of Udayāditya

When Udayāditya raised his temple beside the Betwa, he was not, you must understand, building upon emptiness — no king builds upon emptiness, however much the lonely majesty of a finished thing tempts us to imagine it sprung whole from a single will — for he was the heir of a dynasty already three centuries deep in time, the inheritor of a royal house whose name had once been a lamp held up to all the highest things a medieval Indian kingdom could offer: conquest, yes, but also poetry, and learning, and the slow, patient, almost tender engineering of lakes and cities. To understand the man who built the Rising Lord, then, we must first wade back upstream — into the family he came from, the kingdom they made of their hopes, and the crisis that nearly swallowed both before ever he touched the throne. This is the story of the Paramāras of Malwa: how they rose, how they came to a golden noon under the scholar-king Bhoja, how they very nearly drowned after his death, and how Udayāditya — alone, late, steady — gathered up the broken pieces and made them whole again.

### A land called Avanti

The land itself comes first, as the land always does. Malwa is that rolling plateau of west-central India, watered by the Betwa and the Narmadā and cradled within the arms of the Vindhya hills — a country of black cotton soil, of fields heavy with their own fertility and towns loud with trade, that the medieval poets loved to summon by its older, more resonant, more singing name: **Avanti**. It was rich; by every account that survives, it was rich. A thirteenth-century inscription, set up far away upon the cool heights of Mount Abu, still lifts its voice across the centuries to praise the place: "Glory to Avanti, the home of rich men, which protects the world by the heroism of its lords, cleanses it by the pure and brilliant life of its Brahmans... and which gladdens it through the fragrance arising from the dalliance of its passionate youths." [1] This was a kingdom that knew its own worth, and was not shy to say so.

Two cities anchored it, as two stones anchor a net cast wide over water. The first was **Ujjain** — the Sanskrit Ujjayinī — ancient beyond the reach of memory, sacred to the great god Śiva in his terrible and tender form as Mahākāla, "Lord of Time," and home to one of the most venerated shrines in all of Hindu India. The second was **Dhārā** — the Dhar of today — which the Paramāras made their working capital, the city a Hoysala grant of 1117 remembered, with lovely economy, simply as the place "made prosperous by Bhoja." [2] From these twin seats the Paramāra kings styled themselves *Mālavapati*, "Lord of Malwa," and *Avantinaśa*, "Master of Avanti," and at the high noon of their power their writ ran from the country about Kota and Bundi in the north down to the Narmadā and the edge of Khandesh in the south, from Bhilsa and Sagar in the east westward to the river Mahī. [3]

### Born of fire

But where did this family come from? The Paramāras gave an answer that was less history than sacred poetry — and they told it, you can feel, with their whole conviction. They claimed to be **Agnikula**, "fire-born": descended not from some ordinary forefather of flesh but from a hero who leapt fully formed out of a sacrificial fire-pit upon Mount Abu (the *Arbudācala*), the pit kindled by the sage Vasiṣṭha himself. In the legend the sage's wish-granting cow had been stolen away, and from his *yajña-kuṇḍa* — his fire-altar — sprang a champion to win her back, a warrior named forever for his single deed: *Paramāra*, "the slayer of the foe." Other Rajput houses were sometimes drawn into this same fiery genealogy, like moths to the one flame, yet the Paramāras' claim burned brightest of them all. One modern authority on the matter, Dasharatha Sharma, put it plainly and without hedging: no dynasty other than the Paramāras should really be regarded as an Agnikula lineage at all. [4]

We need not believe in the cow, nor in the leaping fire-pit, to see why the story mattered so — and matter it did. An origin myth of this kind does real and lasting work: it hands a ruling family a divine charter, knots it fast to the Brahmanical world of sacrifice and to the holy mountain of Abu, and lifts it clear above the common run of kings. The Paramāras repeated it lovingly in their own praise-poems — and nowhere more fully than in the great **Udaipur** *praśasti*, that eulogistic inscription which remains, to this day, one of our chief sources for the whole long genealogy of the house. [5]

Behind the shining myth, the line we can actually verify begins more humbly, as such lines tend to. The earliest king the records will name with any confidence is **Upendra**, placed near the opening of the ninth century. After him came a succession of rulers — Vairisiṃha, a Sīyaka, a Vākpati — who held Malwa as something nearer a provincial power than an empire, often in the long shadow of greater neighbours. [6] The true transformation, from local dynasty into imperial house, belongs to the tenth century, and to a king named Sīyaka.

### Sīyaka, Mūñja, and the forging of an empire

**Sīyaka II** — also remembered as Sīyaka-Harṣa — is the king whom the dynasty's own poets credited with the winning of imperial standing. As the Udaipur *praśasti* and the later historians tell it, he "won imperial status for his family by the strength of his arms, and left to his successors the task of building up an ideal empire." [7] He carried war into the north-west against the Hūṇas and crossed swords with the rising Chandellas to the east; and out of his wars the Paramāras emerged, for the first time, as a genuine power upon the Indian stage — no longer the vassals of someone else's ambition, but masters of their own.

It was Sīyaka's adopted son and successor, though, who first showed the world that peculiar Paramāra alloy of sword and pen — the blend that would become the family's very signature. **Vākpati Mūñja** — he answers to both names, and his inscriptions heap upon him besides such grand titles as Śrīvallabha and Pṛthvīvallabha, "Beloved of the Earth" — ruled from about 973 to 995, and became, in the long memory of his people, the dynasty's first golden figure. [8] He was a warrior of furious ambition and a poet of real and tender gift, and the two halves of him, in the end, collided.

Mūñja's great obsession was the south, and his great enemy the Cālukya king Tailapa of the Deccan. The tradition here moves almost in epic rhythm, building like a drumbeat: Mūñja is said to have marched against Tailapa and broken him again, and again, and again — some sixteen times over — until, on the seventeenth crossing, his fortune at last ran dry. Against the urgent counsel of his minister Rudrāditya, who had begged him to leave off these endless southern wars, he crossed once too often, and was taken. [9] His elephant corps, the very pride of his army — the chronicles claim he lost something like fourteen hundred and seventy-six of the great beasts in his Deccan fighting — could not buy him back. [10] He was put to death within the Cālukya capital.

And yet what the tradition lingers over is not the battle, nor even the loss, but the poet turning at last to face his own end. Mūñja, led out to execution, is remembered for a verse that mourns not himself at all but the goddess of learning he was leaving orphaned behind him: "Fortune will go to Govinda; glory and heroism to the house of the Hero; but when Mūñja has passed away, that storehouse of fame, Sarasvatī, will be without support." [11] This was no idle making of epitaphs. Mūñja had been, in living truth, a patron of letters and a writer in his own right — verses ascribed to him survive now only as quotations, copied out lovingly by later anthologists and critics who could not even agree among themselves whether a given stanza was his or his predecessor's, so closely had the royal voice mingled and married with the poets'. [12] He raised the great tank still called Mūñja Sāgar at Dhar, and he gathered about him a court of luminaries — the poet Padmagupta, the dramatist Dhanañjaya, the scholar Halāyudha — who would shine on long after his own light had gone out. [13]

Mūñja's brother **Sindhurāja** came after him and held the kingdom together by main strength. He too led the elephants down into Gujarat, and he too kept a poet near at his elbow: Padmagupta wrote the *Navasāhasāṅkacarita*, a courtly epic, to crown Sindhurāja's marriage into a Nāga princely family. [14] But Sindhurāja's chief gift to history was, quite simply, his son.

### Bhoja, and the golden noon

With **Bhoja**, who came to the throne around the year 1000 and reigned roughly half a century, to about 1055, the Paramāras climbed to their very summit — and Bhoja himself became something rarer and stranger than any merely successful king. He became a legend. He became the very type and pattern of the philosopher-prince, so famous that an everyday Hindi proverb still measures the gulf between the great and the lowly by the sound of his name: *"Kahāṅ Rājā Bhoj, kahāṅ Gaṅgū Telī"* — "Where stands King Bhoja, and where the humble oil-presser?" [15]

Bhoja was a soldier; he practised at the bow, kept the great elephant army, and fought upon several frontiers at once. [16] But it is for his mind, and for the works of his mind, that the centuries have held him close. The tradition credits him with an output so vast it strains belief — some eighty-odd books, ranging across astronomy, medicine, poetics, grammar, architecture, religious philosophy, and even the veterinary care of horses. Among the titles laid to his name are the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* on architecture, the *Sarasvatīkaṇṭhābharaṇa* on poetics, the *Rājamārtaṇḍa* commentary on the Yoga aphorisms, and the *Rājamṛgāṅka* on astronomy and medicine. [17] Modern scholars rightly doubt that one man, however luminous his gifts, set down all of these with his own hand; many were likely composed by a whole stable of learned men labouring beneath his name and under his direction. Only one of them, the *Rājamṛgāṅka*, even carries a date of composition — the equivalent of 1042–43. [18] But that very ambiguity, I think, is the heart of the matter. Under Bhoja the boundary between the king and his court of scholars had grown so faint, so porous, that the entire intellectual harvest of Malwa could be gathered up and filed beneath a single royal signature.

And what a court it was — what a gathering. The *Udaipur praśasti* calls him, with simple finality, "the king of poets." [19] Mughal-era memory, in Abū'l-Faẓl's *Āʾīn-i Akbarī*, gazed back across five whole centuries and reported still: "Bhoja held wisdom in honour, the learned men were treated with distinction, and seekers after knowledge were encouraged by his support. Five hundred sages, the most erudite of the age, shone as the gathered wisdom of his court, and were entertained in a manner becoming their dignity and merit." [20] He bestowed honorific titles upon scholars as a gardener scatters seed — conferring "Vidyāpati," lord of learning, upon the poet Bhāskarabhaṭṭa — and his name became a lodestone, drawing students and writers out of the neighbouring kingdoms to settle in Malwa, until at last the later anthologists, unable even to keep their own chronology straight, began heaping famous poets of every age into his entourage simply because that was where greatness was assumed to dwell. [21] His benevolence reached out beyond even his own Brahmanical faith: under the gentling influence of the Jaina poet Dhanapāla he is remembered as well-disposed toward the Jainas — a sign of that religious tolerance which helped make his Malwa a gathering-place, a refuge, rather than a battleground of creeds. [22]

Tradition makes even the Kamāl Maulā mosque at Dhar — its name now grown wholly Islamic — a college that Bhoja built, and the claim is lent real weight by two remarkable charts found carved upon its walls: the Sanskrit alphabet laid out along the winding body of a single serpent, its very tail bearing the endings that nouns and verbs put on, and the rules of Sanskrit grammar coiled along two intertwined snakes — teaching-aids, these, set up for the pupils who once studied there, and through them we are granted a glimpse, across nearly a thousand years, of the actual method by which children were once taught their letters. Identical charts turn up at the Mahākāla temple in Ujjain and elsewhere besides, so this was no quirk of Dhar alone but a whole pedagogy, breathing across the length and breadth of the kingdom. [23]

The same restless, tireless energy poured itself into stone and into water. Bhoja rebuilt Dhārā into the splendid capital that bore his stamp for centuries to come. [24] South of modern Bhopal he is credited with the founding of the city of Bhojpur and with the damming of a whole valley to summon up the **Bhojpur lake**, once among the very largest artificial lakes in all of India — a vast sheet of held water, restrained by colossal masonry dams of mortarless stone fitted so tightly, stone kissing stone, as to be watertight: an achievement of engineering that travellers still marvelled at long after the lake itself had been drained away to dust. [25] A pretty legend says that Bhoja built it to heal himself of a deadly illness, on a physician's counsel that he bathe in water gathered from three hundred and sixty-five streams; the engineers scoured the Vindhyas, found a site fed by three hundred and fifty-nine springs, and diverted a river to make up the missing difference. [26] The story is fanciful. The dams were real.

Malwa under Bhoja, in the end, came as close to the ideal of a Hindu state as its admirers could ever dream — prosperous, orderly, devout, and luminous through and through with learning. A couplet long bound to his name catches the very way that later memory framed his whole reign: in his lifetime, *"Adya Dhārā sadādhārā"* — "Today Dhārā has a firm support"; and on the day of his death, the same words turned cruelly inside out — *"Adya Dhārā nirādhārā"* — "Today Dhārā is left without support." [27] The pun is untranslatable; the grief is not. For with Bhoja gone, the support did indeed give way beneath them all.

### The kingdom that nearly died

For Bhoja's death threw wide the floodgates. A kingdom that shines abroad as a beacon of culture gleams just as brightly, alas, as a target, and Malwa's neighbours had nursed their resentment of Paramāra power for long generations. [GAP — the specific events of the crisis immediately after Bhoja's death (c. 1055), including the often-cited combined assault on Malwa and the sack of Dhārā, are not narrated in the sources consulted for this chapter; the standard dynastic histories treat them at length and a fuller account belongs to them.]

What the sources before me do make unmistakable is the longer pattern into which that crisis fed, like a single dark current joining a wider flood. From the later eleventh century onward, Malwa was caught in a vice, its jaws closing slowly from either side. To the west pressed the **Cālukyas of Gujarat** — the Caulukyas, or Solaṅkīs, of Anhilwar — whose kings warred against the Paramāras for generation upon generation; the Gujarat ruler Jayasiṃha would in time take Dhārā by storm and carry a Paramāra king away captive to his own capital. [28] To the south, across the Narmadā, loomed the Deccan powers, first the Cālukyas of Kalyāṇī and later the **Yādavas of Devagiri**, whose armies raided Malwa again and again and yet again; their inscriptions boast, with a grim and tolling repetition, of Yādava kings who were "a blast of the day of doom in extinguishing the lamps of the Mālavas." [29] And gathering on the northern horizon — though its full weight would not crash down until the thirteenth century — was the new power of the Delhi Sultanate. The story of the dynasty's last century, as its own historians tell it, becomes "one long series of tales of rapid deterioration and collapse," the story of a kingdom whose very nervous system was shattered by invasion after invasion until at last it broke down altogether. [30]

That collapse was still whole generations away, the far end-point of a long, slow erosion driven on by enemies upon every side. [31] But the vice had already begun to close, and the Paramāra house had now to find a king strong enough to hold its iron jaws apart. It found one. It found Udayāditya.

### Udayāditya, the steadier of the kingdom

He arrives in our records less as a conqueror than as a restorer — the man who set the reeling kingdom back upon its feet. The very texture of his reign, in all that survives of it, is the texture of consolidation: coins, building, the patient, unglamorous reassertion of order, like a man quietly setting a household to rights after a storm has passed through it.

Consider the coins. Gold pieces, struck (so it seems) in Udayāditya's reign, have been recovered from the Central Provinces, modelled upon the currency of the Kalachuri king Gāṅgeyadeva and bearing on the obverse the legend *Śrīmad-Udaya-deva*. [32] A king who can issue good gold coinage is a king whose treasury and whose authority have steadied beneath him. Consider, too, the very language his own inscriptions reach for when they speak of him: Udayāditya, together with his son and successor Naravarman, "declared that their swords were ever ready to protect *varṇa*" — to defend the very order of caste and society that a long bout of invasion and disorder had threatened to tear apart. [33] This is the self-image of a restorer, of a king who saw his whole task as the mending of a frayed and fraying social and political fabric.

He bound the dynasty back into the wide web of Indian royalty through marriage, giving a daughter to the Guhila king of Mewar — a connection that tied Malwa fast to one of the great houses of Rajputana. [34] And like every Paramāra worthy of the name, he built — not merely for use, but for memory, which is the truer reason men build at all. Udayāditya founded a whole new city thirty miles north of Bhilsa and, keeping faith with an old Paramāra habit, named it after his own self: **Udayapur**, "the city of Udaya." [35] He is credited as well with a great tank, the Udayasamudra, now lost to us. [36] A charming folk-legend grew up about the city's founding — a tale of the king swallowing a grateful but treacherous snake, of a second snake set to guard buried treasure, and of a quick-witted queen who overheard the two serpents quarrelling and so saved her husband's life — but the inscriptions are plain and quiet on the matter: the city was Udayāditya's deliberate foundation, no hunter's lucky accident. [37]

And there, at Udayapur, he raised the temple this book is about. The record is precise just where precision counts: the great Śiva temple was built by Udayāditya, and an inscription preserved at the site itself dates the work to the equivalent of 1059. [38] (The reader may notice that the older surveys call this shrine the Nīlakaṇṭheśvara temple, "Lord of the Blue Throat" — one of Śiva's many names — while the kingdom's other inscriptions glory in the god under a whole litany of titles besides, Mahākāla and Amareśvara and Siddhanātha among them; yet the name by which we know it best, and the meaning Chapter 1 has already drawn out of its depths, is the Udayeśvara, the Rising Lord. [39]) It is enough here to say that the temple was no isolated act of private piety. It was the crowning work of a reign given wholly over to the putting-back-together of Malwa — a Paramāra king doing, in stone and in ritual, exactly what his great ancestors Mūñja and Bhoja had done before the long night of the invasions came down: declaring, through sheer magnificence, that the dynasty endured.

For the Paramāras were, before and beneath all else, devout worshippers of Śiva. The list of kings who poured their wealth into his worship reads like a roll-call of the whole house — Sīyaka-Harṣa, Vākpati, Bhoja, and now Udayāditya, who "built a temple to Śiva at Udayapur." [40] In raising the Rising Lord, Udayāditya was at once the heir of Bhoja's golden age and the very answer to the crisis that had followed it: a king who had steadied a reeling kingdom, and who marked the recovery by building one of the most beautiful temples in all of India.

The life that "the ever-memorable Mūñja and Bhoja" had breathed into the dynasty would, in the fullness of time, be blighted; the long defeat of the thirteenth century lay ahead, waiting, and the Paramāras would not escape it. [41] But that is a later chapter of the dynasty's history, and not the one that gave us our temple. The moment that gave us the temple was this one: a kingdom saved, a king named for the rising sun, and a hillside above the Betwa cleared and waiting for the work of the masons. To that work — the building of the Udayeśvara itself — we now turn.

# Chapter 3 — Raising the Udayeśvara: Patron, Date, and the Udayapur Praśasti

## I. A Foundation Sealed Thrice in Stone

Most cities gather; a few are decreed — and Udayapur, surely, belongs to that second and rarer kind, a place that did not pool slowly at a crossroads or a ford the way water finds its level, but was summoned, all at once, into being by a single royal act and given, from the very first breath of its life, a single royal name.[1] Its founder was Udayāditya, the Paramāra king of Malwa, and what he raised on this sun-struck stretch of the Vidisha plateau was no lone temple but a trinity conceived in one imagining: a walled city, a great temple to Śiva set burning at its heart, and beside them — vast, patient, gathering the sky — an artificial lake to cradle the monsoon and carry the town, gulp by measured gulp, through the long parched months.[2]

The three foundations breathe one name. The city is **Udayapura**, "the city of Udaya." The temple's god is **Udayeśvara**, the "Rising Lord." The reservoir is the **Udayasamudra**, the "sea of Udaya." And folded, like a secret signature, into all three is the king's own name — Udayāditya, in which *udaya* means "rising," as the sun rises out of the dark, and *āditya* is the sun itself, blazing and entire. To found this place, then, was to sign it. A traveller arriving from the west would pass through ramparts of giant uncemented stone, would stoop to drink from a lake that bore the king's name, and would bow at last before a god who bore it too.[3] City, temple, and tank: one gesture, one breath of intention, stamped three times over with the imprint of a single man.

And we should pause — we who come hurrying for the temple — upon the lake, because the modern visitor, eyes lifted to the spire, can walk past those waters without ever grasping what they meant. This is semi-arid central India, a country of fierce monsoon downpour followed by long thirsting months, where the upper Betwa runs hard in the wet season and thins to a whisper, or to nothing, after; a king who would plant a city here had first to teach it how to drink, and the **Udayasamudra** — excavated beside the temple at Udayāditya's command — was the very engine that made the foundation possible at all.[4] For in the old Indian understanding of kingship, the digging of a great tank was itself a sacred and meritorious thing, a public benefaction set upon the same moral height as the raising of a temple. Udayāditya did both at a stroke, and named both for himself. The "sea of Udaya" was no ornament beside the holy city. It was the condition of its life — the held breath that let the place go on breathing.

This chapter, then, is about the making of that foundation: about the man who willed it, the years of labour it drank, the dates its own stones still carry like rings in living wood, and the praise-poem the eleventh century carved to explain him to all who would come after. In the chapters before, the temple has stood as a finished thing, a building to be read. Here, instead, we watch it rise.

## II. The Patron: Udayāditya Paramāra

By the time Udayāditya came to the throne, the Paramāra house was old, illustrious, and in trouble — three things that seldom keep such easy company. Its capital was Dhar; its greatest name was **Bhoja**, that scholar-king of the early eleventh century whose court became a byword for learning, and under whose patronage the architectural treatise the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* — one of the earliest texts to name the very Bhūmija style in which the Udayeśvara would one day be built — was compiled.[5] But Bhoja's death gave way to hard and narrowing years. The Paramāra kingdom, pressed on every side by its neighbours, had all but come apart at the seams, and it fell to Udayāditya to gather the scattered threads and weave them whole again.

He was, in the steady verdict of the sources, the king who restored Paramāra fortunes.[6] Anupa Pande, whose decade-long study of the temple stands like bedrock beneath much of this book, puts it without flourish: Udayāditya "faced many challenges in consolidating his empire," and it was beneath his hand that Malwa recovered its footing and "witnessed political stability and flourished as a leading center of religious and cultural activities."[7] The recovery and the foundation belong together, as cause and its crown. A king does not found a new city and lift a masterpiece above it in a season of collapse; he does such a thing only when he has wrested enough peace to build. Udayapur is the monument of a recovery — a kingdom risen again from its knees, given permanent form, given flesh, in red sandstone.

Here, though, we meet the first of the difficulties that honest history cannot smooth away, and would not wish to: the sources do not quite agree on when Udayāditya's reign began. Pande's study has him "ascend the throne in 1070 CE."[8] But D. R. Patil's survey of the cultural heritage of the old Madhya Bharat state, and the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* in its wake, set his reign earlier — Patil's chapters give dates of roughly 1058–1087, and the *Gazetteer*'s history simply names Udayāditya as the successor of Bhoja who "built the magnificent Nilkanthesvar temple at Udaypur."[9] The gap is real and will not be wished closed: an accession around 1059 against one around 1070, more than a decade lying between. It is the kind of discrepancy that rises, naturally as mist, from the very nature of the evidence — regnal dates in this age are reconstructed from scattered inscriptions and genealogies, and reasonable scholars, bending over the same fragments, reach slightly different reckonings. We need not resolve it to tell the story; we need only hold it gently up to the light. What matters is that the temple's *own* dates, as we shall see, are far firmer than its founder's accession — for the temple carved them into itself, and stone keeps faith.

One thing more the sources let us glimpse: the king's temperament, or at least the face it turned to the world. He was a Śaiva, a worshipper of Śiva, and the god he installed at the heart of his city was Śiva under a name of his own coining. Yet the temple's sculpture, read in the chapters before, whispers of a particular emphasis. Among the many forms of Śiva carved upon the walls is **Mṛtyuñjaya**, the "Conqueror of Death," seated deep in meditation and wreathed in serpents, a form invoked — in Pande's reading of the iconography — to grant victory and to overcome the danger of death upon the battlefield.[10] For a king who had spent his very strength holding a contested throne, the choice is telling, almost confessional. The Udayeśvara is the temple of a fighter who wished his god to be a conqueror — and who wished, with a most human and particular dread, not to die in battle.

## III. The Twenty-One-Year Workshop

We can date the building of the Udayeśvara with a precision rare for an eleventh-century Indian monument, and we can do so because the temple is, in part, its own archive — a keeper of its own memory. Its porches and walls carry inscriptions, and from them two dates rise to the surface, bracketing the whole long enterprise like dawn and dusk about a single day.

Construction was begun, by the temple's own testimony, in **Vikrama Saṃvat 1116** — which is to say **A.D. 1059**. The Vikrama Saṃvat, or *saṃvat* for short, is the Indian calendar era most often spoken in these inscriptions; it runs roughly fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era, so that subtracting about fifty-seven from a Vikrama year yields the Christian date. V.S. 1116 falls thus in A.D. 1059, the year the masons first broke the patient ground.[11] And the work was crowned — its great victory-flag lifted, at the last, upon the very summit of the tower — in **V.S. 1137**, A.D. **1080**.[12]

Between those two dates lies a span of twenty-one years: twenty-one years of a royal workshop bent over a single building. That is the human fact behind the finished stone — the warm and aching truth of it. The Udayeśvara was not flung up in haste; it was the sustained devotion of more than two decades, long enough that a master who laid the foundations as a young man might have stood, grey now, to see the flag raised in his maturity, long enough for the carving that so stirred the early surveyors — sculpture the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* judged "finer than Khajuraho" — to be brought to its high and breathing finish.[13] When Chapter 1 marked the small human figure carved clambering up the north-west face of the spire, a flag-bearer frozen forever mid-ascent with the victory banner he means to plant at the very top, it was gazing upon the iconographic echo of this very moment: completion itself, the *dhvaja* raised, the long labour laid at last to rest.[14]

And it is worth dwelling, a while, on what such a workshop meant for the place that grew up around it. The later memory of Udayapur has kept one small, telling thing. Among the satellite structures of the town the *Gazetteer* records the **Pisanari-ka-Mandir** — the "grinding-woman's temple" — said to have been built by a poor woman from the earnings she made grinding flour for the temple's masons.[15] The story may be legend; but legends of this kind keep a real social truth folded inside them like grain in the husk. A royal building project of twenty-one years was an economy as well as a monument — a living thing that drew labour to itself, fed it and paid it, and threw off surplus enough that even a flour-grinder at the very bottom of the chain could, in the end, endow a small shrine of her own. The great temple sat at the centre of a wide and quiet web of lesser making.

The dating evidence deserves one note of caution and one of corroboration. The caution: the inscriptions are not all neatly dated, and the surviving epigraphic record at Udayapur is more tangled than a single tidy pair of dates would suggest. The corroboration, though, is striking. The standard corpus of Paramāra inscriptions lists, for the reign of Udayāditya, two stone records at Udayapur itself — one assigned to A.D. 1080, and one explicitly dated **V.S. 1137 / A.D. 1080**.[16] The flag-raising date, then, is no lone and lonely tradition; it is anchored in the catalogued epigraphy of the dynasty. The temple's own claim about its completion is confirmed from beyond itself — voice answering voice across the centuries.

That same corpus lets us watch Udayapur go on speaking long after its founder had fallen silent. The town Udayāditya raised became a place where later Paramāra kings, too, pressed their words into stone: Udayāditya's successor **Naravarman** in V.S. 1151 / A.D. 1094, and a long procession of later rulers after him — a Devapāla in the thirteenth century, a Jayasiṃha in the early fourteenth, V.S. 1366 / A.D. 1309 — each adding his own record to the town's slowly accumulating stone.[17] A city founded as one king's signature became, across two centuries and a half, a dynastic place of record — proof, in stone, that the foundation took root, that Udayapur lived on as something far more than a single reign's vanity. The temple was a magnet for memory: kings came to it to be remembered, generation after generation, exactly as its founder must have ached and hoped they would.

The fabric of the town bears out the same picture — a place that grew up around its great temple and kept on growing, like a tree thickening ring by ring. Besides the Pisanari-ka-Mandir, the *Gazetteer* records at Udayapur a cluster of further structures: a pillared hall of the eleventh century known as the **Barakhambi**, the "twelve-pillared"; the **Bijamandal** or *Ghadiyalan-ka-makan*, the "clockman's house," bound up with the invocation of the Sun-god; a cavalry stepwell, the *Ghod-daud-ki-baodi*; and, from the later centuries of the town's life, a succession of mosques.[18] Not all of these belong to the foundation moment, and several rise out of the very different world of the Sultanate and Mughal centuries. Yet taken together they show what Udayāditya's act had set in motion: not a lone temple stranded in an empty field, but a living town — walled, watered, worshipping — that went on building for centuries after the royal workshop had packed away its chisels and the dust had settled on the silent yard.

## IV. The Udayapur Praśasti

If the dated inscriptions tell us *when* the temple was built, another inscription tells us — in the high rhetorical register of its age — *why*, or at least how the act of building was meant to be understood, and held, and remembered. This is the **Udayapur Praśasti**.

The word *praśasti* means "praise" — a praise-inscription, a panegyric, a poem of laudation cut into stone so that admiration itself might outlast the admired. The genre was a fixture of medieval Indian royal culture. When a king founded a temple or a city, or won a war, or wished to fix his glory against the erosions of forgetting, he commissioned a court poet to compose a praśasti: a Sanskrit poem, often of considerable length and learning, that set out his lineage, rehearsed his virtues and his victories, and bound his name fast to the pious work his hands had performed. The praśasti is part genealogy, part eulogy, part dedication — and it is, for the modern historian, among the most precious of all sources, because it is the very voice in which a dynasty wished to be remembered, carved by the dynasty's own hand.[19]

The Udayapur Praśasti is exactly such a poem, set upon the temple Udayāditya raised. Its governing image — and this is the thing to grasp, the key that turns the whole — is the sun. We have already met the logic at its root: the king's name is Udayāditya, the "rising sun"; his city, his god, his lake all carry *udaya*, the rising, within their names. The praśasti takes that solar logic and makes it sing. It does not merely call the king a great warrior; it makes of him a second sun, and it builds its central conceit out of the most ancient of all Indian metaphors — light against the dark, the sun dispelling the long night.

The single line that survives most vividly in the sources, the line Chapter 1 quoted in part, runs in full thus:

> "...another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword, and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."[20]

It repays slow reading, for every element of it is doing deliberate and shining work. The king is "another sun" — a second *āditya*, which is, of course, his own name unfolded into a simile, a name turned inside out to become an image. His enemies are "dense darkness," the natural antithesis of the sun, and the conceit lets the poet praise the foes even as the king annihilates them: they are "magnanimous," a touch of chivalry that magnifies the victor precisely by dignifying the vanquished. The "rays issuing from his strong sword" fuse the two registers of the image at a single stroke — the sword's flash *is* the sun's ray, martial and solar at once, so that the very act of war becomes an act of illumination, slaughter transfigured into dawn. And the closing turn is the whole political point of the thing: by destroying the darkness, the king is "gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour." The sun that scorches his enemies is the selfsame sun that warms his subjects. Here is a precise poetic statement of the ideal of kingship the temple was built to embody — the king as the very fountainhead of light, whose violence against his foes is the very thing that gives his people their peace and their joy.

We should be careful, even so, about what such a poem can and cannot tell us. A praśasti is not a chronicle; it is an idealised, ornamental, frankly partisan portrait, composed to flatter and to last. Its description of Udayāditya as a world-conquering second sun is conventional hyperbole, the standard idiom in which any successful medieval king was praised, and we cannot read off from it a literal ledger of battles won.[21] But it is not therefore worthless — far from it. Read for what it *is* — a statement of royal self-presentation — the Udayapur Praśasti tells us a great deal. It tells us that Udayāditya wished to be remembered as a restorer, a bringer of light after the dark, which fits to the very seam the historical situation of a king who hauled the Paramāra kingdom back from its edge. It tells us that the solar imagery was no accident of naming but a fully worked-out programme, a single idea — *udaya*, the rising — drawn out from the king's name through his city, his god, his lake, and on into the poetry carved upon his temple's walls. And it confirms, from the inside, what the architecture proclaims from without: that this whole foundation was conceived as a sunrise made permanent, a kingdom's recovery held forever at the trembling instant of dawn.

Here, too, we must be honest about the limits of our sources. The full text of the Udayapur Praśasti — its length, its complete genealogy of the Paramāra line, the particular victories and gifts it may name — is not preserved in the materials underlying this book; what survives to us here is the single, justly famous solar line and the framework of solar imagery around it. [GAP — the complete text and detailed content of the Udayapur Praśasti are not in the sources consulted for this chapter.] What we can say with confidence is what that surviving line, and the foundation it adorns, were designed to mean.

## V. The Meaning of the Making

Step back from the dates and the poem, and the shape of the whole enterprise comes clear, as a coastline comes clear when the boat draws off from shore. Udayāditya did something of unusual coherence. He took a single idea — rising, the sunrise, his own name — and he built it out at every scale at once: into the name of a city, into the name of a god, into the name of a lake, into a praise-poem that made of him a second sun, and into the very architecture of a temple whose Bhūmija tower was designed to look as though it grew straight up out of the earth and strained toward the sky. The Udayeśvara is sometimes described, in this book and elsewhere, as a building conceived as a single upward gesture. The making of it, traced here, shows that the gesture reached far beyond the stone. The temple is the largest and most lasting utterance of a programme that ran, like a thread of light, through an entire royal foundation.

And the programme carried a political edge that the praśasti makes plain. This was the work of a king who had inherited a kingdom in trouble and carried it back into safety; the imagery of darkness dispelled and light restored was no idle court flattery but a claim about what his reign had truly achieved. To found Udayapur — city, temple, and "sea of Udaya" together — and to crown it with twenty-one years of a royal workshop's patient labour, was to make that claim permanent, to set it past the reach of forgetting. The dated stones tell us the labour ran from 1059 to 1080; the praśasti tells us what those two decades were *for*. They were for fixing, in the most durable medium an eleventh-century king could command, the memory of a kingdom that had risen again into the light.

It worked. The dynasty that built the temple did not outlast the medieval centuries; the city itself dwindled, in the end, to the modest walled village a traveller finds today. But the foundation held its meaning, as a vessel holds water. Later Paramāra kings came back to Udayapur to carve their own records; later restorers, Maratha and modern, kept the Rising Lord in worship. Nine hundred years on, the sun the poet promised has not set. The temple still stands on the Malwa plateau exactly as its founder meant it to stand — a permanent sunrise, in red stone, carrying his name.

# Chapter 4 — The Bhūmija Vision: Plan and Architecture

## I. A Style Born from the Earth

Every great building belongs to a family, and before we can hope to see what is singular in the Udayeśvara — before we can love it for the one thing it is — we must first sit awhile with the kin from which it springs; and the name of that kin is a strange and beautiful one, a word that comes to us almost like a small song carried out of the medieval dark: **Bhūmija**. It is a word that repays the patient unpacking, for it is not merely a label that scholars, centuries after, pinned like a tag to a cluster of temples — it is a statement, made by the builders themselves, about what these buildings, in their innermost being, *were*.[1]

*Bhūmija* means, quite literally, "born from the earth," or "earth-produced," and the wonder of it is that the single word opens, like a seed, into three meanings at once. Read one way, the name is geographical: it marks a regional manner, the native style of Malwa, set against the temple modes of Gujarat, of Orissa, of the Deccan. Read a second way, it is almost a theology whispered in stone. For the standard medieval taxonomy of temple forms held that the loftiest building-types carried a divine, a celestial origin — that the gods themselves had first dwelt in such structures, and that men, ever the imitators, merely copied down the heavenly pattern; and against that shining company of sky-born forms the Bhūmija stood apart, the terrestrially evolved type, the order that had grown up out of the very ground of a particular country rather than descending, ready-made and gleaming, from heaven.[2]

But there is a third reading, and it is the one that matters most for the waking eye. *Bhūmi* means a storey, a level, a tier — and the Bhūmija tower is, before it is anything else under the sun, a thing of stacked storeys. Its spire is built of miniature spires, ranged in rows one above another, climbing in diminishing tiers from the ground to the summit, so that the whole *śikhara* — the tower — seems to rise directly out of the earth, level upon level, a single green growth pushing upward toward the light. *Bhūmija*: born from the earth, and built of storeys that climb from it. The name and the form say the same thing. This, surely, is the deepest sense in which the temple at Udayapur is the temple of the Rising Lord.[3] Its very style is a style of rising.

The Bhūmija order seems to have crystallised in Malwa, and the Paramāra kings — those patient cultivators of stone — brought it to its full and confident maturity. By the second half of the eleventh century, when the Udayeśvara was rising, the style was so completely worked out, so sure of itself, that it must have had a long apprenticeship of years behind it; for styles, like rivers, do not arrive fully formed at the sea — they are fed from far springs. And here the texts and the buildings speak with one voice. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, that great architectural treatise compiled under the Paramāra king Bhoja, is among the earliest texts to name the Bhūmija mode; and the temples themselves cluster thickly in exactly the lands the Paramāras and their neighbours ruled — Malwa, Rajasthan, the northern Deccan of Maharashtra.[4] So that when the great art historian Stella Kramrisch came, in 1946, to define and classify this regional style, she reached, out of all the buildings in that country, for one above the others to stand as its type-specimen, its classic example, the touchstone against which every other might be measured — and that building was the temple at Udayapur.[5] What follows in this chapter is, in truth, nothing more than a long and grateful looking at why she chose it.

## II. The Square That Turns: A Plan Made of Rotation

To understand the Udayeśvara, do not begin with the tower; begin, rather, with the ground — with the quiet shape the walls trace upon the earth. For it is here, in the plan, in the figure drawn in the dust before a single stone was lifted, that the building first announces its singular logic; and it is a logic unlike that of almost any temple a traveller will have met before.

The standard way of designing a North Indian temple plan — the manner art historians call **Nāgara** — begins, as you might expect, with a square. The square is the sacred figure, the perfect and stable form, the form at rest; the inner sanctum is square, and the outer walls are generated from it by a patient series of projections and recesses, like a grid worked outward into the air. The architect adds offsets to the faces of the square — a central projection on each side (the *bhadra*), then subsidiary projections flanking it, then the corner — and if you count the resulting vertical facets you have the temple's "ratha" number: *tri-ratha* (three-faceted), *pañca-ratha* (five-faceted), *sapta-ratha* (seven-faceted). It is, in its essence, a square enriched by stepping its sides in and out, and the eye, however the surface is broken, still reads the steady square beneath.[6]

The Bhūmija plan does something else entirely — and the difference, let us be clear, is not of degree but of kind. Instead of subdividing a square like a careful grid, the Bhūmija architect *rotates* it. He takes a square and turns it about its own centre — the very operation the texts call *parivartanā*, "turning around" — stopping the rotation at regular intervals and recording, at each pause, the position of the turning square's corners. And as the square pivots, its four corners sweep out a circle; the points it leaves behind at each halt map the line that the exterior walls will faithfully follow. The result is a plan that is neither a simple square nor a simple circle but a marriage of the two — a figure that is, in the language of the sources, **stellate-cum-circular**: star-shaped where the corners thrust boldly out, yet gathered onto a circle that governs and quiets the whole.[7]

Take a moment, I beg you, to feel what this means, for it is the secret engine of the entire design. A grid-built plan is, at heart, static: a square is a square, planted four-square upon the earth, settled, at rest. But a plan generated by rotation is, at heart, *dynamic* — it carries, frozen forever into its geometry, the living memory of a turning motion. The walls of the Udayeśvara do not merely stand; they seem to wheel, to have spun outward from the still centre of the sanctum like rays flung off a turning hub. The plan is a movement caught and held in stone. And it is the very same movement, repeated upward into the air, that will become the climbing storeys of the tower. Earth-plan and sky-tower are born of a single idea.

In the particular figures of the Udayeśvara, the rotation is stopped so as to yield a sanctum that is *sapta-ratha* — seven-faceted — on plan, and the elevation answers it, note for note, with seven tiers, *saptabhūmi*, seven storeys, in the rise of the tower.[8] Seven on the ground, seven in the air: the building rhymes with itself from foundation to finial. This is why Chapter 1 could offer, as the headline fact about the temple's shape, that its plan is "rotated" and *saptaratha*; and here at last we can see what lies behind that headline — that the rotation is no decorative flourish but the very generative act of the whole composition, the single geometric decision out of which all the rest unfolds, as a tree unfolds from one quiet choice made in the dark of the seed.

One consequence of the design deserves to be spoken plainly, for it governs how a worshipper meets the building face to face. Temples of the Bhūmija class are, almost without exception, **nirandhara** — "without a passage." There is no enclosed ambulatory corridor running around the sanctum within the walls, no dim circuit for the devotee to pace around the god in the inner dark. The wall of the sanctum is, in effect, a single thickness; the faceted exterior we have been tracing *is* itself the outer face of the sanctum wall.[9] And this has a profound effect upon the elevation, as we shall presently see: with no ambulatory to wrap and cushion, the wall can be thinner, the buttresses sharper, the whole exterior more vertical and more knife-edged. The plan's economy becomes the tower's very energy.

## III. From the Ground Up: The Base and the Wall

If the plan is the building's whispered secret, the elevation is its public argument — the upward sentence it speaks aloud to anyone who stands before it in the morning light. The Udayeśvara makes that sentence in clearly marked stages, each with its own register of mouldings, and the discipline of reading them is, in truth, the discipline of watching the building climb. We move from the bottom up, because that is the one direction the whole design insists upon.

At the foot of the temple is the **pīṭha**, the plinth or base — the platform of mouldings upon which the wall proper stands. At Udayapur it is built of five distinct horizontal courses, and they ascend in a calculated crescendo of ornament, from plain to rich, like a melody gathering its voices. The lowest course (*bhiṭṭa*) is severe and unadorned, the bedrock note struck low. Above it a second *bhiṭṭa* breaks into carved leaf and garland work, capped by a "shoulder" course of lotus petals. Then comes the **jāḍyakumbha**, a bold moulding cut in the profile of an inverted cyma — a lotus-shaped swell, detailed with rosettes along its flanks and a fringe of little caitya-window motifs across its top. Above that, a sharp knife-edged moulding, the **karṇaka**, carries an arrow-and-spiral band; and crowning the whole base there runs the **grāsapaṭṭi**, a frieze of grinning gorgon-faces — *grāsa* or *kīrtimukha*, the "face of glory," the swallowing mask that Indian builders set at thresholds to devour the evil that would creep in.[10] Already, before the wall has so much as begun, the building has taught the eye its method: a vertical sequence of bands, each one different, each more elaborate than the last, marching the patient gaze upward.

Above the plinth stands the **maṇḍovara** — the wall, the very body of the temple between base and roofline. This is the zone where the building shows its gods to the world, and it too is read as a stack of horizontal members rising, at length, into a field of vertical ones. Its lower part is the **vedibandha**, the "altar-binding," a girdle of three principal mouldings: the *khura* (a hoof-shaped member, banded here with a row of cut diamonds), the **kumbha** (a swelling pot-shaped moulding, ornamented with tiny shrine-niches that house small deities, with its own diamond band and lotus-petal shoulder), and the *kalaśa* (a rounded torus, a "pitcher" moulding, hemmed between fillets and carrying that same arrow-and-spiral band we met below).[11] And how the names themselves tell their quiet story — *khura*, hoof; *kumbha*, pot; *kalaśa*, pitcher — for the Indian moulding vocabulary is drawn, with tender homeliness, from the ordinary furniture of ritual life, the vessels and the altars of daily worship, stacked up and lifted into the very structure of the divine house.

Then, above this girdle, the wall changes its character utterly. The lower mouldings were horizontal, encircling bands, arms laid round the building; but now the surface breaks upward into **the jaṅghā**, the upper wall, and here the design turns vertical with a kind of vengeance. The faceted plan we traced upon the ground now stands up into space as a row of tall, pillar-like buttresses — the projecting facets of the rotated square, risen at last into the wall as slender vertical shafts. Each is treated as a faceted pilaster, and each frames a deep, beautifully carved niche, a **khaṭṭaka**, in which a major god or goddess stands.[12] Between the buttresses fall deep shadowed recesses, so that the wall is no longer a flat and lifeless skin but a corrugated, breathing, rhythmic alternation of jutting light and sunken dark — projection, recess, projection, recess, around the whole turning circuit of the building. (The deities who dwell in these niches — the guardians of the directions, the dancing forms of Śiva, the great goddesses — are the burden of the next chapter; here we are concerned only with the architecture that holds and cradles them.) At their tops the buttresses are crowned with capital-like mouldings, an ornamental fillet, a recess, and a projecting eave-moulding (*chādya*) that throws its clean line of shadow across the wall.[13]

And now comes the single most important fact about the elevation of the Udayeśvara — the fact that turns a fine wall into a Bhūmija masterpiece. **The vertical buttresses do not stop at the roofline.** The pillar-like shafts of the *jaṅghā* run straight up off the top of the wall and on into the tower above, where they transform — without a break, without a stammer, as one continuous and unbroken gesture — into the climbing rows of miniature spires that compose the *śikhara*. One member of the elevation, beginning humbly at the foot of the wall, rises through the whole height of the building to the summit. Anupa Pande's account fixes the image precisely: the buttress-lines, continued upward, become rows of little spires that hang "one below the other," resembling "long pearl strings suspended from the spire."[14] Hold that picture close, for it is the whole building in a single phrase. A string of pearls is exactly what the eye sees on the great tower — vertical chains of small bright forms, threaded from sky to earth, each pearl a tiny spire, the whole chain hanging like a necklace down the curving flank of the *śikhara*. The wall and the tower are not two things merely joined; they are one continuous upward growth, and the pearl-strings are the very thread that ties them.

## IV. The Climbing Tower: The Śikhara and Its Strings of Pearls

So at last we arrive at the tower itself — the **śikhara**, the great curvilinear spire that is the glory of the building and the signature of its style. And to understand it truly we must set it, for a moment, against its more famous cousins, for the Bhūmija *śikhara* is most easily seen, as we so often see ourselves, by contrast.

The classic Nāgara tower of central India — the kind that crowns the celebrated temples of Khajuraho, the Chandella masterpieces but a few days' travel to the north-east — works by *accumulation* and *clustering*. The Khajuraho spire of the developed type is a **śekharī** tower: a tall central spire wreathed about with a rising mass of half-spires and quarter-spires (*uraḥśṛṅgas* and *śṛṅgas*) clustered against its flanks, so that the whole mountainous form seems to swarm upward in a profusion of smaller peaks, a single summit attended by a teeming retinue of lesser ones. The effect is of sheer organic abundance — a mountain breeding mountains, ridge crowding upon ridge, the eye almost overwhelmed, almost drowned, by multiplicity.[15]

The Bhūmija *śikhara* achieves its height by a wholly different and, in its own way, more austere logic. It is governed not by clustering but by *the grid* — by the disciplined, rational ordering of its parts into clean rows and columns. Down the centre of each of the four cardinal faces there runs a **latā** — a flat vertical spine or band, a broad raised stripe climbing from the base of the tower to its shoulder. The four *latā*s, set to the four cardinal directions, divide the tower into four quadrants, and they are filled with a dense, exquisite lace-work of **gavākṣa** motifs — interlaced "cow's-eye" or caitya-arch ornaments, the honeycomb of little dormer-windows that is the most characteristic surface-texture of the Indian tower.[16]

But it is the *quadrants between the latās* that give the Bhūmija tower its character and its name. Each quadrant is filled — not with clustered half-spires swarming at random, but with neat ranks of complete **miniature spires**, *kūṭastambhas*, "spire-pillars," each one a tiny perfect temple-tower in its own right — arranged in orderly rows and columns, diminishing in size as they climb toward the summit. At the Udayeśvara each quadrant carries these miniature spires in a regular array: rows climbing vertically and ranks running horizontally across the curve of the face, a tidy multiplication of the same small form, growing ever smaller as it rises.[17] (The sources transmit the count of these ranks in slightly varying figures — Krishna Deva's description gives the Udayeśvara seven vertical rows and five horizontal ranks of miniature spires to each quadrant, while Pande's general account of the style gives ranges of three to five vertical and five to nine horizontal — the variation reflecting only the difference between a count of this one temple and a description of the class as a whole.)[18]

Here, then, is the visual heart of the whole matter, and the deepest meaning of the word *Bhūmija*. Where the Khajuraho tower *clusters*, the Udayeśvara tower *stacks*. Its spire is built of storeys — *bhūmis* — of miniature spires, ranged level upon level in legible rows; and because the columns of those miniature spires line up vertically all the way down and continue, as we have already seen, into the buttress-shafts of the wall below, the whole tower reads as a set of vertical chains running unbroken from summit to ground. These are the "strings of pearls": each vertical column of stacked miniature spires is a string, each spire a single pearl, hanging the length of the tower's curving flank.[19] The Khajuraho spire makes you feel the swelling, organic mass of a holy mountain; the Bhūmija spire makes you feel a clean, rational, irresistible *ascent* — order rising, storey by counted storey, from the earth into the air. It is architecture made into a single upward gesture, and the Udayeśvara is its supreme statement.

The summit completes the figure. The tower draws in at the **skandha**, the "shoulder," where the climbing miniature spires reach their smallest size and the curve gathers itself toward the top. And the star-shaped logic of the plan, carried faithfully all the way up, persists even here: each sub-quarter of the tower is finished with a five-pointed serrated edge, a little zig-zag stellate crenellation, and the same serrated rhythm is repeated in the corrugated rim of the heavy crowning member. That crowning member is the **āmalaka** — the great fluted stone disc, ribbed like the *āmalaka* fruit (the myrobalan) from which it takes its name, that caps every North Indian tower and over which, at the very last, the pot-finial and the victory-flag are raised.[20] And it is on the upper tower, on the north-west face just below the summit, that the medieval sculptors carved their most human touch of all: the small figure of a man climbing the spire — the flag-bearer, frozen forever in the act of mounting to plant the *dhvaja*, the banner of completion, at the very top — the moment, met in earlier chapters, when twenty-one years of labour came at last to their end.[21]

On the eastern, front-facing side the tower does one thing more. There the central *latā* does not simply climb the face but thrusts forward, away from the body of the tower, into a great projecting frontal mass called the **śukanāsa** — the "parrot's-beak," an antefix or fronton that juts out over the vestibule like the prow of the spire, masking and announcing the front of the building. The Bhūmija *śukanāsa* is invariably prominent, and at Udayapur it is a vast, balanced composition of sculpture in its own right (its imagery, again, belongs to the next chapter). Architecturally, its role is to give the otherwise symmetrical, radial tower a *front* — to declare which way the building faces, which way the god looks, which way the worshipper must come.[22] The *śukanāsa* faces east, toward the rising sun, toward the original grand stairway and its guardian door-keepers; and so even the tower, for all the wheeling symmetry of its plan, turns its face to the dawn.

## V. The Hall, the Roof, and the Reach for the Sky

A great Indian temple is not a single tower but a sequence of spaces strung along an axis, each roofed in its own manner, the roofs themselves forming a deliberate progression from low to high — a stairway of summits. The Udayeśvara's axis runs, from the sacred core outward: the **garbhagṛha**, the sanctum or "womb-chamber," square within, where the *liṅga* of the Rising Lord stands in its dark; the **antarāla**, the short vestibule that links sanctum to hall; the **gūḍhamaṇḍapa**, the great closed hall where worshippers gather, opened to the air on three sides by projecting balconied porches (**mukhamaṇḍapas**) to the east, the north, and the south; and, set apart on the same axis before the temple, the low flat-roofed **Vedī**, the open ritual hall. All of this rests upon the **jagati**, the lofty platform-terrace, once ringed by a girdle of eight subsidiary shrines — the **aṣṭāyatana** complex — several of them now broken.[23] These were named in Chapter 1 as the building's parts; what concerns us here is how their *roofs* contribute to the single upward argument the architecture is making.

For the design is governed throughout by a rising silhouette. The closed hall, the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa*, does not carry a flat or a simple roof but a **saṃvaraṇā** — a "bell-roof," a pyramidal roof built up of stepped, receding tiers of mouldings, ring above ring, climbing to a small finial.[24] Set this beside the soaring *śikhara* over the sanctum, and the temple presents, in profile against the sky, a deliberate ascent: the low platform, the lower stepped pyramid of the hall, and behind it the great curvilinear tower springing far higher — a procession of summits, each taller than the last, leading the eye from the gathered space of the worshippers toward the secret core where the god sits beneath the climbing spire. The bell-roof is the foothill; the *śikhara* is the peak. To approach the temple along its axis is to walk, in stone, a graded pilgrimage toward the sky.

Inside, the design turns its energy from height to depth. The great hall is carried on four massive central pillars, and these are themselves small lessons in the very geometry the whole building loves: they shift, as they rise, from square bases through polygonal mid-sections to circular ring-capitals — the very progression from *caturaśra* to *vṛtta*, squared to circular, that governs the plan outside.[25] Above them the hall is closed by a spectacular domed ceiling of deep concentric rings carved with *kīrtimukhas* and lotus-work, descending at the centre to a great hanging pendant, the **padmaśīla**, a stone lotus-bud suspended from the apex.[26] The eye, drawn up into this dome, finds the same circle the plan traced on the ground, now turned overhead and gathered to a single point. Outside, rotation throws the walls into a wheeling star; inside, that same rotation draws the ceiling into a deepening spiral. The geometry that climbs the tower descends, inverted, from the dome — and we stand between the two, held in the turning.

## VI. The Theory of the Temple: Prāsādas in the Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra

We have been describing what the medieval builders made; it remains to ask what they *thought* — how they themselves understood the temple as a category of object — and for that we may turn to a text that the Paramāras themselves produced, a treatise attributed to the very king whose dynasty perfected the Bhūmija style. This is the **Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra**, the great compendium of architecture (*vāstuśāstra*) ascribed to the Paramāra monarch Bhoja — Udayāditya's illustrious predecessor — and it offers us, in its chapters on the **prāsāda**, the temple as the eleventh century's own theorists conceived it.[27]

The word *prāsāda* means a lofty building — a palace, a mansion, and, supremely, a temple, the house of a god. And the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* opens its treatment of the subject not with a dry builder's manual but with a myth of origins; and the myth is worth our pausing over, for it reveals at a single stroke how these architects ranked their forms. In the beginning, the text says, the creator-god Brahmā fashioned five **vimānas** — five celestial cars or aerial palaces — and gave them as the dwellings of the highest gods: Vairāja, Kailāśa, Puṣpaka, Manika, and Triviṣṭapa, made of gold and inlaid with gems, sailing through the open skies.[28] From these primal five, the text says, all the manifold types of temple were derived; Brahmā created "many others," shaped severally for every god, and the *prāsādas* of the towns — built now in stone and baked brick rather than gold — are the earthly images of those heavenly originals.[29] Here, embedded in the myth like a jewel in its setting, is precisely the hierarchy against which the *Bhūmija* defined itself: the grandest forms are sky-born, divine in origin, descended from Brahmā's celestial cars. The earth-born style stands deliberately, and with a quiet pride, apart from that lineage.

And there is a further detail in the myth that speaks directly, almost intimately, to the Udayeśvara. The text glosses these primal heavenly cars as **saptabhūmi prāsādas** — "seven-storeyed palaces."[30] The seven storeys, the *saptabhūmi* elevation that the Udayeśvara's tower realises in its seven climbing tiers, is thus no arbitrary number but a figure with deep resonance in the architectural imagination: the heavenly mansion is itself a seven-storeyed thing, and a temple built *saptabhūmi* is an earthly echo of that celestial seven-fold rise. The "born-from-the-earth" style and the "seven-storeyed" heavenly model meet, in the Udayeśvara, in a single building: terrestrial in its style, celestial in its sevenfold ascent — earth reaching up, and heaven answering down.

The bulk of the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*'s treatment, however, is given over not to myth but to a vast and meticulous classification. The text enumerates **sixty-four prāsādas**, sorted with patient geometric rigour according to the shape of their ground-plan: twenty-four are *caturaśra*, square in plan; ten are *vṛtta*, circular; ten *caturaśrāyata*, square-oblong or rectangular; ten *vṛttāyata*, round-and-oblong; and ten *aṣṭāśra*, octagonal.[31] Each named type — Rucaka, Citrakūṭa, Siṃhapañjara, Bhadra, Nandyāvarta, Svastika, Meru, Mandara, and on through the long roll of sixty-four — is defined by the most exacting prescriptions: how many parts to divide the field into, where to set the central shrine, how many pillars to the hall, how many porches and on which sides, how high the door, how the boundary-walls and the lattices and the eaves are to be disposed.[32] It is a system of breathtaking comprehensiveness, an attempt — and one cannot help but marvel at the ambition of it — to map the entire possible universe of temple-forms onto an ordered grid of names and measures.

Two things in this classification illuminate the building at Udayapur. The first is its governing principle: the prāsādas are sorted, above all else, by the *shape of the plan* — square, circular, oblong, octagonal. The whole theory rests upon the conviction that a temple's identity is determined at the ground, by the figure its walls trace upon the earth — exactly the conviction that makes the Udayeśvara's rotated, stellate-cum-circular plan the generative fact about it. The medieval theorist and the Bhūmija builder agree, across the centuries, that everything begins with the shape on the ground. The second is the text's insistence that the form of the temple is *charged with consequence*. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* does not treat its sixty-four types as neutral options laid out for the choosing. A whole chapter is devoted to the auspicious and the inauspicious building — and its language is startlingly grave. A temple well-proportioned, with even flanks and matched pillars, "neither very lofty nor very small," solidly founded and rightly decorated, is "the bestower of beneficence for men," the augmenter of honour, and "the builder and the one arranging the building act... may attain to utmost propriety." But a temple ill-made — uneven, deficient in its parts, its members badly joined — is a "foreboder of fright, bondage and chagrin," and may even prove "the source of death for the owner."[33] One striking line warns that a temple built *too high* brings danger to the king, while one built too low sees his army "churned up," annihilated.[34]

This, then, is the mental world in which the Udayeśvara was raised. To build a temple, for an eleventh-century Paramāra king, was not merely to commission a beautiful object; it was to perform an act of cosmic and political consequence, in which the exactness of the proportions, the rightness of the measures, the matching of the parts, bore directly upon the welfare of the realm and the very life of the king himself. We have seen, in the previous chapter, that Udayāditya raised this temple at the hinge of his reign, as a kingdom pulled back from the brink of collapse and made permanent in stone. The theory in his predecessor's treatise tells us how gravely that act would have been taken. A king who had nearly lost his kingdom did not build carelessly. The discipline we have watched at work in the architecture — the rigour of the rotated plan, the legible order of the climbing tower, the precise crescendo of the mouldings — is the visible face of a conviction that getting the building *right* was a matter, quite literally, of the kingdom's life and death.

## VII. Seeing the Whole

Stand back, now, from the parts, and let the building resolve at last into the single thing it was made to be.

It begins as a square that turns. From that one act of rotation about a fixed centre — the still point of the sanctum where the god will stand — the whole form unfolds. The turning square throws its corners out onto a circle and leaves behind a plan that is at once star and wheel, motion frozen into stone. That same wheeling, faceted geometry stands up off the ground into the buttresses of the wall, which carry the gods in their niches and then, refusing to stop at the roofline, run straight on up into the tower and become the climbing rows of miniature spires — the strings of pearls, hung level upon level, storey upon storey, *bhūmi* upon *bhūmi*, from the earth to the sky. The hall before the sanctum lifts its stepped bell-roof as a foothill to the tower's peak; the dome within draws the same circle overhead and lets it down again as a hanging lotus; and at the summit, where the seven tiers gather to the shoulder and the great ribbed disc crowns the whole, a small carved man climbs the last of the way to plant the flag.

Every element pulls in one direction. The plan rises into the wall; the wall rises into the tower; the tower rises, seven-storeyed, toward the heaven of the seven-storeyed celestial cars; and the building's very name — *Bhūmija*, born of the earth and built of climbing storeys — declares the gesture that its every line performs. This is what the Bhūmija vision came to, in its supreme example: not a mountain of clustered peaks like Khajuraho's, not a square merely enriched like the ordinary Nāgara shrine, but a single, disciplined, unbroken reach from the ground into the air. The Udayeśvara does not merely house the Rising Lord. In the logic of its design, from the turning square at its foundation to the flag at its crown, it *is* a rising — earth made to climb toward heaven, and held there, in red sandstone, for nine hundred years.

# Chapter 5 — The Temple's Visual World: Sculpture and Iconography

## I. A Walk That Is a Theology

In the last chapter we held the Udayeśvara in our hands as architecture — the rotated plan, the seven facets and the seven storeys, the climbing rows of miniature spires that mount the air like a slow exhalation of stone. But to a worshipper who came up onto the platform nine hundred years ago, the building would have spoken first, before any thought of geometry, as a crowd — a throng, a multitude, a great gathering held breathless in rock. Every surface that could carry a figure carries one. Gods stand in the faceted niches of the walls; goddesses dance in the arches of the tower; guardians and planets and river-deities and celestial musicians press into the doorways and crowd the eaves, as if the divine world had leaned in close to look back at us. The building is densely, almost vertiginously, populated — and the population, we soon discover, is not random. It is arranged.

That is the single most important thing to grasp about the sculpture of the Udayeśvara, and it is what this chapter sets out to show. The carving is not a garment of decoration thrown over a structure; it is a system laid into one, a logic carried in the bone. The deities are placed according to rule — by direction, by rank, by a theological reasoning patient as water finding its level — so that the temple's outer skin becomes a kind of map of the divine order, a chart of heaven pressed into mineral. To walk slowly around the building, reading the niches in sequence, is to be led, gently and deliberately, through a vision of the cosmos with Śiva burning at its centre. The deeper philosophy behind that vision — the metaphysics of Śaiva Siddhānta — belongs to the next chapter. Here our task is humbler and nearer to the hand: to identify what is carved, to see where it stands and why, and to register the plain religious sense of the imagery before we dare to ask what, in the end, it means.

We will begin with the ordering principle that governs the whole outer wall — the eight guardians of the directions, the sentinels who hold the quarters of the world. Then we will turn to the many forms of Śiva himself, the presiding lord whose theology the building exists to declare. After that we will meet the goddesses and the celestial women, who soften and enrich the programme as light softens stone toward evening; and finally we will linger — for some things ask to be lingered over — upon the rarities, the images so unusual that they whisper something particular about this temple, this king, and this single, vanished moment.

A word of method before we set out on the circuit. Almost everything described here is recorded in the close iconographic study by Anupa Pande, who spent more than a decade reading the temple's imagery against the Sanskrit ritual and architectural manuals — a pilgrimage of attention I can only stand in some awe of.[1] Where her account places a deity on a named facet, this chapter follows her; where the placement is only general, the text will say so plainly, rather than feign a precision the sources do not give. The Udayeśvara has also suffered, as living things suffer: many of its lower figures were deliberately defaced in later centuries, and some niches now hold only broken stone, a silence where a face once was.[2] What survives, though — and this is the wonder of it — is more than enough to read the design.

## II. The Eight Guardians of the Quarters

The skeleton of the whole sculptural programme — the frame on which all the rest is hung — is a set of eight deities called the **Dikpālas**, literally the "guardians of the directions" (*dik*, direction; *pāla*, protector). In Indian cosmology the eight directions of the compass are not empty geometry, not cold abstraction; each is a province with a living regent who rules it, and the standard set assigns one god to each. The cardinal and intercardinal points run, in their appointed order: **Indra** in the east, **Agni** in the south-east, **Yama** in the south, **Nirṛti** in the south-west, **Varuṇa** in the west, **Vāyu** in the north-west, **Kubera** in the north, and **Īśāna** in the north-east.[3]

The convention that makes them so useful to a temple's maker is quietly beautiful: each guardian is carved on the wall that faces the very quarter he governs. The result is a built compass, a working instrument of the sacred. Stand at any corner of the Udayeśvara and you can, in principle, orient yourself by the gods — the deity in the niche tells you which way you are looking, so that to be lost here is to have only forgotten how to read. This was not unique to Udaypur — the placement of the Dikpālas by direction is a widespread norm of medieval North Indian temple-building — but the Udayeśvara carries the scheme out with care, and several of its guardians are among the most vivid figures on the walls.[4]

Read them, then, as a circuit, a slow turning of the body that is also a turning of the mind. **Indra**, regent of the east and king of the gods, stands in a slightly flexed posture (*ābhaṅga*, that gentle bend of the body away from the straight vertical, as though stone itself had learned to breathe) and holds his two great attributes: the thunderbolt, the *vajra*, the weapon with which he splits storm-clouds and enemies alike, and the *aṅkuśa*, the elephant-goad. Beside him stands his mount, the celestial elephant **Airāvata**, so that the identification is doubly sure — secured by weapon and by vehicle, those two things that fix a Hindu deity's name more faithfully than any face.[5]

At the south-east corner — the quarter of fire — comes **Agni**, the fire-god himself, the divine priest who bears the offering up to the gods upon his flames. He too stands in the flexed *ābhaṅga* pose; but where Indra is a king, Agni is shown as something older and stranger, something out of the deep memory of the sacrifice: pot-bellied, with a stylised beard, holding a spear (*śakti*), a book, and a water-pot, and seated upon — or attended by — his vehicle the ram.[6] The book and the water-pot mark him as a sacrificial, priestly power; the ram is his ancient mount, the animal whose form the sacrificial fire was once imagined to ride.

Turn now to the south, the direction of death, and you meet its lord. **Yama**, regent of the south and judge of the dead, is given dark limbs and a fierce expression with bulging eyes, and he carries the two instruments of his office: the rod of justice, the *daṇḍa*, with which he chastises the wicked, and the noose with which he draws the soul out of the dying body, as one draws a thread from cloth. His mount, the buffalo, stands at his side.[7] Yama is a deity we are meant to find frightening — and the carver, with a candour I cannot help admiring, has obliged.

The south-western quarter belongs to a still grimmer figure, **Nirṛti**, regent of decay and dissolution, the very personification of misfortune and of the dark direction. He is shown naked and emaciated, standing upon a corpse — a *naravāhana*, a "man-vehicle," the human body that serves him for a mount — and holding a sword and a severed head.[8] This is among the most unsettling images on the building, and we shall return below to the wider taste for the macabre that it represents; for now it is enough to note, with a kind of shiver, how exactly the iconography fits the theology. The guardian of the quarter of ruin is himself a figure of ruin — wasted, naked, treading upon death.

The west is the province of the waters, the deep. **Varuṇa**, ancient lord of the cosmic ocean and of moral order, stands upon a *makara* — that composite aquatic creature, part crocodile, part fish, which is the emblem of the unfathomed deep — and holds a noose, the *pāśa*, the cord with which, in the oldest hymns of all, he binds the liar and the wrongdoer.[9] And to the north-west, the quarter of the winds, stands **Vāyu**, the wind-god, green of complexion, seated upon a deer, holding a waving banner or flag whose very flutter is the visible sign, the made-flesh proof, of the moving air he embodies.[10]

That accounts for six of the eight. Of the remaining two of the classical set — Kubera, lord of wealth in the north, and Īśāna, the auspicious aspect of Śiva himself in the north-east — the surviving record at Udaypur grows less certain, and honesty asks that we admit it. Pande's account notes an **Īśāna** figure, three-eyed and seated on a bull, wearing the piled matted hair of Śiva (the *jaṭāmukuṭa*), but observes that it is placed "alternatively" — that is, not in the textbook north-east position but somewhere adapted to the temple's own scheme.[11] For the eighth guardian, Kubera of the north, the sources read here do not give a clear individual notice; and rather than conjure one from nothing, this chapter leaves the matter open, an honest gap left unfilled. [GAP — a securely identified north-wall Kubera is not described in the sources consulted.]

What the circuit shows, even with those uncertainties, is the principle at work, steady beneath the doubt. The walls are ordered. Before a single figure of Śiva is so much as read, the building has already declared that the world it depicts is a governed world, parcelled among regents, each one waiting in his appointed place. The Dikpālas are the frame. The forms of Śiva are the picture set within it.

## III. The Many Forms of Śiva

A Śaiva temple exists to make Śiva present — and the quiet genius of medieval Śaiva art is that it never tried to do so with a single image. Śiva is, in the theology, both the formless absolute and the god of a thousand stories; and the Udayeśvara gives us the second, generously and overflowingly, so that through it we may intuit the first — as one tastes the sea in a single cupped handful. Across the building he appears in form after form — dancing, destroying, meditating, married, half-female, fused with other gods — and each form carries its own weather, its own mood and meaning. To move among them is to feel the full range of the deity the temple was raised to house.

### The Cosmic Dancer

The form to which the Udayeśvara returns most insistently, like a refrain a heart keeps returning to, is Śiva as the dancer — **Naṭeśa**, the "Lord of Dance," caught in the *tāṇḍava*, that fierce cosmic dance whose rhythm sustains and dissolves the universe in a single beat. This is not the serene dance one might idly imagine; the *tāṇḍava* is violent, world-ending energy, and the carvers render it with one knee flung high in a posture the manuals name the *ūrdhvajānu karaṇa* — the dance-pose of the "raised knee," the leg lifted dramatically across the body in a stillness that somehow blazes with motion.[12]

The grandest of these dancing Śivas presides over the very brow of the temple. Above the eastern entrance, set in the great projecting fronton of the tower — the *śukanāsa* — a multi-armed Naṭeśa performs the *tāṇḍava* in the raised-knee pose, his matted hair gathered into a tall conical crown (the *jaṭā-kalāpa*), his face held balanced and composed even in the very storm of the dance, surrounded by smaller dancing goddesses and by the dwarfish attendant spirits called *gaṇas*.[13] It is the first great image a worshipper climbing the original eastern stair would have met, high overhead — the god dancing the world into being and out of it, set like a sign above the door of his own house, the first word of the whole long sentence.

And the dancing Śiva recurs, and recurs, and will not let us go. At the base of each of the tower's three other vertical spines — north, south, and west — sits a sculpted medallion within a large arch (the *śūrasenaka*), and each of these is centred upon Śiva dancing in the same raised-knee posture: a powerful figure on the west spine; another on the south, set above a small pyramidal shrine; another on the north, shown in full *tāṇḍava* above a seated Śiva who holds a trident and the skull-topped staff called the *khaṭvāṅga*.[14] The repetition is deliberate, and it is everything. The dance is not one scene among many; it is the temple's recurring refrain, the gesture, repeated like a pulse, by which Śiva is shown to be the active power that turns the wheel of the cosmos.

### The Destroyer of the Three Cities

If the dance shows Śiva as cosmic force, another form shows him as cosmic warrior, and the temple needed both. On the south-western part of the hall wall stands **Tripurāntaka** — the "Ender of Tripura," Śiva in the act of destroying the *Tripura*, the three flying fortress-cities of the demons, which in the myth he burned to ash with a single arrow loosed from a single bow.[15] The Udayeśvara's Tripurāntaka is given a muscular torso and a powerful warrior's stance, the body braced as in the very instant of the draw, the breath held before release.[16] The myth is one of the great set-pieces of Śaiva storytelling, and its meaning is plain as daylight: Śiva is the destroyer of the strongholds of evil, the god who, against an enemy that seemed unassailable, needed only the one shot. For the temple of a king fighting to hold his realm, the resonance hardly needs spelling out — though, as we shall see, another image on these walls draws that royal resonance sharper still.

### The Conqueror of Death

That image stands on the west, and it is the one most often singled out as the key that unlocks the whole programme. Beneath the dancing Naṭeśa on the western spine of the tower sits a six-armed figure of **Mṛtyuñjaya** — the "Conqueror of Death" (*mṛtyu*, death; *jaya*, victory), Śiva as the vanquisher of mortality itself, the one who looks death in its hollow face and is not moved. He is shown not in motion but in stillness, a stillness deeper than any dance: seated in a meditative *yogamudrā*, the posture of yogic absorption, his body wreathed in serpents — snakes for earrings (*sarpa-kuṇḍala*), a snake for the sacred thread that crosses the chest (*sarpa-yajñopavīta*).[17][18] The serpent, which sheds its skin and seems to be born again out of itself, is the natural emblem of a god who has mastered death — life that keeps refusing to end.

Pande is explicit about why this figure is here, and her plainness only deepens the poignancy. Mṛtyuñjaya was "specifically invoked to grant victory and overcome the danger of death on the battlefield."[19] Recall the king who built this temple. Udayāditya came to the Paramāra throne in a time of crisis and spent his reign fighting to hold together a threatened realm; his own foundation inscription praises him as a sword-wielding second sun scattering his foes.[20] A six-armed Conqueror of Death, set upon the western face of the royal temple, is no abstract theological flourish, no idle ornament. It is, in the most intimate and pointed sense, the personal deity of a warrior-king — Śiva asked to do for the patron precisely what the patron most desperately needed: to keep death from the battlefield, and to grant the gift of victory. Of all the temple's images, this is the one in which theology and a single human life are most nearly fused into one breath.

### Śiva Half-and-Half, and Śiva Married

Two further forms show Śiva in relationship rather than in action — the god turned toward another. On the south-eastern part of the hall stands **Ardhanārīśvara**, the "Lord who is half woman" — Śiva fused down the very midline with his consort Pārvatī into a single shared body. The right, male half holds a trident and keeps the hard contours of the god; the left, female half holds a mirror and is given a rounded breast and the fuller, flowing curve of hip.[21] It is one of the subtlest of all Hindu images, a way of saying in patient stone that the divine is neither male nor female but the union of both, the god and his energy (*śakti*) inseparable as light from its shining. Elsewhere the same union is shown more gently, as a couple at rest: **Umā-Maheśvara**, Śiva ("the Great Lord," Maheśvara) seated at ease in the relaxed *lalitāsana* pose with his consort Umā upon his lap, flanked by a lion and by Śiva's bull Nandi — the divine marriage shown not as metaphysical fusion but as something nearer, warmer, more familiar: domestic tenderness rendered eternal.[22]

### The Fierce Form and the Family

Śiva also has a terrible aspect, and the temple, faithful to the whole of him, does not look away. On the south-eastern hall wall stands **Mahābhairava** — "the great terror," Śiva in his most fearsome guise — multi-armed, in a flexed stance, holding a shield, a sword, a *ḍamaru* (that small hour-glass drum which beats out the rhythm of the dance), and even a hill, wearing a garland of skulls and a headdress of living snakes.[23] And around the lord the carvers have gathered his household, as a family gathers around a hearth. On the facets of the sanctum itself appear his elephant-headed son **Gaṇeśa** with his own female counterpart **Gaṇeśānī**, four-armed and holding an axe and a bowl of sweets; the fierce attendant **Vīrabhadra**, Śiva's avenging emanation, shown on the rear facet in the act of strangling a figure who is most likely Dakṣa, the offending father-in-law of the great sacrifice-myth; the bull-headed devotee **Nandīśvara**; and the gaunt three-legged dancer **Bhṛṅgī**, that skeletal sage so wholly devoted to Śiva alone that the myth left him barely able to stand upon his own bones.[24] Śiva's other son appears too, upon the wall belonging to the upper directions: **Kārttikeya**, also called Skanda, the god of war, six-faced (*Ṣaḍānana*) and twelve-armed, riding his great peacock and bristling with spear, bow, arrow, and shield.[25]

Read all together, these forms compose a portrait of a single deity in the fullness of his range — dancer and destroyer, ascetic and bridegroom, terror and tender father. The temple does not ask the worshipper to choose among them, to take one and forsake the rest. It surrounds him with all of them at once, so that the building itself becomes the argument, written in stone and standing all around, that these are one god.

## IV. The Goddesses and the Celestial Women

If Śiva is the temple's subject, the feminine divine is its constant companion, walking everywhere at his side; and it appears in two distinct registers, two keys of the same music: the great goddesses, who are powers in their own sovereign right, and the *surasundarīs*, the celestial beauties who quicken the surfaces with grace.

The major goddesses are placed, like the gods, with intent — nothing here is left to chance. On the north-eastern part of the hall — and the north-east, remember, is the auspicious quarter — stands **Mahiṣāsuramardinī**, the "Slayer of the Buffalo Demon": the goddess Durgā in her supreme heroic act, ten-armed, her body thrown into the most dynamic of all poses (*atibhaṅga*, the extreme bend, the body bent like a drawn bow), driving her trident into the chest of the demon Mahiṣāsura while her lion savages him.[26] This is the central image of the goddess's own great myth, the very moment when the gods' combined energy, gathered and poured into a single female form, accomplishes what none of them could do alone. And beside her, in deliberate and breathtaking contrast, comes **Cāmuṇḍā**, the goddess in her terrifying aspect: emaciated to a skeleton, her belly sunken, dancing upon a corpse, holding a dagger and a skull-cup and garlanded with snakes.[27] The pairing of the radiant warrior-goddess and the death's-head dancer, set side by side upon the same quarter of the building, is itself a theological statement carved into rock — that the divine feminine, no less than Śiva, holds within her both the saving and the terrible, the mercy and the abyss.

The goddesses appear, too, as the consorts who complete the gods, in the divine couples carved upon the sanctum's northern facet: **Umā-Maheśvara** again, **Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa** (Viṣṇu with his arm encircling Lakṣmī's waist), and **Brahmā-Brahmāṇī**.[28] And the goddess of learning, **Sarasvatī**, appears in more than one form — a seated Sarasvatī associated with knowledge, shown unusually without her customary lute (*vīṇā*) and defined instead by the attributes of text and scholarship.[29] We will come back, presently, to a far more startling Sarasvatī.

Then there are the *surasundarīs* — literally the "beautiful women of the gods," the celestial nymphs and dancers who are a near-universal presence upon the walls of medieval North Indian temples, as constant as light upon water. They are not, strictly, deities to be worshipped; they are something subtler — the auspicious, life-affirming beauty of the divine world made visible, women shown dancing, adorning themselves, holding mirrors, swaying in graceful triple-bent poses among the major figures. At the Udayeśvara they crowd the arches and the side faces of the tower, and the dancing goddesses who attend the great Naṭeśa of the *śukanāsa*, mirroring his raised-knee dance, belong to this same celestial company.[30] It would be a mistake — and a small impoverishment of the soul — to read them as mere ornament. In the visual theology of the temple they are essential: they are the sensuous abundance of a cosmos wholly pervaded by the god, the standing proof that the world Śiva dances is not bleak but fertile, beautiful, and unmistakably alive.

## V. The Rare and the Striking

Every great temple keeps its surprises — images that depart from the expected repertoire and, in departing, tell us something precise and unrepeatable. The Udayeśvara holds a remarkable number of them, gifts to the patient eye, and three in particular ask to be looked at closely.

### A Dancing Sarasvatī

In one of the arches flanking the great fronton over the eastern entrance is a representation of **Sarasvatī as a dancer** — and this is genuinely rare, rare enough to stop the breath. Sarasvatī, the goddess of learning, speech, and music, is almost always shown seated and serene, the calm patroness of scholars; to find her *dancing*, in the very same raised-knee posture as Naṭeśa, a swan carved near her right leg, holding the hour-glass drum, wearing a necklace worked to imitate long lotus-buds, is unusual enough to demand an explanation.[31]

The likeliest explanation is dynastic, and it is a tender one. The Paramāras were a house of learning, and the greatest of them, Bhoja — Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor — was a scholar-king who endowed a temple to Sarasvatī at his capital (the famous Bhojaśālā of Dhara). Pande reads the dancing Sarasvatī of Udaypur as an echo of that family devotion, the goddess of learning given pride of place near the temple's very front as a sign of the Paramāra patronage of letters.[32] It is a small but telling instance of how a temple's iconography can carry a dynasty's image of itself, its quiet pride: even in a Śaiva building dedicated to a warrior-king's god, the house's love of scholarship found its way, dancing, onto the wall.

### A Goddess Seated on a Corpse

On the southern side of the great fronton, among the tiers of seated goddesses, is one of the temple's most arresting images, one that holds the eye and will not release it: a goddess — named in the sources as **Śreyā** or **Śrī** — seated upon a *preta*, a corpse or ghost, and holding an iguana (*godhā*) in one lower hand, a combination read as symbolising the destruction of enemies.[33] The corpse-seat is a charged and terrible motif. To sit enthroned upon a dead body is to declare absolute dominion over death and over the forces of dissolution — the same grim vocabulary we met in Nirṛti treading his corpse-mount and Cāmuṇḍā dancing upon hers. Here, though, that vocabulary is turned toward a goddess of fortune and prosperity, fusing auspiciousness with the macabre in a way that is hard to parallel anywhere, and that points, once again, toward the theme of triumph over enemies which runs like a bright thread through this royal temple's whole programme.

### The God Who Is Four Gods

The single most ambitious image on the building — the boldest claim it dares to make — is a **syncretic deity** in the central niche of the eastern fronton, set below the dancing Naṭeśa. Pande names it **Hariharārkāpitāmaha**, a compound that unpacks, like a folded thing opening, into its components: *Hari* (Viṣṇu), *Hara* (Śiva), *Arka* (Sūrya, the sun), and *Pitāmaha* (Brahmā, the "grandfather" creator). It is, in other words, a single figure combining four of the great gods of the Hindu pantheon into one shared body.[34]

Syncretic images of this kind are the temple's most explicit theological statements, its plainest professions of faith, and the building offers a small graded series of them, a rising stair. The simplest is **Harihara**, the half-Viṣṇu, half-Śiva figure on the south-eastern hall wall, its two halves marked by two different crowns — the matted-hair *jaṭāmukuṭa* of Śiva upon the one side, the cylindrical *kirīṭamukuṭa* of Viṣṇu upon the other — and holding Viṣṇu's conch (*śaṅkha*) and discus (*cakra*).[35] More complex is a three-faced, ten-armed goddess-panel fusing **Gāyatrī, Sāvitrī, and Sarasvatī**, a composite of the great Vedic and solar feminine powers holding lotus-bud, rosary, noose, and book.[36] And the most ambitious of all is the four-fold Hariharārkāpitāmaha, in which Śiva, Viṣṇu, the Sun, and Brahmā are declared, in a single carved body, to be aspects of one reality.

This is the threshold at which the sculpture begins to reach toward the philosophy of the chapter that follows — where stone strains toward thought. A composite of four gods is no confession of indecision, no muddle; it is a confession of faith — the assertion, central to the inclusive theism of the period and to Śaiva Siddhānta in particular, that the many gods are forms of a single supreme principle, many rivers of one sea. That principle, for this temple, is Śiva: he is the dancer who presides above the composite, the lord whose house this is, the one into whom the others are gathered as streams are gathered home. The four-faced image makes the claim visible. The next chapter will follow it inward, into thought.

## VI. Reading the Whole

Step back, finally, from the individual figures and consider the design entire — let the eye unfocus until the crowd resolves into a single shape. What the Udayeśvara offers is not a gallery but a structured argument, a sermon in stone that builds and builds. Its outer skin is ordered first by direction — the eight Dikpālas setting the world in its quarters, each regent waiting in his place — and then filled, within that frame, with the forms of Śiva: the dancer who turns the cosmos, the warrior who burns the cities of evil, the conqueror who masters death, the bridegroom, the ascetic, the half-female lord, the father of gods. Around them and among them move the goddesses, saving and terrible by turns, and the celestial women whose beauty itself declares the world worth saving. And at the front, over the door, the building's boldest images gather the many gods into one, and set Śiva, dancing, above them all.

It is, as the king's own praise-poem might have it, a sunrise rendered in stone — a whole divine order shown rising into visibility upon the walls of a single temple, dawn made permanent. The placement is exact, the repetition deliberate, the rarities pointed and full of meaning. A goddess of learning dances for a dynasty of scholars; a six-armed Conqueror of Death sits on the west wall for a king who fought simply to live; four gods share one body to declare that, in the end, beneath all the many faces, there is one. To walk the circuit of the Udayeśvara, reading the niches in turn, really is to be walked through a theology — and it remains only, in the chapter ahead, to ask what that theology, set down so carefully in the eleventh century and waiting all this while in the stone, understood itself to mean.

# Chapter 6 — Philosophy in Stone: Śaiva Siddhānta and Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*

## I. From the Measured Wall to the Meaning Within

The chapters before this one have looked hard at a building, and looked lovingly — they have measured its plan and named its mouldings; they have followed the Bhūmija tower as it rises out of the very ground in its serried, climbing rows of miniature spires; they have walked the long outer walls and counted the gods carved upon them, the dancing Naṭeśa, the meditating Mṛtyuñjaya, the guardians of the eight directions ranged each in his proper quarter, the fierce goddesses, and the great syncretic forms in which Śiva gathers Viṣṇu and the Sun and Brahmā into the single compass of his body. We have, in short, read the Udayeśvara as an object — as architecture, as sculpture, as stone that one may stand before and admire.

This chapter asks something else of us. Not *what* is carved, but *why* — and why arranged just so. For a temple of this kind was never meant to be only a beautiful thing. It was meant to be true. Beneath its design there lay a complete and rigorous account of what reality is, of how the soul is bound within it, and of how that soul may at last be freed — an account the building was raised to make visible to the eye. The scholar whose decade of patient study underlies so much of this book, Anupa Pande, titled her work not merely on the *art and architecture* of the Udayeśvara but on its *philosophy* — the philosophy of the **Śaiva Siddhānta**, the great medieval system of thought about Śiva.[1] To understand the temple as its makers understood it, we must learn something of that system. And here we have a rare and precise reason to do so, for the Paramāra dynasty that lifted this temple out of the earth produced, in the person of its most famous king, one of the recognised authorities on exactly this philosophy. Bhoja Paramāra — scholar-king, patron of the architectural treatise that first gave the Bhūmija style its name — also composed a treatise on Śaiva Siddhānta itself, the *Tattvaprakāśa*.[2]

So this is the interpretive heart of the book: the chapter in which the stone is at last disclosed as theology, and the dynasty's buildings and the dynasty's books are shown to be two utterances of a single vision. Yet we begin with a word of honesty about evidence. The exposition of Śaiva Siddhānta that follows rests on the settled scholarly account of that school — its doctrines are well attested across a great body of Sanskrit scripture and commentary — and on what the sources behind this book tell us of how that doctrine was understood and lived in eleventh-century Malwa. Where the sources are explicit, I follow them closely; where the system's finer technicalities run beyond what those sources spell out, I have said so plainly rather than invent. Philosophy is the one place in a book like this where a confident sentence can do real harm, and I have tried to earn each one.

## II. The Most Prosperous Faith of the Age

First, then, the setting. It is easy, looking back across nine centuries, to imagine medieval India as a single, undifferentiated "Hinduism." It was nothing of the kind. The religious world into which the Udayeśvara was born was a thronged and competitive landscape of sects, and in the eleventh century one of them rose above the rest. "Śaivism," writes the historian Mahesh Singh in his study of Bhoja and his times, "was the most prosperous sect of the age," patronised by kings and commoners alike, its power and influence mirrored in the multitude of Śiva temples then rising across the subcontinent.[3] To be a king in this period and a worshipper of Śiva was to stand squarely in the broad mainstream of the age.

The Paramāras of Malwa stood there without hesitation. They were, in Singh's words, "devout worshippers of Śiva" and "great builders of Śiva temples"; their land-grants open with benedictory verses in Śiva's honour, and their kings styled themselves *Parama-Māheśvara* — "supremely devoted to Maheśvara," to the Great Lord Śiva.[4] Their very heartland was Śaiva ground. Malwa's ancient capital, Ujjayinī, had been famous through the ages as the seat of **Mahākāla**, Śiva as "Great Time" — and Bhoja, the sources tell us, used to live in the vicinity of the Mahākāla temple, whose *liṅga* is counted to this day among the twelve great *Jyotirliṅgas*, the "shining liṅgas," of India.[5] So when Udayāditya, generations later, founded a new city and crowned it with a temple to Śiva under a name of his own coining — Udayeśvara, the Rising Lord — he was not making an idiosyncratic gesture. He was doing the most characteristic thing a Paramāra king could do.

But "Śaivism" is itself too broad a word, a single lamp held over many rooms. Within the worship of Śiva the medieval texts drew distinctions among several schools, and those distinctions matter for our temple. The sources behind this book name them. There were the **Pāśupatas**, the oldest of the organised Śaiva orders, founded (in the tradition) by the teacher Lakulīśa — ascetics who smeared themselves with ash and wore the matted hair of the god.[6] There were the more extreme orders — the **Kāpālikas** and **Kālāmukhas**, the "skull-bearers" and "black-faces," who courted the terrifying, *ghora*, aspect of the god in cremation-ground rites with skull-bowls and bone ornaments.[7] And there was, set apart from all of these, the school that gives this chapter its title: the **Śaiva Siddhānta**.

The Śaiva Siddhānta — the name means, roughly, "the established conclusion (*siddhānta*) of the Śaivas," the settled doctrine, the orthodoxy — was the great theological mainstream of the movement, the deep central channel through which the rest ran. Singh classes it among the schools whose authority rested upon the *Āgamas*, the body of scripture that Śaivas held to be revealed by Śiva himself, and he traces its remarkable institutional reach.[8] Its ascetic teachers ran in unbroken lineages of *rājagurus*, royal preceptors, who initiated kings into the faith and received, in return, extraordinary patronage — whole kingdoms, on occasion, were formally made over to a Śaiva-Siddhānta preceptor as a spiritual honorarium.[9] These lineages spread, Singh shows, from the Punjab in the north to the Tamil south, and they wielded "considerable influence in central India" — the Paramāras' own country.[10] One detail brings the kinship startlingly close: an early Paramāra king of Malwa, Sīyaka, is recorded as the royal disciple of a Śaiva-Siddhānta ascetic who had "heard the whole of Śaiva-siddhānta philosophy and was like a Śiva at Kailāsa."[11] The dynasty that built the Udayeśvara had Śaiva-Siddhānta teachers at its court generations before the temple was so much as imagined.

This, then, is the frame within which we must stand. The Udayeśvara is no generic Hindu temple but a particular one: a Śaiva temple, of the Śaiva-Siddhānta persuasion, raised by a dynasty steeped to the marrow in that exact tradition. To read its meaning we must now turn to what that tradition actually taught.

## III. The Lord, the Soul, and the Bonds

At the centre of the Śaiva Siddhānta stands a single supreme reality: **Śiva**. Not Śiva as one god among many — though the temple's walls, as we shall see, are crowded with many gods — but Śiva as the absolute, the ground of all being, the conscious source from which everything that exists proceeds and to which the liberated soul, at the end of its long pilgrimage, returns. The whole architecture of the system, like the whole architecture of the temple, is an attempt to give shape to that one conviction: that at the heart of things there is a single Lord.

The classical Śaiva Siddhānta organised its account of reality around three words — three words that an educated reader of the tradition would have carried always with him, like a master-key worn close at the breast. They are **pati**, **paśu**, and **pāśa**: the Lord, the bound soul, and the bonds. The three are best taken together, held in the hand at once, as a single picture of the universe and of the soul's predicament within it.

**Pati** means "lord," "master" — the very word an ordinary speaker would use for the master of a herd or the lord of a household. In the system it names Śiva: the Lord, supreme, eternal, all-knowing, all-powerful, himself never bound by anything at all, the conscious sovereign of the whole order of being. He is the goal toward which everything tends, the one reality that was never, for an instant, in chains.

**Paśu** means, literally, "beast," "cattle" — the animal that belongs to the herd and answers to the herdsman's call. It is a deliberately humbling word, and it names the individual soul: each one of us, the countless conscious selves, who are real and eternal and, in our deepest nature, akin to Śiva himself — and who are nonetheless, for now, *bound*. The soul is *paśu* because it stands to the Lord as the beast stands to its master: belonging to him, dependent upon him, yet for the present tethered and unfree. The image is not contemptuous so much as exact. We are, in the system's eyes, conscious beings of immense dignity who have forgotten our own freedom.

And **pāśa** — the word that completes the herding metaphor with a grim precision — means "noose," "tether," "fetter": the rope that binds the beast. The *pāśa* is the whole apparatus of bondage, all that holds the soul down in finitude and forgetfulness and the turning round of birth and death. The classical tradition analysed these bonds into several strands — most centrally a kind of innate, beginningless spiritual impurity that veils the soul's true infinitude, together with the binding force of *karman*, the moral residue of past action, and the material stuff of the cosmos that clothes the soul in body after body.[12]

The beauty of the scheme — and the reason it could organise a whole temple — lies in how cleanly it states the human situation and its remedy. Reality holds exactly three kinds of thing: one Lord, many souls, and the bonds that hold the souls. The Lord is forever free; the souls are presently bound; salvation is nothing but the loosening of the bond. And here the herding image turns from bleak to consoling, from prison to promise. A noose can be untied. The soul's bondage is not its nature but its condition — something added, something that may in principle be taken away — and when at last it is taken away, the *paśu* is revealed to have been, all along, of the very same nature as the *pati*. Liberation, in the Śaiva Siddhānta, is neither annihilation nor absorption into a featureless absolute; it is the soul, loosed of its fetters, coming home into its own infinite Śiva-nature in the presence of Śiva.[13] The whole point of the system is that the beast was always, secretly, more than a beast.

The reader will already feel how such a doctrine might shape a building. A temple founded upon the *pati–paśu–pāśa* scheme is a machine for staging exactly this drama: it sets the Lord at a still centre, surrounds him with the ordered ranks of lesser beings, and offers the worshipper a path inward and upward — from the bound and busy world of the outer walls toward the single free reality at the heart. We shall return to that. First we must add the dimension that turns the three categories into a cosmos: the *tattvas*.

## IV. The Ladder of Reality

If *pati*, *paśu*, and *pāśa* tell us *what kinds* of thing exist, the doctrine of the **tattvas** tells us *how reality is layered* — how it is stacked, level upon level, from the dense to the diaphanous. The word *tattva* means, literally, "that-ness," "the truth of a thing," and by extension a "reality-level" or "principle of existence." The Indian philosophical schools used the word to enumerate the basic constituents of the universe, the rungs by which the cosmos descends from the subtle to the gross — and the Śaiva Siddhānta inherited and expanded this scheme into one of its most characteristic doctrines: a graded hierarchy of reality, conventionally counted as thirty-six *tattvas*, running from the dense physical world at the bottom up to the divine at the top.

The lower reaches of this ladder the Śaivas shared with older Indian philosophy. At the very foot stand the five gross elements — earth, water, fire, air, and ether — the stuff of the visible world. Above them lie the subtle elements and the faculties of sense and action, the mind, the ego, and the discriminating intellect; and above those again, the principles of primordial matter and its qualities. This much is the common inheritance of the Indian schools, a map of the manifest cosmos rising from solid earth through ever-finer grades toward pure consciousness. What the Śaiva Siddhānta did — and this is its own distinctive contribution — was to crown the older ladder with a series of higher, properly Śaiva levels: the principles through which Śiva's pure consciousness contracts itself, veils its own infinitude, and steps down by slow degrees into the bound and limited condition of the individual soul. And at the summit of the whole hierarchy stands Śiva himself, the highest *tattva*, pure and infinite consciousness, the reality from which the entire descending order unfolds like light from a source.[14]

A reader meeting this for the first time may find the sheer enumeration daunting, and the detailed list need not detain us. The essential thing to grasp is the *shape* of the doctrine, for the shape is what the temple embodies. The *tattvas* describe reality as a single graded ascent: a continuous ladder from the most material to the most spiritual, with the dense physical world at the foot and Śiva, pure consciousness, at the crown. Existence is not a flat field but a hierarchy with a direction. To climb "up" the ladder of *tattvas* is to move from matter toward spirit, from the many toward the one, from bondage toward the Lord. The cosmos has, built into its very structure, the same upward orientation that the soul has in its thirst for release.

Hold that image — a layered reality, rising from earth to Śiva — and turn back, for a moment, to the building described in the earlier chapters of this book. A temple whose tower was conceived, in the Bhūmija manner, as rising *directly out of the earth* and climbing in story upon story (*bhūmi* upon *bhūmi*) toward a single crowning point; a temple that is *saptabhūmi*, "seven-storeyed," in elevation, its miniature spires diminishing as they ascend toward the summit. The correspondence is not a coincidence to be argued for. It is one idea, told twice — once as metaphysics, once as masonry. The ladder of the *tattvas* and the ascent of the *śikhara* are the selfsame ascent.

## V. Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*: The Dynasty's Own Theology

We can now draw the link that gives this chapter its peculiar force. The Śaiva Siddhānta was not, for the Paramāras, an imported or merely inherited creed patronised from a courteous distance. One of them wrote it. The great Bhoja — Bhoja the scholar-king, under whose patronage the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, the architectural treatise that first names the Bhūmija style, was compiled — was himself an author of Śaiva-Siddhānta philosophy. Among the works credited to his pen, alongside the *Yuktikalpataru* on practical and economic matters, the *Rājamārtaṇḍa*, and the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* on architecture, there stands a treatise called the **Tattvaprakāśa** — the "Light on the Tattvas," the illumination of the reality-principles.[15]

Mahesh Singh puts the matter with welcome bluntness. Bhoja, he writes, "was not only a follower but also an exponent of Śaivism. One of his works, the *Tattvaprakāśa*, deals with Śaivism."[16] The word *exponent* is the one to fasten on, and to turn in the light. A follower worships; an exponent expounds — sets out the doctrine, argues it, teaches it. Bhoja did both. He styled himself *Parama-Māheśvara* on his copperplates and opened his grants with hymns to Śiva; and then he sat down and composed a treatise whose very title announces its subject — the *tattvas*, the reality-levels we have just been climbing.[17] The *Tattvaprakāśa* is, in fact, a recognised text of the Śaiva Siddhānta canon, a concise versified statement of the school's doctrine that later teachers thought worth a commentary; and that it should have issued from the hand of a reigning Paramāra emperor is one of the more remarkable facts in the whole intellectual history of medieval India.

This is the hinge of the entire chapter, so let me state it plainly. The dynasty that built the Udayeśvara was not merely a Śaiva dynasty in the loose sense that most kings of the age were Śaiva. It was a dynasty one of whose own kings was a published authority on the precise philosophy — Śaiva Siddhānta — that the temple embodies. The same royal house, within a few generations, produced a treatise that lights up the *tattvas* in words and a temple that raises them in stone. The book and the building are the dynasty's two testimonies to one faith.

We must, however, be careful not to overclaim, and here the limits of the sources must be honoured. Udayāditya, who actually built the Udayeśvara, was Bhoja's successor in the house, not Bhoja himself; the temple post-dates the king who wrote the *Tattvaprakāśa*. I have found in the sources behind this book no statement that Udayāditya read his predecessor's treatise, or that the temple was consciously designed as an illustration of it, and I will not pretend otherwise. [GAP — the sources consulted do not document a direct, deliberate link between the text of Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa* and the design of Udayāditya's temple.] What the sources *do* establish is the deeper and more interesting thing: that the *Tattvaprakāśa* and the Udayeśvara grow from a single soil — one royal Śaiva-Siddhānta culture, sustained across Paramāra reigns, in which a king could expound the *tattvas* in a book and a king could raise them in a tower, because both were doing, in their different media, the work of the same faith. The continuity is dynastic and cultural, and it is real. It does not need to be turned into a footnote-to-footnote dependence to be worth the telling.

## VI. The Temple as Cosmic Diagram

Return now, with the doctrine in hand, to the building — and watch it change before you. What looked, in the earlier chapters, like an inventory of architectural members and a catalogue of sculptures begins, read through Śaiva Siddhānta, to disclose an argument. The temple is a diagram of the very cosmos the doctrine describes: a model, in living stone, of reality as the Śaiva Siddhānta understood it. Three features carry the meaning.

**The sanctum as the still centre.** At the heart of the Udayeśvara, reached through vestibule and hall, lies the **garbhagṛha** — literally the "womb-chamber" — a small, square, dark, and almost wholly unornamented room. Within it stands the **Śivaliṅga**, the aniconic emblem of Śiva, the one image in the whole vast building that represents not a form of the god but the formless Lord himself.[18] Everything we have learned about the *pati* is gathered and concentrated here, in this one dim cell. The sanctum is the architectural statement of Śiva as supreme reality: the single still point at the centre of the whole structure, dark and plain because the absolute is beyond image and ornament, square and stable because it is the unmoving ground on which everything else comes to rest. The countless carved figures of the outer walls are all in motion — dancing, striding, fighting, riding their beasts; the liṅga in the dark does not move at all. It is the *pati* at the centre of the *paśus*, the one free reality around which the bound and busy world is ranged. To walk inward to the sanctum is to walk from multiplicity toward unity, from the many gods of the walls toward the one Lord at the core — a small pilgrimage taken in a few quiet steps.

**The ascent of the tower.** Above the sanctum rises the **śikhara**, the tower, and we have already seen what it figures. Built in the Bhūmija manner so as to seem to grow straight out of the earth, climbing in diminishing tiers of miniature spires toward a single crowning point, *saptabhūmi* — seven-storeyed — in its elevation, the tower is the ladder of the *tattvas* made vertical and visible. It carries the eye, and with the eye the mind, up the graded hierarchy of reality: from the earth at its foot, through the rising stories, toward the unity at the summit. If the sanctum states the doctrine of the Lord, the tower states the doctrine of the *tattvas* — the layered cosmos rising from matter to spirit. And the two are joined in the most literal way imaginable, for the tower stands directly over the sanctum: the ascent of reality rises, in the architecture exactly as in the theology, straight up out of the still centre where the Lord abides. The pearl-strings of miniature spires that the earlier chapters traced from ground to summit are the rungs of the cosmos, and they all climb toward the one point where Śiva is.

**The ordered ranks of deities.** Finally, the populous outer walls. Chapter 5 catalogued them: the guardians of the eight directions set each in his own quarter — Indra in the east, Agni in the south-east, Yama in the south, and so onward around the compass; the great forms of Śiva and the goddess; the syncretic images in which Śiva absorbs Viṣṇu, the Sun, and Brahmā into a single body.[19] Read as theology, this crowded host is neither decoration nor a random pantheon. It is the ordered world of bound beings — the *paśus* and the lesser divine powers — arrayed in their proper places around the central Lord. The directional guardians pin the cosmos to its quarters; the ranked gods fill out its hierarchy; and the syncretic forms, in which other great deities are shown as aspects gathered into Śiva, state in sculpture the very doctrine of the sanctum: that beneath all multiplicity there is one supreme reality, and that all the powers of the cosmos are, in the end, finally his. The walls are the manifest world in its order and its plurality; the sanctum is the one reality beneath it; the tower is the ascent that joins them. The whole building is the *pati*, the *paśu*, and the rising ladder of *tattvas*, set out in stone for anyone who knows how to read it.

There is even a textual warrant, drawn from the Paramāras' own architectural tradition, for treating a temple as a cosmic object rather than a mere shelter. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* — Bhoja's architectural treatise — opens its account of temple-types not with bricks and mortar but with a myth of origins: in the beginning, it says, the creator-god fashioned the celestial palaces of the gods, the heavenly cars that move through the sky, and the temples built on earth of stone and baked brick were made *in their image*, as the ornament of cities.[20] An earthly temple, in this understanding, is the replica of a divine archetype, a piece of the heavenly order brought down into the world of men. The treatise even classifies the dozens of named temple-forms by their geometry — square, circular, octagonal, oblong — as so many disciplined ways of giving the divine a stable shape; and it warns, in its chapter on the auspicious and the inauspicious, that a temple's proportions are no neutral matter, that a well-formed building brings beneficence to its builder and a malformed one brings ruin, disease, even death.[21] A temple, for the tradition that produced the Udayeśvara, was a thing whose very measurements were charged with cosmic consequence. To build one rightly was to align a patch of earth with the order of heaven.

## VII. Theology Made Visible

We can now gather up the threads. The Udayeśvara is a Śaiva temple, raised by a dynasty that was Śaiva to its marrow and that had kept Śaiva-Siddhānta preceptors at its court for generations. The philosophy it embodies — that of the Śaiva Siddhānta — holds that reality is composed of one supreme Lord (*pati*), many souls presently bound (*paśu*), and the fetters that bind them (*pāśa*); and that the cosmos is a graded hierarchy of reality-levels (*tattvas*) rising from dense matter at the foot to Śiva, pure consciousness, at the crown. That same dynasty produced, in the scholar-king Bhoja, an actual exponent of this philosophy, whose treatise the *Tattvaprakāśa* — the "Light on the Tattvas" — stands within the recognised canon of the school. And the temple, read with the doctrine in mind, turns out to be that doctrine in three dimensions: the still, dark, formless sanctum for the Lord; the seven-storeyed tower for the ascending ladder of the *tattvas*; the ranked and ordered deities of the walls for the bound multiplicity of the manifest world, with the syncretic forms declaring that all of it is, finally, Śiva's.

This is what it means to call the Udayeśvara philosophy in stone. The architecture and sculpture that the earlier chapters described as form and ornament are revealed, in the end, as theology made visible — a complete vision of God, soul, and cosmos, set out not in the words of a treatise but in the silent grammar of a building. The flag-bearer carved climbing the spire toward the summit; the worshipper walking inward through the hall toward the dark chamber where the liṅga stands; the eye drawn up the rising tiers of the tower toward the single point at the top — all of these are one movement, the movement at the centre of the whole faith: the movement of the bound soul toward the one free Lord. The Rising Lord's temple was built so that this ascent might be something a person could not merely think but walk, and stand inside, and see.

# Chapter 7 — An Epigraphic Archive: Eight Centuries of Inscriptions

A great temple is never only a building. It is also a surface — vast acres of cut stone that, the moment a kingdom resolves that this place matters, become a kind of public ledger held open to the sky, and onto which the centuries lean down to write. Kings record their gifts there. Pilgrims scratch their names, hoping, perhaps, to be remembered a little past their own brief breath. Conquerors carve their victories; restorers note their patient repairs. And so, year upon year, the stone keeps gathering writing the way a riverbed gathers silt, slowly, almost tenderly, until the monument is no longer merely a thing seen but a thing *read* — a continuous text composed by hundreds of hands that never once touched one another, in languages that shared no alphabet, professing faiths that warred over the very ground beneath their feet.

The Udayeśvara is such a surface. We have come to it, so far, as architecture and as theology — as the bhūmija masterpiece lifted over the Malwa plateau in the late eleventh century, and as the seat of the Rising Lord whose name it carries like a vow. But the building keeps a second, quieter distinction, one that has only lately begun to receive the wonder it is owed. It is among the most remarkable epigraphic archives in all of central India: a single monument bearing a continuous sequence of inscriptions in Sanskrit, Persian, and Hindi that runs across roughly eight centuries — from the temple's consecration in the year *saṃvat* 1137 (1080 CE) to its archaeological restoration in *saṃvat* 1985 (1928 CE).[1] Nearly the whole span of north India's medieval and early-modern history can be read off these walls, if one only knows where to look, and which scripts to carry to the stone like lamps into a dark room.

This chapter listens to the inscriptions not one by one — Chapter 3 has already given the foundation eulogy, the *Udayapur Praśasti*, its proper hearing — but as a single accumulating corpus across time. We will watch the archive build itself, layer by patient layer: first the dense Sanskrit stratum of the Paramāra and post-Paramāra centuries, then one extraordinary inscription that has slipped almost unnoticed past the scholarly eye — the serpentine "scimitar of letters," a magico-political device unique to a pair of eleventh-century kings — and at last the Persian records that mark the moment the conquerors arrived and a part of the temple became a mosque. The chapter closes, as the book closes, with the temple's long afterlife: defacement, repurposing, restoration, and a worship that has never quite ceased.

## The shape of the corpus

Begin, if you will, with the plainest fact of all — the almost bureaucratic fact of *quantity*. The Udayeśvara is not a temple with an inscription or two; it is a temple swarming with them, alive with them, the way a hive is alive. The Gwalior State archaeology department, in a campaign of survey and copying that ran patiently through the 1920s, transcribed and listed inscription after inscription from the complex, and the labour was carried forward, decades on, by the epigraphy branch of the Archaeological Survey of India.[2] Only a fraction of all this has ever been formally edited and published — chiefly in the corpus volumes of H. V. Trivedi and in the studies of A. C. Mittal — which means that the temple's walls still hold a reserve of unread and half-read text, water in a well not yet drawn: a working archive rather than a closed book.[3]

What survives, gathered together, sketches a biography — the life-story of a place. The Paramāra inventory of inscriptions drawn from Udayapur alone shows the site returning again and again as a place of royal record across the long arc of the dynasty. The founder Udayāditya is named on the great stone inscription dated *saṃvat* 1137 / 1080 CE — the consecration year, the year of beginnings — and on a second undated stone bound to the same moment.[4] His son and successor Naravarman left a record here dated *saṃvat* 1151 / 1094 CE, the year after his accession.[5] More than a century onward, the temple was still drawing kings to its walls: the Paramāra king Devapāla is recorded at Udayapur in *saṃvat* 1286 / 1229 CE, and again a few years later, around 1232.[6] And remarkably late, in the dynasty's very twilight, Jayasiṃha II appears on a stone here dated *saṃvat* 1366 / 1309 CE — only a handful of years before the Paramāra line itself dissolved into the rising tide of the Delhi Sultanate.[7]

Set those dates down in a row — 1080, 1094, 1229, 1232, 1309 — and something rises off the list that is far more than antiquarian. For well over two centuries, through the dynasty's high noon and down into its long decline, the Udayeśvara remained a *living* royal foundation: a place where king after king chose to be seen, to record his gifts and grants, to renew the bond between the throne and the Rising Lord. This was no monument the Paramāras raised once and then forgot. It was an instrument they kept in their hands and kept playing. And when we read these inscriptions as a corpus rather than as scattered curiosities, this is the first thing the stone confides to us — that the Udayeśvara was, for the whole life of the dynasty, the dynastic shrine *par excellence*, the Paramāra answer to the oldest question a Śaiva king can ask: where does one go to make one's kingship visible?

The languages and the scripts deepen the picture still. The earliest layer is Sanskrit, written in the rectilinear Nāgarī of the late eleventh century — characters described by those who have bent close to them as well-formed and inch-high, cut in a rigidly upright style, their verticals finishing in a sharp bend to the right: the local hand of Udayāditya's own masons, the very gesture of their wrists preserved in stone.[8] Over this medieval Sanskrit stratum lie the far later additions: Persian, in the Arabic script, recording the arrival of a wholly different polity; and Hindi, marking the temple's modern caretaking. Three languages, three scripts, three religious and political worlds, all incised on one building. There is the archive in miniature — not a harmony but a stratigraphy, each layer pressing its weight down upon the one before, the way years press down upon a heart.

## A serpentine scimitar of letters

Among all this writing there is one inscription that deserves to be lifted out and held up to the light, for it is unlike anything most of us will ever have imagined a temple could carry. It is not a praise-poem; it is not a grant; it is not a date. It is a *diagram* — a serpent, drawn out of letters.

The stone itself is unassuming, even humble: a rectangular slab of pinkish sandstone, some seventy centimetres high, built into the outer parapet wall — the *prākāra* — at the point where the temple's quadrangular courtyard projects southward.[9] Anyone who has studied that wall closely can read at once that the slab is no longer where it began its life. Its flat, inscribed face is turned *inward*, pressed blind against the masonry, while its decorative side — carved with an ornamental balustrade, a *vedikā* — looks outward, the exact reverse of every well-preserved stretch of courtyard wall around it. The block plainly belonged to this parapet, yet it once looked *into* the courtyard; and at some point it was prised out, flipped, and reset the wrong way round — almost certainly during one of the twentieth-century restoration campaigns, when masons rebuilding the wall did not recognize the treasure in their hands and set it back facing the wrong way.[10] There is a small parable of the whole archive in that accident of survival: a precious text saved precisely by being mortared, sightless, into a wall — refuge granted, all unknowing, in the very act of concealment.

Turn the stone over now in the mind's eye, to its inscribed face, and here is what you find waiting. The centrepiece is a snake — but a snake whose entire body is built from the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, each letter resting in its own little compartment along the serpent's coiling length.[11] Read from the top down, the creature's geometrical "head" carries the fourteen vowels, marshalled in the classical order of short-and-long pairs — *a, ā; i, ī; u, ū; ṛ, ṝ; ḷ, ḹ* — rising to the complex vowels *e, ai* and the compounds *o, au*. Down the tubular "body" run the consonants: the four semivowels at the neck, then the five classes of stops gathered into a rhomboidal knot at the torso, then the three sibilants trailing down one leg. The whole series opens and closes with the voiced aspirate *ha*, exactly as the alphabet is arranged in the Śivasūtras that open Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, the foundational grammar of Sanskrit.[12] At the diagram's base sit the rarer breathed sounds; and at the very tail, looping up and down upon itself, are the grammatical *endings* — twenty-one terminations for nouns across all seven of their cases, and eighteen for verbs in the present tense, in both the active and the middle voice.[13] Which is to say: a complete machine of the language, a living engine of speech. Hold this one serpent in view and you hold, all at once, the very sounds of Sanskrit and the rules for inflecting them, coiled together into a single image — the breath of a whole tongue caught and quieted on a stone.

And what is it *for*? The answer is given by two short lines of writing on the slab — a damaged but partly legible verse — and confirmed, crucially, by an identical diagram surviving elsewhere. The verse on the Udayapur stone calls the diagram, in so many words, a *varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā*: a "serpentine scimitar of letters."[14] The compound is built to startle, and it does. *Varṇa* means both a *letter* of the alphabet and, in another register, the *order of society*; *nāga* is the serpent; *kṛpāṇikā* is a small curved sword, a scimitar. The grammar of the surviving verse runs as a command in the causative — roughly, *"may the protector cause this serpentine scimitar of letters to protect…"* — though precisely what is to be protected has flaked away with the stone, lost to time as so much is lost.[15] The image, all the same, is unmistakable: the vowels form the snake's flaring hood, the consonants its coiled body, the grammatical endings its tail; and that same shape reads, in the selfsame instant, as a weapon — the hood become the curved blade, the knotted torso the ornamented hilt, the winding tail the sword-strap. A snake, an alphabet, and a sword, fused into one device. As the inscription's modern student so finely puts it, the object presents "an unusual synthesis of aurality, visuality and efficacy" — sound, sight, and power braided together into a single bright cord.[16]

For the meaning of that power we must journey west, to the great Mahākāla temple of Ujjain, the sacred heart of the Paramāra realm, where an identical diagram bearing the same name survives in a far better-preserved setting, embedded in a longer dedicatory inscription.[17] There the verses speak plainly. They call the diagram a "serpent-scimitar of letters marked with Udayāditya's name" — *udayāditya-nāmāṅka-varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā* — and say it was composed, along with other "poetic gems," by a figure described as "the friend of talented poets," apparently King Naravarman himself.[18] The diagram, the Ujjain text declares, is an "ornament to be affixed on the chest of both poets and kings," and — most tellingly of all — a "unique magical sword," a *siddhāsiputrikā*, belonging to those two royal devotees of Śiva, Udayāditya and his son Naravarman, and intended *varṇasthityai*: "for the preservation of language and social order."[19]

That phrase is the key that turns in the lock of the whole strange object. Scholars long supposed these letter-serpents to be schoolroom aids — clever mnemonics for drilling Sanskrit into the heads of young boys.[20] The Ujjain verses show us otherwise, and show us something stranger and grander. The serpent-scimitar was a *device of kingship*. The pun in *varṇa* is doing real and weighty work: to "preserve the letters" is, in the very same breath, to "preserve the social order," so that the king who guards the alphabet thereby guards the proper arrangement of the world entire. Śiva, the Ujjain inscription elsewhere declares, is language itself personified; and the Paramāra kings, who held their crowns as Śiva's lieutenants, claimed the alphabet — the very substance of the god — as a magical weapon of their own.[21] To inscribe the serpent-scimitar on the wall of a royal Śiva temple was thus to forge a single object out of grammar, magic, and sovereignty: a talisman that protected language, dynasty, and cosmic order in one coiled and gleaming stroke.

This is why the device travelled. Versions of the serpentine scimitar attributed to Udayāditya are known not from Ujjain alone but from Dhar, the Paramāra capital, and from the temple cluster at Un — and now, with this slab, from Udaypur itself.[22] (At Un the diagram is built into the Chaubārā Ḍerā temple, a stone's throw from another inscription that names the great king Bhoja, Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor.)[23] Together these copies form a small family of "serpentine scimitars of letters," all radiating outward from the same two kings in the last decades of the eleventh century, like sparks struck from one flint. That Udaypur should possess one is exactly what we ought to have expected. The town and its tank were founded and named for Udayāditya himself, around 1080; its temple was his temple, his name made stone. Of all the shrines in Malwa, this was precisely the place to plant the dynasty's grammatical sword — and so it was planted, and here it stayed, flipped and forgotten, until a fieldworker turned the stone over in his imagination and read the snake.

## When the conquerors wrote

The Sanskrit archive is the record of a single civilization talking quietly to itself across two centuries. The next layer is something harsher, and we should not soften it: the record of that civilization being overtaken.

By the early fourteenth century the Paramāras were gone, swallowed — as that very late Jayasiṃha II inscription of 1309 had all but foretold — by the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate across Malwa. And the conquest left its mark on the Udayeśvara in the most literal way imaginable. At the rear of the temple, on the western side of the platform, a mosque was raised — built, in the standard practice of the age, partly out of the temple's own dismantled stone, the body of one faith taken up to house another.[24] Tradition and the local record place this work in the Tughluq period; the relevant order to dismantle and repurpose part of the complex is set around 1325 CE, in the years of the early Delhi Sultanate, and the construction of the rear mosque in the late 1330s.[25] Sources differ on the exact attribution and date — the Madhya Bharat site directory assigns the Tughluq mosque to the years 1336–1338 CE (A.H. 737–739), under Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, while Pande's study frames the dismantling order more broadly within the early Sultanate's reach into Malwa.[26] What is not in dispute is the physical fact, the wound itself: a corner of a Śaiva temple converted into a Muslim house of prayer, its rear hall fitted with a *miḥrāb* — the niche that turns the faithful toward Mecca — cut into a building that had been raised for the Rising Lord.[27]

And here the archive changes its very language. Set into this converted portion of the complex are two Persian inscriptions, in the Arabic script, recording the new dispensation.[28] They are the third great stratum of the Udayeśvara's text: after the Sanskrit of the founders and the grammatical magic of their grammarians, the Persian of the conquerors. We must be candid about the limits of our sources here, as honesty requires. The reference materials available for this chapter confirm the *existence* and the *placement* of the two Persian inscriptions — and confirm that they belong to the mosque-conversion of the rear of the temple — but they do not preserve a full translation of their contents. [GAP — the precise wording and full reading of the two Persian inscriptions are not given in the sources consulted; they are listed and located but not transcribed.] What we can say with confidence is structural rather than verbal: that at the very moment the temple ceased to be purely a Paramāra monument and became contested ground, the writing on its walls switched scripts, and a new sovereignty signed its name in a new hand.

There is something almost unbearably eloquent in that switch of script alone. One does not need to read the Persian to grasp what it means. For two and a half centuries the only writing the Udayeśvara had ever carried was Sanskrit — the god's own language, set down in the kings' own hand. Then, abruptly, a second alphabet appears, running right to left, professing a different god and a different king. The serpent-scimitar had promised to *preserve the letters and the social order*; the Persian inscriptions are the documentary proof that the order it guarded had, in the end, given way. The two layers face one another across the same building like the two halves of a single, painful sentence — and we, latecomers, read it whole.

## Afterlife: a permanent sunrise

What happened next is the part of the story that monuments are so rarely granted to tell, because so few of them survive to tell it. The Udayeśvara did.

The fourteenth-century mosque was not the last violence visited upon it. A persistent local tradition holds that the temple's carvings were defaced again in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, late in the seventeenth century — the same emperor whose 1682 demolition of the Vijay Mandir at nearby Bhilsa stands on firmer record.[29] The tradition at Udaypur is exactly that — a tradition, carried in local memory and repeated in the descriptive literature rather than fixed by a dated inscription — and it should be reported as such, honestly; but the mutilation of the temple's images is plain enough upon the stone, whatever the precise hand that wrought it.[30] For a long stretch, then, the Rising Lord rose over a wounded shrine: images battered, a corner surrendered to another faith, the great red-sandstone spire presiding over a complex that had passed out of the hands that built it. A god keeping vigil over his own injury.

Then the wheel turned once more. In the eighteenth century the Marathas rose to power across Malwa, and with them came the Scindia (Sindhia) house of Gwalior — Hindu rulers who restored Hindu worship to the great shrines their predecessors' enemies had repurposed. At the Udayeśvara the restoration left a precise and touching memorial. In 1775 CE a general of Mahādjī Scindia named Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave the temple a new face for its god: a *liṅga* sheathed in a fitted cover of brass — a *mukhaliṅga*, the brass mask that gives the aniconic stone of Śiva a human face — installed in the sanctum, where it abides to this very day.[31] One ought to pause, and pause long, over the modesty and the meaning of that gift. After the dismantling order, after the mosque, after the defacement, the act that restored the temple to worship was not some grand rebuilding but a single, careful, gleaming face of brass, fitted by a soldier-donor over the ancient stone. The Rising Lord was given back his features. (Sources record the donor's name and rank in slightly varying forms — a general of Mahādjī Scindia, named in the Pande study as Khaṇḍerāo Appājī — and agree on the date of 1775 and the fact of the brass face; the minor variation in how the donor is styled is worth noting honestly.)[32]

The final stratum of the archive is the one that makes this whole chapter possible at all: the modern campaigns of survey, conservation, and repair. Under the Gwalior State, the archaeology department mounted a sustained effort through the 1920s — the very campaign, recorded in its annual reports for the years 1923–24 through 1928–29, that copied and listed the temple's inscriptions and reset its displaced stones, including, as we have seen, the serpent-scimitar slab now mortared the wrong way round.[33] The work passed in time to the Archaeological Survey of India, whose own epigraphy branch and field surveys returned to the temple across the following decades, into the 1980s, to record and to conserve.[34] These restorers, too, wrote upon the building — in Hindi, the third living language of the archive — closing the eight-century sequence that had opened in *saṃvat* 1137 with a final layer dated to *saṃvat* 1985.[35] The men who saved the temple thereby became its last inscribers, their workmanlike notices the most recent silt to settle in the riverbed.

And so the corpus stands complete — or as complete as a living thing can ever be, which is to say never wholly, never finally — for the Udayeśvara is not a ruin, not a museum, not a cleared archaeological site fenced off against its own past. It is a temple still in worship. The brass-faced liṅga that Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave it in 1775 is still attended; the doors still open at morning; the Rising Lord still rises. Eight centuries of writing — Sanskrit praise and Sanskrit magic, Persian conquest, Hindi repair — run across the red sandstone like the rings of a great tree, each marking a year the monument lived through and outlived.

It is fitting to end the whole book on the image its title has promised us throughout. King Udayāditya took for his temple the name of the *Rising Lord* — Udayeśvara — and the foundation eulogy that opens this archive compared the king himself, with a poet's beautiful audacity, to "another sun, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his foes."[36] The metaphor was meant to last a reign. It has lasted, instead, the better part of a millennium. Conquerors came and signed their names in another script; emperors are said to have hacked at the god's face; dynasties fell and rose and fell again. And through all of it the great bhūmija spire has stood upon the Malwa plateau and caught the first light of every single morning on its seven storeys of carved sandstone — a permanent sunrise fixed in stone above a temple that has never quite stopped being a temple. The kings who built it are dust now, and the empires that fought over it are only names in a gazetteer. The Rising Lord is still rising. That, in the end, is what the archive on his walls records — not the triumph of any one of the powers that wrote there, but the endurance of the stone they all wrote on.
