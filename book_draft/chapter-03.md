# Chapter 3 — Raising the Udayeśvara: Patron, Date, and the Udayapur Praśasti

## I. A Foundation Sealed Thrice in Stone

Most cities gather; a few are decreed — and Udayapur, surely, belongs to that second and rarer kind, a place that did not pool slowly at a crossroads or a ford the way water finds its level, but was summoned, all at once, into being by a single royal act and given, from the very first breath of its life, a single royal name.[1] Its founder was Udayāditya, the Paramāra king of Malwa, and what he raised on this sun-struck stretch of the Vidisha plateau was no lone temple but a trinity conceived in one imagining: a walled city, a great temple to Śiva set burning at its heart, and beside them — vast, patient, gathering the sky — an artificial lake to cradle the monsoon and carry the town, gulp by measured gulp, through the long parched months.[2]

The three foundations breathe one name. The city is **Udayapura**, "the city of Udaya." The temple's god is **Udayeśvara**, the "Rising Lord." The reservoir is the **Udayasamudra**, the "sea of Udaya." And folded, like a secret signature, into all three is the king's own name — Udayāditya, in which *udaya* means "rising," as the sun rises out of the dark, and *āditya* is the sun itself, blazing and entire. To found this place, then, was to sign it. A traveller arriving from the west would pass through ramparts of giant uncemented stone, would stoop to drink from a lake that bore the king's name, and would bow at last before a god who bore it too.[3] City, temple, and tank: one gesture, one breath of intention, stamped three times over with the imprint of a single man.

And we should pause — we who come hurrying for the temple — upon the lake, because the modern visitor, eyes lifted to the spire, can walk past those waters without ever grasping what they meant. This is semi-arid central India, a country of fierce monsoon downpour followed by long thirsting months, where the upper Betwa runs hard in the wet season and thins to a whisper, or to nothing, after; a king who would plant a city here had first to teach it how to drink, and the **Udayasamudra** — excavated beside the temple at Udayāditya's command — was the very engine that made the foundation possible at all.[4] For in the old Indian understanding of kingship, the digging of a great tank was itself a sacred and meritorious thing, a public benefaction set upon the same moral height as the raising of a temple. Udayāditya did both at a stroke, and named both for himself. The "sea of Udaya" was no ornament beside the holy city. It was the condition of its life — the held breath that let the place go on breathing.

This chapter, then, is about the making of that foundation: about the man who willed it, the years of labour it drank, the dates its own stones still carry like rings in living wood, and the praise-poem the eleventh century carved to explain him to all who would come after. In the chapters before, the temple has stood as a finished thing, a building to be read. Here, instead, we watch it rise.

## II. The Patron: Udayāditya Paramāra

By the time Udayāditya came to the throne, the Paramāra house was old, illustrious, and in trouble — three things that seldom keep such easy company. Its capital was Dhar; its greatest name was **Bhoja**, that scholar-king of the early eleventh century whose court became a byword for learning, and under whose patronage the architectural treatise the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* — one of the earliest texts to name the very Bhūmija style in which the Udayeśvara would one day be built — was compiled.[5] But Bhoja's death gave way to hard and narrowing years. The Paramāra kingdom, pressed on every side by its neighbours, had all but come apart at the seams, and it fell to Udayāditya to gather the scattered threads and weave them whole again.

He was, in the steady verdict of the sources, the king who restored Paramāra fortunes.[6] Anupa Pande, whose decade-long study of the temple stands like bedrock beneath much of this book, puts it without flourish: Udayāditya "faced many challenges in consolidating his empire," and it was beneath his hand that Malwa recovered its footing and "witnessed political stability and flourished as a leading center of religious and cultural activities."[7] The recovery and the foundation belong together, as cause and its crown. A king does not found a new city and lift a masterpiece above it in a season of collapse; he does such a thing only when he has wrested enough peace to build. Udayapur is the monument of a recovery — a kingdom risen again from its knees, given permanent form, given flesh, in red sandstone.

Here, though, we meet the first of the difficulties that honest history cannot smooth away, and would not wish to: the sources do not quite agree on when Udayāditya's reign began. Pande's study has him "ascend the throne in 1070 CE."[8] But D. R. Patil's survey of the cultural heritage of the old Madhya Bharat state, and the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* in its wake, set his reign earlier — Patil's chapters give dates of roughly 1058–1087, and the *Gazetteer*'s history simply names Udayāditya as the successor of Bhoja who "built the magnificent Nilkanthesvar temple at Udaypur."[9] The gap is real and will not be wished closed: an accession around 1059 against one around 1070, more than a decade lying between. It is the kind of discrepancy that rises, naturally as mist, from the very nature of the evidence — regnal dates in this age are reconstructed from scattered inscriptions and genealogies, and reasonable scholars, bending over the same fragments, reach slightly different reckonings. We need not resolve it to tell the story; we need only hold it gently up to the light. What matters is that the temple's *own* dates, as we shall see, are far firmer than its founder's accession — for the temple carved them into itself, and stone keeps faith.

One thing more the sources let us glimpse: the king's temperament, or at least the face it turned to the world. He was a Śaiva, a worshipper of Śiva, and the god he installed at the heart of his city was Śiva under a name of his own coining. Yet the temple's sculpture, read in the chapters before, whispers of a particular emphasis. Among the many forms of Śiva carved upon the walls is **Mṛtyuñjaya**, the "Conqueror of Death," seated deep in meditation and wreathed in serpents, a form invoked — in Pande's reading of the iconography — to grant victory and to overcome the danger of death upon the battlefield.[10] For a king who had spent his very strength holding a contested throne, the choice is telling, almost confessional. The Udayeśvara is the temple of a fighter who wished his god to be a conqueror — and who wished, with a most human and particular dread, not to die in battle.

## III. The Twenty-One-Year Workshop

We can date the building of the Udayeśvara with a precision rare for an eleventh-century Indian monument, and we can do so because the temple is, in part, its own archive — a keeper of its own memory. Its porches and walls carry inscriptions, and from them two dates rise to the surface, bracketing the whole long enterprise like dawn and dusk about a single day.

Construction was begun, by the temple's own testimony, in **Vikrama Saṃvat 1116** — which is to say **A.D. 1059**. The Vikrama Saṃvat, or *saṃvat* for short, is the Indian calendar era most often spoken in these inscriptions; it runs roughly fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era, so that subtracting about fifty-seven from a Vikrama year yields the Christian date. V.S. 1116 falls thus in A.D. 1059, the year the masons first broke the patient ground.[11] And the work was crowned — its great victory-flag lifted, at the last, upon the very summit of the tower — in **V.S. 1137**, A.D. **1080**.[12]

Between those two dates lies a span of twenty-one years: twenty-one years of a royal workshop bent over a single building. That is the human fact behind the finished stone — the warm and aching truth of it. The Udayeśvara was not flung up in haste; it was the sustained devotion of more than two decades, long enough that a master who laid the foundations as a young man might have stood, grey now, to see the flag raised in his maturity, long enough for the carving that so stirred the early surveyors — sculpture the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* judged "finer than Khajuraho" — to be brought to its high and breathing finish.[13] When Chapter 1 marked the small human figure carved clambering up the north-west face of the spire, a flag-bearer frozen forever mid-ascent with the victory banner he means to plant at the very top, it was gazing upon the iconographic echo of this very moment: completion itself, the *dhvaja* raised, the long labour laid at last to rest.[14]

And it is worth dwelling, a while, on what such a workshop meant for the place that grew up around it. The later memory of Udayapur has kept one small, telling thing. Among the satellite structures of the town the *Gazetteer* records the **Pisanari-ka-Mandir** — the "grinding-woman's temple" — said to have been built by a poor woman from the earnings she made grinding flour for the temple's masons.[15] The story may be legend; but legends of this kind keep a real social truth folded inside them like grain in the husk. A royal building project of twenty-one years was an economy as well as a monument — a living thing that drew labour to itself, fed it and paid it, and threw off surplus enough that even a flour-grinder at the very bottom of the chain could, in the end, endow a small shrine of her own. The great temple sat at the centre of a wide and quiet web of lesser making.

The dating evidence deserves one note of caution and one of corroboration. The caution: the inscriptions are not all neatly dated, and the surviving epigraphic record at Udayapur is more tangled than a single tidy pair of dates would suggest. The corroboration, though, is striking. The standard corpus of Paramāra inscriptions lists, for the reign of Udayāditya, two stone records at Udayapur itself — one assigned to A.D. 1080, and one explicitly dated **V.S. 1137 / A.D. 1080**.[16] The flag-raising date, then, is no lone and lonely tradition; it is anchored in the catalogued epigraphy of the dynasty. The temple's own claim about its completion is confirmed from beyond itself — voice answering voice across the centuries.

That same corpus lets us watch Udayapur go on speaking long after its founder had fallen silent. The town Udayāditya raised became a place where later Paramāra kings, too, pressed their words into stone: Udayāditya's successor **Naravarman** in V.S. 1151 / A.D. 1094, and a long procession of later rulers after him — a Devapāla in the thirteenth century, a Jayasiṃha in the early fourteenth, V.S. 1366 / A.D. 1309 — each adding his own record to the town's slowly accumulating stone.[17] A city founded as one king's signature became, across two centuries and a half, a dynastic place of record — proof, in stone, that the foundation took root, that Udayapur lived on as something far more than a single reign's vanity. The temple was a magnet for memory: kings came to it to be remembered, generation after generation, exactly as its founder must have ached and hoped they would.

The fabric of the town bears out the same picture — a place that grew up around its great temple and kept on growing, like a tree thickening ring by ring. Besides the Pisanari-ka-Mandir, the *Gazetteer* records at Udayapur a cluster of further structures: a pillared hall of the eleventh century known as the **Barakhambi**, the "twelve-pillared"; the **Bijamandal** or *Ghadiyalan-ka-makan*, the "clockman's house," bound up with the invocation of the Sun-god; a cavalry stepwell, the *Ghod-daud-ki-baodi*; and, from the later centuries of the town's life, a succession of mosques.[18] Not all of these belong to the foundation moment, and several rise out of the very different world of the Sultanate and Mughal centuries. Yet taken together they show what Udayāditya's act had set in motion: not a lone temple stranded in an empty field, but a living town — walled, watered, worshipping — that went on building for centuries after the royal workshop had packed away its chisels and the dust had settled on the silent yard.

## IV. The Udayapur Praśasti

If the dated inscriptions tell us *when* the temple was built, another inscription tells us — in the high rhetorical register of its age — *why*, or at least how the act of building was meant to be understood, and held, and remembered. This is the **Udayapur Praśasti**.

The word *praśasti* means "praise" — a praise-inscription, a panegyric, a poem of laudation cut into stone so that admiration itself might outlast the admired. The genre was a fixture of medieval Indian royal culture. When a king founded a temple or a city, or won a war, or wished to fix his glory against the erosions of forgetting, he commissioned a court poet to compose a praśasti: a Sanskrit poem, often of considerable length and learning, that set out his lineage, rehearsed his virtues and his victories, and bound his name fast to the pious work his hands had performed. The praśasti is part genealogy, part eulogy, part dedication — and it is, for the modern historian, among the most precious of all sources, because it is the very voice in which a dynasty wished to be remembered, carved by the dynasty's own hand.[19]

The Udayapur Praśasti is exactly such a poem, set upon the temple Udayāditya raised. Its governing image — and this is the thing to grasp, the key that turns the whole — is the sun. We have already met the logic at its root: the king's name is Udayāditya, the "rising sun"; his city, his god, his lake all carry *udaya*, the rising, within their names. The praśasti takes that solar logic and makes it sing. It does not merely call the king a great warrior; it makes of him a second sun, and it builds its central conceit out of the most ancient of all Indian metaphors — light against the dark, the sun dispelling the long night.

The single line that survives most vividly in the sources, the line Chapter 1 quoted in part, runs in full thus:

> "...another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword, and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."[20]

It repays slow reading, for every element of it is doing deliberate and shining work. The king is "another sun" — a second *āditya*, which is, of course, his own name unfolded into a simile, a name turned inside out to become an image. His enemies are "dense darkness," the natural antithesis of the sun, and the conceit lets the poet praise the foes even as the king annihilates them: they are "magnanimous," a touch of chivalry that magnifies the victor precisely by dignifying the vanquished. The "rays issuing from his strong sword" fuse the two registers of the image at a single stroke — the sword's flash *is* the sun's ray, martial and solar at once, so that the very act of war becomes an act of illumination, slaughter transfigured into dawn. And the closing turn is the whole political point of the thing: by destroying the darkness, the king is "gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour." The sun that scorches his enemies is the selfsame sun that warms his subjects. Here is a precise poetic statement of the ideal of kingship the temple was built to embody — the king as the very fountainhead of light, whose violence against his foes is the very thing that gives his people their peace and their joy.

We should be careful, even so, about what such a poem can and cannot tell us. A praśasti is not a chronicle; it is an idealised, ornamental, frankly partisan portrait, composed to flatter and to last. Its description of Udayāditya as a world-conquering second sun is conventional hyperbole, the standard idiom in which any successful medieval king was praised, and we cannot read off from it a literal ledger of battles won.[21] But it is not therefore worthless — far from it. Read for what it *is* — a statement of royal self-presentation — the Udayapur Praśasti tells us a great deal. It tells us that Udayāditya wished to be remembered as a restorer, a bringer of light after the dark, which fits to the very seam the historical situation of a king who hauled the Paramāra kingdom back from its edge. It tells us that the solar imagery was no accident of naming but a fully worked-out programme, a single idea — *udaya*, the rising — drawn out from the king's name through his city, his god, his lake, and on into the poetry carved upon his temple's walls. And it confirms, from the inside, what the architecture proclaims from without: that this whole foundation was conceived as a sunrise made permanent, a kingdom's recovery held forever at the trembling instant of dawn.

Here, too, we must be honest about the limits of our sources. The full text of the Udayapur Praśasti — its length, its complete genealogy of the Paramāra line, the particular victories and gifts it may name — is not preserved in the materials underlying this book; what survives to us here is the single, justly famous solar line and the framework of solar imagery around it. ## V. The Meaning of the Making

Step back from the dates and the poem, and the shape of the whole enterprise comes clear, as a coastline comes clear when the boat draws off from shore. Udayāditya did something of unusual coherence. He took a single idea — rising, the sunrise, his own name — and he built it out at every scale at once: into the name of a city, into the name of a god, into the name of a lake, into a praise-poem that made of him a second sun, and into the very architecture of a temple whose Bhūmija tower was designed to look as though it grew straight up out of the earth and strained toward the sky. The Udayeśvara is sometimes described, in this book and elsewhere, as a building conceived as a single upward gesture. The making of it, traced here, shows that the gesture reached far beyond the stone. The temple is the largest and most lasting utterance of a programme that ran, like a thread of light, through an entire royal foundation.

And the programme carried a political edge that the praśasti makes plain. This was the work of a king who had inherited a kingdom in trouble and carried it back into safety; the imagery of darkness dispelled and light restored was no idle court flattery but a claim about what his reign had truly achieved. To found Udayapur — city, temple, and "sea of Udaya" together — and to crown it with twenty-one years of a royal workshop's patient labour, was to make that claim permanent, to set it past the reach of forgetting. The dated stones tell us the labour ran from 1059 to 1080; the praśasti tells us what those two decades were *for*. They were for fixing, in the most durable medium an eleventh-century king could command, the memory of a kingdom that had risen again into the light.

It worked. The dynasty that built the temple did not outlast the medieval centuries; the city itself dwindled, in the end, to the modest walled village a traveller finds today. But the foundation held its meaning, as a vessel holds water. Later Paramāra kings came back to Udayapur to carve their own records; later restorers, Maratha and modern, kept the Rising Lord in worship. Nine hundred years on, the sun the poet promised has not set. The temple still stands on the Malwa plateau exactly as its founder meant it to stand — a permanent sunrise, in red stone, carrying his name.
