# Chapter 5 — The Temple's Visual World: Sculpture and Iconography

## I. A Walk That Is a Theology

In the last chapter we held the Udayeśvara in our hands as architecture — the rotated plan, the seven facets and the seven storeys, the climbing rows of miniature spires that mount the air like a slow exhalation of stone. But to a worshipper who came up onto the platform nine hundred years ago, the building would have spoken first, before any thought of geometry, as a crowd — a throng, a multitude, a great gathering held breathless in rock. Every surface that could carry a figure carries one. Gods stand in the faceted niches of the walls; goddesses dance in the arches of the tower; guardians and planets and river-deities and celestial musicians press into the doorways and crowd the eaves, as if the divine world had leaned in close to look back at us. The building is densely, almost vertiginously, populated — and the population, we soon discover, is not random. It is arranged.

That is the single most important thing to grasp about the sculpture of the Udayeśvara, and it is what this chapter sets out to show. The carving is not a garment of decoration thrown over a structure; it is a system laid into one, a logic carried in the bone. The deities are placed according to rule — by direction, by rank, by a theological reasoning patient as water finding its level — so that the temple's outer skin becomes a kind of map of the divine order, a chart of heaven pressed into mineral. To walk slowly around the building, reading the niches in sequence, is to be led, gently and deliberately, through a vision of the cosmos with Śiva burning at its centre. The deeper philosophy behind that vision — the metaphysics of Śaiva Siddhānta — belongs to the next chapter. Here our task is humbler and nearer to the hand: to identify what is carved, to see where it stands and why, and to register the plain religious sense of the imagery before we dare to ask what, in the end, it means.

We will begin with the ordering principle that governs the whole outer wall — the eight guardians of the directions, the sentinels who hold the quarters of the world. Then we will turn to the many forms of Śiva himself, the presiding lord whose theology the building exists to declare. After that we will meet the goddesses and the celestial women, who soften and enrich the programme as light softens stone toward evening; and finally we will linger — for some things ask to be lingered over — upon the rarities, the images so unusual that they whisper something particular about this temple, this king, and this single, vanished moment.

A word of method before we set out on the circuit. Almost everything described here is recorded in the close iconographic study by Anupa Pande, who spent more than a decade reading the temple's imagery against the Sanskrit ritual and architectural manuals — a pilgrimage of attention I can only stand in some awe of.[1] Where her account places a deity on a named facet, this chapter follows her; where the placement is only general, the text will say so plainly, rather than feign a precision the sources do not give. The Udayeśvara has also suffered, as living things suffer: many of its lower figures were deliberately defaced in later centuries, and some niches now hold only broken stone, a silence where a face once was.[2] What survives, though — and this is the wonder of it — is more than enough to read the design.

## II. The Eight Guardians of the Quarters

The skeleton of the whole sculptural programme — the frame on which all the rest is hung — is a set of eight deities called the **Dikpālas**, literally the "guardians of the directions" (*dik*, direction; *pāla*, protector). In Indian cosmology the eight directions of the compass are not empty geometry, not cold abstraction; each is a province with a living regent who rules it, and the standard set assigns one god to each. The cardinal and intercardinal points run, in their appointed order: **Indra** in the east, **Agni** in the south-east, **Yama** in the south, **Nirṛti** in the south-west, **Varuṇa** in the west, **Vāyu** in the north-west, **Kubera** in the north, and **Īśāna** in the north-east.[3]

The convention that makes them so useful to a temple's maker is quietly beautiful: each guardian is carved on the wall that faces the very quarter he governs. The result is a built compass, a working instrument of the sacred. Stand at any corner of the Udayeśvara and you can, in principle, orient yourself by the gods — the deity in the niche tells you which way you are looking, so that to be lost here is to have only forgotten how to read. This was not unique to Udaypur — the placement of the Dikpālas by direction is a widespread norm of medieval North Indian temple-building — but the Udayeśvara carries the scheme out with care, and several of its guardians are among the most vivid figures on the walls.[4]

Read them, then, as a circuit, a slow turning of the body that is also a turning of the mind. **Indra**, regent of the east and king of the gods, stands in a slightly flexed posture (*ābhaṅga*, that gentle bend of the body away from the straight vertical, as though stone itself had learned to breathe) and holds his two great attributes: the thunderbolt, the *vajra*, the weapon with which he splits storm-clouds and enemies alike, and the *aṅkuśa*, the elephant-goad. Beside him stands his mount, the celestial elephant **Airāvata**, so that the identification is doubly sure — secured by weapon and by vehicle, those two things that fix a Hindu deity's name more faithfully than any face.[5]

At the south-east corner — the quarter of fire — comes **Agni**, the fire-god himself, the divine priest who bears the offering up to the gods upon his flames. He too stands in the flexed *ābhaṅga* pose; but where Indra is a king, Agni is shown as something older and stranger, something out of the deep memory of the sacrifice: pot-bellied, with a stylised beard, holding a spear (*śakti*), a book, and a water-pot, and seated upon — or attended by — his vehicle the ram.[6] The book and the water-pot mark him as a sacrificial, priestly power; the ram is his ancient mount, the animal whose form the sacrificial fire was once imagined to ride.

Turn now to the south, the direction of death, and you meet its lord. **Yama**, regent of the south and judge of the dead, is given dark limbs and a fierce expression with bulging eyes, and he carries the two instruments of his office: the rod of justice, the *daṇḍa*, with which he chastises the wicked, and the noose with which he draws the soul out of the dying body, as one draws a thread from cloth. His mount, the buffalo, stands at his side.[7] Yama is a deity we are meant to find frightening — and the carver, with a candour I cannot help admiring, has obliged.

The south-western quarter belongs to a still grimmer figure, **Nirṛti**, regent of decay and dissolution, the very personification of misfortune and of the dark direction. He is shown naked and emaciated, standing upon a corpse — a *naravāhana*, a "man-vehicle," the human body that serves him for a mount — and holding a sword and a severed head.[8] This is among the most unsettling images on the building, and we shall return below to the wider taste for the macabre that it represents; for now it is enough to note, with a kind of shiver, how exactly the iconography fits the theology. The guardian of the quarter of ruin is himself a figure of ruin — wasted, naked, treading upon death.

The west is the province of the waters, the deep. **Varuṇa**, ancient lord of the cosmic ocean and of moral order, stands upon a *makara* — that composite aquatic creature, part crocodile, part fish, which is the emblem of the unfathomed deep — and holds a noose, the *pāśa*, the cord with which, in the oldest hymns of all, he binds the liar and the wrongdoer.[9] And to the north-west, the quarter of the winds, stands **Vāyu**, the wind-god, green of complexion, seated upon a deer, holding a waving banner or flag whose very flutter is the visible sign, the made-flesh proof, of the moving air he embodies.[10]

That accounts for six of the eight. Of the remaining two of the classical set — Kubera, lord of wealth in the north, and Īśāna, the auspicious aspect of Śiva himself in the north-east — the surviving record at Udaypur grows less certain, and honesty asks that we admit it. Pande's account notes an **Īśāna** figure, three-eyed and seated on a bull, wearing the piled matted hair of Śiva (the *jaṭāmukuṭa*), but observes that it is placed "alternatively" — that is, not in the textbook north-east position but somewhere adapted to the temple's own scheme.[11] For the eighth guardian, Kubera of the north, the sources read here do not give a clear individual notice; and rather than conjure one from nothing, this chapter leaves the matter open, an honest gap left unfilled. What the circuit shows, even with those uncertainties, is the principle at work, steady beneath the doubt. The walls are ordered. Before a single figure of Śiva is so much as read, the building has already declared that the world it depicts is a governed world, parcelled among regents, each one waiting in his appointed place. The Dikpālas are the frame. The forms of Śiva are the picture set within it.

## III. The Many Forms of Śiva

A Śaiva temple exists to make Śiva present — and the quiet genius of medieval Śaiva art is that it never tried to do so with a single image. Śiva is, in the theology, both the formless absolute and the god of a thousand stories; and the Udayeśvara gives us the second, generously and overflowingly, so that through it we may intuit the first — as one tastes the sea in a single cupped handful. Across the building he appears in form after form — dancing, destroying, meditating, married, half-female, fused with other gods — and each form carries its own weather, its own mood and meaning. To move among them is to feel the full range of the deity the temple was raised to house.

### The Cosmic Dancer

The form to which the Udayeśvara returns most insistently, like a refrain a heart keeps returning to, is Śiva as the dancer — **Naṭeśa**, the "Lord of Dance," caught in the *tāṇḍava*, that fierce cosmic dance whose rhythm sustains and dissolves the universe in a single beat. This is not the serene dance one might idly imagine; the *tāṇḍava* is violent, world-ending energy, and the carvers render it with one knee flung high in a posture the manuals name the *ūrdhvajānu karaṇa* — the dance-pose of the "raised knee," the leg lifted dramatically across the body in a stillness that somehow blazes with motion.[12]

The grandest of these dancing Śivas presides over the very brow of the temple. Above the eastern entrance, set in the great projecting fronton of the tower — the *śukanāsa* — a multi-armed Naṭeśa performs the *tāṇḍava* in the raised-knee pose, his matted hair gathered into a tall conical crown (the *jaṭā-kalāpa*), his face held balanced and composed even in the very storm of the dance, surrounded by smaller dancing goddesses and by the dwarfish attendant spirits called *gaṇas*.[13] It is the first great image a worshipper climbing the original eastern stair would have met, high overhead — the god dancing the world into being and out of it, set like a sign above the door of his own house, the first word of the whole long sentence.

And the dancing Śiva recurs, and recurs, and will not let us go. At the base of each of the tower's three other vertical spines — north, south, and west — sits a sculpted medallion within a large arch (the *śūrasenaka*), and each of these is centred upon Śiva dancing in the same raised-knee posture: a powerful figure on the west spine; another on the south, set above a small pyramidal shrine; another on the north, shown in full *tāṇḍava* above a seated Śiva who holds a trident and the skull-topped staff called the *khaṭvāṅga*.[14] The repetition is deliberate, and it is everything. The dance is not one scene among many; it is the temple's recurring refrain, the gesture, repeated like a pulse, by which Śiva is shown to be the active power that turns the wheel of the cosmos.

### The Destroyer of the Three Cities

If the dance shows Śiva as cosmic force, another form shows him as cosmic warrior, and the temple needed both. On the south-western part of the hall wall stands **Tripurāntaka** — the "Ender of Tripura," Śiva in the act of destroying the *Tripura*, the three flying fortress-cities of the demons, which in the myth he burned to ash with a single arrow loosed from a single bow.[15] The Udayeśvara's Tripurāntaka is given a muscular torso and a powerful warrior's stance, the body braced as in the very instant of the draw, the breath held before release.[16] The myth is one of the great set-pieces of Śaiva storytelling, and its meaning is plain as daylight: Śiva is the destroyer of the strongholds of evil, the god who, against an enemy that seemed unassailable, needed only the one shot. For the temple of a king fighting to hold his realm, the resonance hardly needs spelling out — though, as we shall see, another image on these walls draws that royal resonance sharper still.

### The Conqueror of Death

That image stands on the west, and it is the one most often singled out as the key that unlocks the whole programme. Beneath the dancing Naṭeśa on the western spine of the tower sits a six-armed figure of **Mṛtyuñjaya** — the "Conqueror of Death" (*mṛtyu*, death; *jaya*, victory), Śiva as the vanquisher of mortality itself, the one who looks death in its hollow face and is not moved. He is shown not in motion but in stillness, a stillness deeper than any dance: seated in a meditative *yogamudrā*, the posture of yogic absorption, his body wreathed in serpents — snakes for earrings (*sarpa-kuṇḍala*), a snake for the sacred thread that crosses the chest (*sarpa-yajñopavīta*).[17][18] The serpent, which sheds its skin and seems to be born again out of itself, is the natural emblem of a god who has mastered death — life that keeps refusing to end.

Pande is explicit about why this figure is here, and her plainness only deepens the poignancy. Mṛtyuñjaya was "specifically invoked to grant victory and overcome the danger of death on the battlefield."[19] Recall the king who built this temple. Udayāditya came to the Paramāra throne in a time of crisis and spent his reign fighting to hold together a threatened realm; his own foundation inscription praises him as a sword-wielding second sun scattering his foes.[20] A six-armed Conqueror of Death, set upon the western face of the royal temple, is no abstract theological flourish, no idle ornament. It is, in the most intimate and pointed sense, the personal deity of a warrior-king — Śiva asked to do for the patron precisely what the patron most desperately needed: to keep death from the battlefield, and to grant the gift of victory. Of all the temple's images, this is the one in which theology and a single human life are most nearly fused into one breath.

### Śiva Half-and-Half, and Śiva Married

Two further forms show Śiva in relationship rather than in action — the god turned toward another. On the south-eastern part of the hall stands **Ardhanārīśvara**, the "Lord who is half woman" — Śiva fused down the very midline with his consort Pārvatī into a single shared body. The right, male half holds a trident and keeps the hard contours of the god; the left, female half holds a mirror and is given a rounded breast and the fuller, flowing curve of hip.[21] It is one of the subtlest of all Hindu images, a way of saying in patient stone that the divine is neither male nor female but the union of both, the god and his energy (*śakti*) inseparable as light from its shining. Elsewhere the same union is shown more gently, as a couple at rest: **Umā-Maheśvara**, Śiva ("the Great Lord," Maheśvara) seated at ease in the relaxed *lalitāsana* pose with his consort Umā upon his lap, flanked by a lion and by Śiva's bull Nandi — the divine marriage shown not as metaphysical fusion but as something nearer, warmer, more familiar: domestic tenderness rendered eternal.[22]

### The Fierce Form and the Family

Śiva also has a terrible aspect, and the temple, faithful to the whole of him, does not look away. On the south-eastern hall wall stands **Mahābhairava** — "the great terror," Śiva in his most fearsome guise — multi-armed, in a flexed stance, holding a shield, a sword, a *ḍamaru* (that small hour-glass drum which beats out the rhythm of the dance), and even a hill, wearing a garland of skulls and a headdress of living snakes.[23] And around the lord the carvers have gathered his household, as a family gathers around a hearth. On the facets of the sanctum itself appear his elephant-headed son **Gaṇeśa** with his own female counterpart **Gaṇeśānī**, four-armed and holding an axe and a bowl of sweets; the fierce attendant **Vīrabhadra**, Śiva's avenging emanation, shown on the rear facet in the act of strangling a figure who is most likely Dakṣa, the offending father-in-law of the great sacrifice-myth; the bull-headed devotee **Nandīśvara**; and the gaunt three-legged dancer **Bhṛṅgī**, that skeletal sage so wholly devoted to Śiva alone that the myth left him barely able to stand upon his own bones.[24] Śiva's other son appears too, upon the wall belonging to the upper directions: **Kārttikeya**, also called Skanda, the god of war, six-faced (*Ṣaḍānana*) and twelve-armed, riding his great peacock and bristling with spear, bow, arrow, and shield.[25]

Read all together, these forms compose a portrait of a single deity in the fullness of his range — dancer and destroyer, ascetic and bridegroom, terror and tender father. The temple does not ask the worshipper to choose among them, to take one and forsake the rest. It surrounds him with all of them at once, so that the building itself becomes the argument, written in stone and standing all around, that these are one god.

## IV. The Goddesses and the Celestial Women

If Śiva is the temple's subject, the feminine divine is its constant companion, walking everywhere at his side; and it appears in two distinct registers, two keys of the same music: the great goddesses, who are powers in their own sovereign right, and the *surasundarīs*, the celestial beauties who quicken the surfaces with grace.

The major goddesses are placed, like the gods, with intent — nothing here is left to chance. On the north-eastern part of the hall — and the north-east, remember, is the auspicious quarter — stands **Mahiṣāsuramardinī**, the "Slayer of the Buffalo Demon": the goddess Durgā in her supreme heroic act, ten-armed, her body thrown into the most dynamic of all poses (*atibhaṅga*, the extreme bend, the body bent like a drawn bow), driving her trident into the chest of the demon Mahiṣāsura while her lion savages him.[26] This is the central image of the goddess's own great myth, the very moment when the gods' combined energy, gathered and poured into a single female form, accomplishes what none of them could do alone. And beside her, in deliberate and breathtaking contrast, comes **Cāmuṇḍā**, the goddess in her terrifying aspect: emaciated to a skeleton, her belly sunken, dancing upon a corpse, holding a dagger and a skull-cup and garlanded with snakes.[27] The pairing of the radiant warrior-goddess and the death's-head dancer, set side by side upon the same quarter of the building, is itself a theological statement carved into rock — that the divine feminine, no less than Śiva, holds within her both the saving and the terrible, the mercy and the abyss.

The goddesses appear, too, as the consorts who complete the gods, in the divine couples carved upon the sanctum's northern facet: **Umā-Maheśvara** again, **Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa** (Viṣṇu with his arm encircling Lakṣmī's waist), and **Brahmā-Brahmāṇī**.[28] And the goddess of learning, **Sarasvatī**, appears in more than one form — a seated Sarasvatī associated with knowledge, shown unusually without her customary lute (*vīṇā*) and defined instead by the attributes of text and scholarship.[29] We will come back, presently, to a far more startling Sarasvatī.

Then there are the *surasundarīs* — literally the "beautiful women of the gods," the celestial nymphs and dancers who are a near-universal presence upon the walls of medieval North Indian temples, as constant as light upon water. They are not, strictly, deities to be worshipped; they are something subtler — the auspicious, life-affirming beauty of the divine world made visible, women shown dancing, adorning themselves, holding mirrors, swaying in graceful triple-bent poses among the major figures. At the Udayeśvara they crowd the arches and the side faces of the tower, and the dancing goddesses who attend the great Naṭeśa of the *śukanāsa*, mirroring his raised-knee dance, belong to this same celestial company.[30] It would be a mistake — and a small impoverishment of the soul — to read them as mere ornament. In the visual theology of the temple they are essential: they are the sensuous abundance of a cosmos wholly pervaded by the god, the standing proof that the world Śiva dances is not bleak but fertile, beautiful, and unmistakably alive.

## V. The Rare and the Striking

Every great temple keeps its surprises — images that depart from the expected repertoire and, in departing, tell us something precise and unrepeatable. The Udayeśvara holds a remarkable number of them, gifts to the patient eye, and three in particular ask to be looked at closely.

### A Dancing Sarasvatī

In one of the arches flanking the great fronton over the eastern entrance is a representation of **Sarasvatī as a dancer** — and this is genuinely rare, rare enough to stop the breath. Sarasvatī, the goddess of learning, speech, and music, is almost always shown seated and serene, the calm patroness of scholars; to find her *dancing*, in the very same raised-knee posture as Naṭeśa, a swan carved near her right leg, holding the hour-glass drum, wearing a necklace worked to imitate long lotus-buds, is unusual enough to demand an explanation.[31]

The likeliest explanation is dynastic, and it is a tender one. The Paramāras were a house of learning, and the greatest of them, Bhoja — Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor — was a scholar-king who endowed a temple to Sarasvatī at his capital (the famous Bhojaśālā of Dhara). Pande reads the dancing Sarasvatī of Udaypur as an echo of that family devotion, the goddess of learning given pride of place near the temple's very front as a sign of the Paramāra patronage of letters.[32] It is a small but telling instance of how a temple's iconography can carry a dynasty's image of itself, its quiet pride: even in a Śaiva building dedicated to a warrior-king's god, the house's love of scholarship found its way, dancing, onto the wall.

### A Goddess Seated on a Corpse

On the southern side of the great fronton, among the tiers of seated goddesses, is one of the temple's most arresting images, one that holds the eye and will not release it: a goddess — named in the sources as **Śreyā** or **Śrī** — seated upon a *preta*, a corpse or ghost, and holding an iguana (*godhā*) in one lower hand, a combination read as symbolising the destruction of enemies.[33] The corpse-seat is a charged and terrible motif. To sit enthroned upon a dead body is to declare absolute dominion over death and over the forces of dissolution — the same grim vocabulary we met in Nirṛti treading his corpse-mount and Cāmuṇḍā dancing upon hers. Here, though, that vocabulary is turned toward a goddess of fortune and prosperity, fusing auspiciousness with the macabre in a way that is hard to parallel anywhere, and that points, once again, toward the theme of triumph over enemies which runs like a bright thread through this royal temple's whole programme.

### The God Who Is Four Gods

The single most ambitious image on the building — the boldest claim it dares to make — is a **syncretic deity** in the central niche of the eastern fronton, set below the dancing Naṭeśa. Pande names it **Hariharārkāpitāmaha**, a compound that unpacks, like a folded thing opening, into its components: *Hari* (Viṣṇu), *Hara* (Śiva), *Arka* (Sūrya, the sun), and *Pitāmaha* (Brahmā, the "grandfather" creator). It is, in other words, a single figure combining four of the great gods of the Hindu pantheon into one shared body.[34]

Syncretic images of this kind are the temple's most explicit theological statements, its plainest professions of faith, and the building offers a small graded series of them, a rising stair. The simplest is **Harihara**, the half-Viṣṇu, half-Śiva figure on the south-eastern hall wall, its two halves marked by two different crowns — the matted-hair *jaṭāmukuṭa* of Śiva upon the one side, the cylindrical *kirīṭamukuṭa* of Viṣṇu upon the other — and holding Viṣṇu's conch (*śaṅkha*) and discus (*cakra*).[35] More complex is a three-faced, ten-armed goddess-panel fusing **Gāyatrī, Sāvitrī, and Sarasvatī**, a composite of the great Vedic and solar feminine powers holding lotus-bud, rosary, noose, and book.[36] And the most ambitious of all is the four-fold Hariharārkāpitāmaha, in which Śiva, Viṣṇu, the Sun, and Brahmā are declared, in a single carved body, to be aspects of one reality.

This is the threshold at which the sculpture begins to reach toward the philosophy of the chapter that follows — where stone strains toward thought. A composite of four gods is no confession of indecision, no muddle; it is a confession of faith — the assertion, central to the inclusive theism of the period and to Śaiva Siddhānta in particular, that the many gods are forms of a single supreme principle, many rivers of one sea. That principle, for this temple, is Śiva: he is the dancer who presides above the composite, the lord whose house this is, the one into whom the others are gathered as streams are gathered home. The four-faced image makes the claim visible. The next chapter will follow it inward, into thought.

## VI. Reading the Whole

Step back, finally, from the individual figures and consider the design entire — let the eye unfocus until the crowd resolves into a single shape. What the Udayeśvara offers is not a gallery but a structured argument, a sermon in stone that builds and builds. Its outer skin is ordered first by direction — the eight Dikpālas setting the world in its quarters, each regent waiting in his place — and then filled, within that frame, with the forms of Śiva: the dancer who turns the cosmos, the warrior who burns the cities of evil, the conqueror who masters death, the bridegroom, the ascetic, the half-female lord, the father of gods. Around them and among them move the goddesses, saving and terrible by turns, and the celestial women whose beauty itself declares the world worth saving. And at the front, over the door, the building's boldest images gather the many gods into one, and set Śiva, dancing, above them all.

It is, as the king's own praise-poem might have it, a sunrise rendered in stone — a whole divine order shown rising into visibility upon the walls of a single temple, dawn made permanent. The placement is exact, the repetition deliberate, the rarities pointed and full of meaning. A goddess of learning dances for a dynasty of scholars; a six-armed Conqueror of Death sits on the west wall for a king who fought simply to live; four gods share one body to declare that, in the end, beneath all the many faces, there is one. To walk the circuit of the Udayeśvara, reading the niches in turn, really is to be walked through a theology — and it remains only, in the chapter ahead, to ask what that theology, set down so carefully in the eleventh century and waiting all this while in the stone, understood itself to mean.
