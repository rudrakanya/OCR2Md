# Chapter 5 — The Temple's Visual World: Sculpture and Iconography

## I. A Walk That Is a Theology

In the last chapter we looked at the Udayeśvara as architecture — the rotated plan, the seven facets and seven storeys, the climbing rows of miniature spires. But to a worshipper arriving on the platform nine hundred years ago, the temple would have announced itself first of all as a crowd. Every surface that could carry a figure carries one. Gods stand in the faceted niches of the walls; goddesses dance in the arches of the tower; guardians, planets, river-deities, and celestial musicians press into the doorways and the eaves. The building is densely, almost vertiginously, populated, and the population is not random. It is arranged.

That is the single most important thing to grasp about the sculpture of the Udayeśvara, and it is what this chapter sets out to show. The carving is not decoration laid over a structure; it is a system laid into one. The deities are placed according to rule — by direction, by rank, by theological logic — so that the temple's outer skin becomes a kind of map of the divine order. To walk slowly around the building, reading the niches in sequence, is to be walked through a vision of the cosmos with Śiva at its centre. The deeper philosophy behind that vision — the metaphysics of Śaiva Siddhānta — belongs to the next chapter. Here our task is more immediate: to identify what is carved, to see where it stands and why, and to register the imagery's plain religious sense before we ask what it ultimately means.

We will begin with the ordering principle that governs the whole outer wall — the eight guardians of the directions. Then we will turn to the many forms of Śiva himself, the presiding lord whose theology the building exists to declare. After that we will meet the goddesses and the celestial women who soften and enrich the programme, and finally we will linger over the rarities — the images so unusual that they tell us something particular about this temple, this king, and this moment.

A word of method before we set out. Almost everything described here is recorded in the close iconographic study by Anupa Pande, who spent more than a decade reading the temple's imagery against the Sanskrit ritual and architectural manuals.[1] Where her account places a deity on a named facet, this chapter follows her; where the placement is only general, the text will say so rather than pretend to a precision the sources do not supply. The Udayeśvara has also suffered: many of its lower figures were deliberately defaced in later centuries, and some niches now hold only broken stone.[2] What survives, though, is more than enough to read the design.

## II. The Eight Guardians of the Quarters

The skeleton of the whole sculptural programme is a set of eight deities called the **Dikpālas** — literally the "guardians of the directions" (*dik*, direction; *pāla*, protector). In Indian cosmology the eight directions of the compass are not empty geometry; each is a province with a divine regent who rules it, and the standard set assigns one god to each. The cardinal and intercardinal points run, in order: **Indra** in the east, **Agni** in the south-east, **Yama** in the south, **Nirṛti** in the south-west, **Varuṇa** in the west, **Vāyu** in the north-west, **Kubera** in the north, and **Īśāna** in the north-east.[3]

The convention that makes them so useful to a temple designer is that each guardian is carved on the wall facing the quarter he governs. The result is a built compass. Stand at any corner of the Udayeśvara and you can, in principle, orient yourself by the gods: the deity in the niche tells you which way you are looking. This was not unique to Udaypur — the placement of the Dikpālas by direction is a widespread norm of medieval North Indian temple-building — but the Udayeśvara carries the scheme out with care, and several of its guardians are among the more vivid figures on the walls.[4]

Read them as a circuit. **Indra**, regent of the east and king of the gods, stands in a slightly flexed posture (*ābhaṅga*, the gentle bend of the body away from a straight vertical) holding his two great attributes: the thunderbolt, the *vajra*, the weapon with which he splits storm-clouds and enemies alike, and the *aṅkuśa*, the elephant-goad. Beside him stands his mount, the celestial elephant **Airāvata**, so that the identification is doubly secured — by weapon and by vehicle, the two things that fix a Hindu deity's identity more reliably than any face.[5]

At the south-east corner — the quarter of fire — comes **Agni**, the fire-god himself, the divine priest who carries the offering up to the gods in his flames. He too stands in the flexed *ābhaṅga* pose, but where Indra is a king, Agni is shown as something older and stranger: pot-bellied, with a stylised beard, holding a spear (*śakti*), a book, and a water-pot, and seated upon — or accompanied by — his vehicle the ram.[6] The book and the water-pot mark him as a sacrificial, priestly power; the ram is his ancient mount, the animal whose form the sacrificial fire was imagined to ride.

Turn to the south, the direction of death, and you meet its lord. **Yama**, the regent of the south and the judge of the dead, is given dark limbs and a fierce expression with bulging eyes, and he carries the two instruments of his office: the rod of justice, the *daṇḍa*, with which he chastises the wicked, and the noose with which he draws the soul out of the dying body. His mount, the buffalo, stands at his side.[7] Yama is a deity one is meant to find frightening, and the carver has obliged.

The south-western quarter belongs to a still grimmer figure, **Nirṛti**, the regent of decay and dissolution, the personification of misfortune and the dark direction. He is shown naked and emaciated, standing upon a corpse — a *naravāhana*, a "man-vehicle," the human body that serves him as a mount — and holding a sword and a severed head.[8] This is among the most unsettling images on the building, and we will return below to the wider taste for the macabre that it represents; for now it is enough to note how exactly the iconography fits the theology. The guardian of the quarter of ruin is himself a figure of ruin: wasted, naked, treading on death.

The west is the province of the waters. **Varuṇa**, the ancient lord of the cosmic ocean and of moral order, stands upon a *makara* — the composite aquatic creature, part crocodile, part fish, that is the emblem of the deep — and holds a noose, the *pāśa*, the cord with which, in the oldest hymns, he binds the liar and the wrongdoer.[9] To the north-west, the quarter of the winds, stands **Vāyu**, the wind-god, green of complexion, seated on a deer, holding a waving banner or flag whose flutter is the visible sign of the moving air he embodies.[10]

That accounts for six of the eight. Of the remaining two of the classical set — Kubera, lord of wealth in the north, and Īśāna, the auspicious aspect of Śiva himself in the north-east — the surviving record at Udaypur is less straightforward. Pande's account notes an **Īśāna** figure, three-eyed and seated on a bull, wearing the piled matted hair of Śiva (the *jaṭāmukuṭa*), but observes that it is placed "alternatively" — that is, not in the textbook north-east position but somewhere adapted to the temple's own scheme.[11] For the eighth guardian, Kubera of the north, the sources read here do not give a clear individual notice, and rather than invent one this chapter leaves the matter open. What the circuit shows, even with those uncertainties, is the principle at work. The walls are organised. Before a single figure of Śiva is read, the building has already declared that the world it depicts is a governed world, parcelled out among regents, each in his appointed place. The Dikpālas are the frame; the forms of Śiva are the picture set within it.

## III. The Many Forms of Śiva

A Śaiva temple exists to make Śiva present, and the genius of medieval Śaiva art is that it did not try to do so with a single image. Śiva is, in the theology, both the formless absolute and the god of a thousand stories, and the Udayeśvara gives us the second so that we may intuit the first. Across the building he appears in form after form — dancing, destroying, meditating, married, half-female, fused with other gods — and each form carries its own mood and its own meaning. To move among them is to feel the range of the deity the temple was built to house.

### The Cosmic Dancer

The form that the Udayeśvara returns to most insistently is Śiva as the dancer — **Naṭeśa**, the "Lord of Dance," caught in the *tāṇḍava*, the fierce cosmic dance whose rhythm sustains and dissolves the universe. This is not the serene dance one might imagine; the *tāṇḍava* is violent, world-ending energy, and the carvers render it with one knee flung high in a posture the manuals call the *ūrdhvajānu karaṇa* — the dance-pose of the "raised knee," the leg lifted dramatically across the body.[12]

The grandest of these dancing Śivas presides over the very front of the temple. Above the eastern entrance, set in the great projecting fronton of the tower — the *śukanāsa* — a multi-armed Naṭeśa performs the *tāṇḍava* in the raised-knee pose, his matted hair gathered into a tall conical crown (the *jaṭā-kalāpa*), his face balanced and composed even in the storm of the dance, surrounded by smaller dancing goddesses and by the dwarfish attendant spirits called *gaṇas*.[13] It is the first major image a worshipper coming up the original eastern stair would have seen, high overhead — the god dancing the world, set like a sign above the door of his house.

And the dancing Śiva recurs. At the base of each of the tower's three other vertical spines — north, south, and west — sits a sculpted medallion within a large arch (the *śūrasenaka*), and each of these is centred on Śiva dancing in the same raised-knee posture: a powerful figure on the west spine, another on the south set above a small pyramidal shrine, another on the north shown in full *tāṇḍava* above a seated Śiva who holds a trident and the skull-topped staff called the *khaṭvāṅga*.[14] The repetition is deliberate. The dance is not one scene among many; it is the temple's recurring refrain, the gesture by which Śiva is shown to be the active power that turns the cosmos.

### The Destroyer of the Three Cities

If the dance shows Śiva as cosmic force, another form shows him as cosmic warrior. On the south-western part of the hall wall stands **Tripurāntaka** — the "Ender of Tripura," Śiva in the act of destroying the *Tripura*, the three flying fortress-cities of the demons, which in the myth he burned to ash with a single arrow from a single bow.[15] The Udayeśvara's Tripurāntaka is given a muscular torso and a powerful warrior's stance, the body braced as if in the instant of drawing.[16] The myth is one of the great set-pieces of Śaiva storytelling, and its meaning is plain: Śiva is the destroyer of the strongholds of evil, the god who, against an enemy that seemed unassailable, needed only one shot. For the temple of a king fighting to hold his realm, the resonance hardly needs spelling out — though, as we shall see, another image on the walls makes that royal resonance even sharper.

### The Conqueror of Death

That image stands on the west, and it is the one most often singled out as the key to the whole programme. Beneath the dancing Naṭeśa on the western spine of the tower sits a six-armed figure of **Mṛtyuñjaya** — the "Conqueror of Death" (*mṛtyu*, death; *jaya*, victory), Śiva as the vanquisher of mortality itself.[17] He is shown not in motion but in stillness: seated in a meditative *yogamudrā*, the posture of yogic absorption, his body wreathed in serpents — snakes for earrings (*sarpa-kuṇḍala*), a snake for the sacred thread that crosses the chest (*sarpa-yajñopavīta*).[18] The serpent, which sheds its skin and seems to renew itself, is the natural emblem of a god who has mastered death.

Pande is explicit about why this figure is here. Mṛtyuñjaya was "specifically invoked to grant victory and overcome the danger of death on the battlefield."[19] Recall the king who built this temple. Udayāditya came to the Paramāra throne in a time of crisis and spent his reign fighting to consolidate a threatened realm; his own foundation inscription praises him as a sword-wielding second sun scattering his foes.[20] A six-armed Conqueror of Death, set on the western face of the royal temple, is not an abstract theological flourish. It is, in the most pointed sense, the personal deity of a warrior-king — Śiva asked to do for the patron exactly what the patron most needed, to keep death from the battlefield and grant victory. Of all the temple's images, this is the one that most clearly fuses theology with biography.

### Śiva Half-and-Half, and Śiva Married

Two further forms show Śiva in relationship rather than in action. On the south-eastern part of the hall stands **Ardhanārīśvara**, the "Lord who is half woman" — Śiva fused down the midline with his consort Pārvatī into a single body. The right, male half holds a trident and keeps the hard contours of the god; the left, female half holds a mirror and is given a rounded breast and a fuller curve of hip.[21] It is one of the subtlest of all Hindu images, a way of saying in stone that the divine is neither male nor female but the union of both, the god and his energy (*śakti*) inseparable. Elsewhere the same union is shown more gently as a couple: **Umā-Maheśvara**, Śiva ("the Great Lord," Maheśvara) seated at ease in the relaxed *lalitāsana* pose with his consort Umā on his lap, flanked by a lion and by Śiva's bull Nandi — the divine marriage shown as domestic tenderness rather than metaphysical fusion.[22]

### The Fierce Form and the Family

Śiva also has a terrible aspect, and the temple does not omit it. On the south-eastern hall wall stands **Mahābhairava** — "the great terror," Śiva in his most fearsome guise — multi-armed, in a flexed stance, holding a shield, a sword, a *ḍamaru* (the small hour-glass drum that beats the rhythm of the dance), and even a hill, wearing a garland of skulls and a headdress of snakes.[23] And around the lord the carvers set his household. On the facets of the sanctum itself appear his elephant-headed son **Gaṇeśa** with his own female counterpart **Gaṇeśānī**, four-armed and holding an axe and a bowl of sweets; the fierce attendant **Vīrabhadra**, Śiva's avenging emanation, shown on the rear facet in the act of strangling a figure who is most likely Dakṣa, the offending father-in-law of the great sacrifice-myth; the bull-headed devotee **Nandīśvara**; and the gaunt three-legged dancer **Bhṛṅgī**, the skeletal sage so devoted to Śiva alone that the myth left him barely able to stand.[24] Śiva's other son appears too, on the wall belonging to the upper directions: **Kārttikeya**, also called Skanda, the god of war, six-faced (*Ṣaḍānana*) and twelve-armed, riding his great peacock and bristling with spear, bow, arrow, and shield.[25]

Read together, these forms are a portrait of a single deity in his full range — dancer and destroyer, ascetic and bridegroom, terror and father. The temple does not ask the worshipper to choose among them. It surrounds him with all of them at once, so that the building itself becomes the argument that these are one god.

## IV. The Goddesses and the Celestial Women

If Śiva is the temple's subject, the feminine divine is its constant companion, and it appears in two distinct registers: the great goddesses, who are powers in their own right, and the *surasundarīs*, the celestial beauties who animate the surfaces with grace.

The major goddesses are placed, like the gods, with intent. On the north-eastern part of the hall — and the north-east is the auspicious quarter — stands **Mahiṣāsuramardinī**, the "Slayer of the Buffalo Demon": the goddess Durgā in her supreme heroic act, ten-armed, her body thrown into the most dynamic of all poses (*atibhaṅga*, the extreme bend), driving her trident into the chest of the demon Mahiṣāsura while her lion savages him.[26] This is the central image of the goddess's own great myth, the moment when the gods' combined energy, gathered into a single female form, accomplishes what none of them could do alone. Beside her, in deliberate contrast, comes **Cāmuṇḍā**, the goddess in her terrifying aspect: emaciated to a skeleton, her belly sunken, dancing upon a corpse, holding a dagger and a skull-cup and garlanded with snakes.[27] The pairing of the radiant warrior-goddess and the death's-head dancer, side by side on the same quarter of the building, is itself a theological statement — that the divine feminine, like Śiva, contains both the saving and the terrible.

The goddesses also appear as the consorts who complete the gods, in the divine couples carved on the sanctum's northern facet: **Umā-Maheśvara** again, **Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa** (Viṣṇu with his arm encircling Lakṣmī's waist), and **Brahmā-Brahmāṇī**.[28] And the goddess of learning, **Sarasvatī**, appears in more than one form — a seated Sarasvatī associated with knowledge, shown unusually without her customary lute (*vīṇā*) and defined instead by the attributes of text and scholarship.[29] We will come back to a more startling Sarasvatī below.

Then there are the *surasundarīs* — literally the "beautiful women of the gods," the celestial nymphs and dancers who are a near-universal presence on the walls of medieval North Indian temples. They are not, strictly, deities to be worshipped; they are the auspicious, life-affirming beauty of the divine world made visible, women shown dancing, adorning themselves, holding mirrors, swaying in graceful triple-bent poses among the major figures. At the Udayeśvara they crowd the arches and the side faces of the tower, and the dancing goddesses who attend the great Naṭeśa of the *śukanāsa*, mirroring his raised-knee dance, belong to this same celestial company.[30] It would be a mistake to read them as mere ornament. In the visual theology of the temple they are essential: they are the sensuous abundance of a cosmos pervaded by the god, the proof that the world Śiva dances is not bleak but fertile, beautiful, and alive.

## V. The Rare and the Striking

Every great temple has its surprises — images that depart from the expected repertoire and, in departing, tell us something specific. The Udayeśvara has a remarkable number of them, and three in particular deserve to be looked at closely.

### A Dancing Sarasvatī

In one of the arches flanking the great fronton over the eastern entrance is a representation of **Sarasvatī as a dancer** — and this is genuinely rare. Sarasvatī, the goddess of learning, speech, and music, is almost always shown seated and serene, the patroness of scholars; to find her *dancing*, in the same raised-knee posture as Naṭeśa, swan carved near her right leg, holding the hour-glass drum, wearing a necklace worked to imitate long lotus-buds, is unusual enough to demand explanation.[31]

The likely explanation is dynastic. The Paramāras were a house of learning, and the greatest of them, Bhoja — Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor — was a scholar-king who endowed a temple to Sarasvatī at his capital (the famous Bhojaśālā of Dhara). Pande reads the dancing Sarasvatī of Udaypur as an echo of that family devotion, the goddess of learning given pride of place near the temple's front as a sign of the Paramāra patronage of letters.[32] It is a small but telling instance of how a temple's iconography can carry a dynasty's self-image: even in a Śaiva building dedicated to a warrior-king's god, the house's pride in scholarship finds its way onto the wall.

### A Goddess Seated on a Corpse

On the southern side of the great fronton, among the tiers of seated goddesses, is one of the temple's most arresting images: a goddess — named in the sources as **Śreyā** or **Śrī** — seated upon a *preta*, a corpse or ghost, and holding an iguana (*godhā*) in one lower hand, a combination read as symbolising the destruction of enemies.[33] The corpse-seat is a charged motif. To sit enthroned upon a dead body is to declare absolute dominion over death and over the forces of dissolution — the same grim vocabulary we met in Nirṛti treading his corpse-mount and Cāmuṇḍā dancing on hers. Here, though, it is turned to a goddess of fortune and prosperity, fusing auspiciousness with the macabre in a way that is hard to parallel and that points, again, toward the theme of triumph over enemies that runs through this royal temple's whole programme.

### The God Who Is Four Gods

The single most ambitious image on the building is a **syncretic deity** in the central niche of the eastern fronton, below the dancing Naṭeśa. Pande names it **Hariharārkāpitāmaha** — a compound that unpacks into its components: *Hari* (Viṣṇu), *Hara* (Śiva), *Arka* (Sūrya, the sun), and *Pitāmaha* (Brahmā, the "grandfather" creator). It is, in other words, a single figure combining four of the great gods of the Hindu pantheon into one body.[34]

Syncretic images of this kind are the temple's most explicit theological statements, and the building offers a small graded series of them. The simplest is **Harihara**, the half-Viṣṇu, half-Śiva figure on the south-eastern hall wall, its two halves marked by two different crowns — the matted-hair *jaṭāmukuṭa* of Śiva on one side, the cylindrical *kirīṭamukuṭa* of Viṣṇu on the other — and holding Viṣṇu's conch (*śaṅkha*) and discus (*cakra*).[35] More complex is a three-faced, ten-armed goddess-panel fusing **Gāyatrī, Sāvitrī, and Sarasvatī**, a composite of the great Vedic and solar feminine powers holding lotus-bud, rosary, noose, and book.[36] And the most ambitious is the four-fold Hariharārkāpitāmaha, in which Śiva, Viṣṇu, the Sun, and Brahmā are declared, in a single carved body, to be aspects of one reality.

This is the point at which the sculpture begins to reach toward the philosophy of the chapter that follows. A composite of four gods is not a confession of indecision; it is a confession of faith — the assertion, central to the inclusive theism of the period and to Śaiva Siddhānta in particular, that the many gods are forms of a single supreme principle. That principle, for this temple, is Śiva: he is the dancer who presides above the composite, the lord whose house this is, the one into whom the others are gathered. The four-faced image makes the claim visible; the next chapter will follow it into thought.

## VI. Reading the Whole

Step back, finally, from the individual figures and consider the design entire. What the Udayeśvara offers is not a gallery but a structured argument. Its outer skin is ordered first by direction — the eight Dikpālas setting the world in its quarters, each regent in his place — and then filled, within that frame, with the forms of Śiva: the dancer who turns the cosmos, the warrior who burns the cities of evil, the conqueror who masters death, the bridegroom, the ascetic, the half-female lord, the father of gods. Around and among them move the goddesses, saving and terrible, and the celestial women whose beauty declares the world worth saving. And at the front, over the door, the building's boldest images gather the many gods into one and set Śiva, dancing, above them all.

It is, as the king's own praise-poem might have it, a sunrise rendered in stone — a whole divine order shown rising into visibility on the walls of a single temple. The placement is exact, the repetition deliberate, the rarities pointed. A goddess of learning dances for a dynasty of scholars; a six-armed Conqueror of Death sits on the west wall for a king who fought to live; four gods share one body to declare that, in the end, there is one. To walk the circuit of the Udayeśvara, reading the niches in turn, really is to be walked through a theology — and it remains only, in the chapter ahead, to ask what that theology, set down so carefully in the eleventh century, understood itself to mean.
