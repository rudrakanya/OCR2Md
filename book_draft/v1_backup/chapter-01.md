# Chapter 1 — The Rising Lord

## I. A Temple on the Betwa Country

Travel east from the railway halt at Bareth, across some six kilometres of the broad, dry-season plain of central Malwa, and the land will eventually give you a walled town. Its ramparts are not the neat brick-and-mortar of later centuries but giant blocks of stone, laid up dry — uncemented, fitted by weight and skill alone — so that the wall itself seems to have been quarried out of the plateau rather than built upon it.[1] This is Udayapur, today a modest municipal village in the Basoda tahsil of Vidisha district, Madhya Pradesh. Nine hundred years ago it was a new royal city, and at its heart its founder raised a building that scholars have not stopped praising since.

The building is a Śiva temple, and it announces itself from a distance. Its tower — the *śikhara*, the soaring superstructure that crowns a North Indian temple's sanctum — climbs in red sandstone above the rooftops, ribbed and faceted, dense with carving from base to summit. Come closer and the surface resolves into something almost vertiginous: hundreds of miniature spires, stacked in tidy diminishing rows like an orchard of stone, climbing each face of the great tower toward the heavy ribbed disc that caps it. On one shoulder of the spire, a small human figure is shown clambering upward — a flag-bearer, frozen mid-ascent, carrying the victory banner he means to plant at the very top.[2] The whole monument is a study in rising. That is fitting, for it carries its patron's name. The king was Udayāditya — *udaya* meaning "rising," as of the sun — and the god he installed here he called **Udayeśvara**, the Rising Lord.[3]

The setting matters as much as the stone. This is the Betwa country. The river the ancients called Vetravatī rises in the hills of Raisen district to the west and runs broadly south to north across the Vidisha plateau before it eventually surrenders its water to the Yamuna, far away in the plains of the north.[4] It is not a grand perennial flood for most of its length; in its upper reaches, the reaches that drain this region, the Betwa is largely intermittent, running hard in the rains and thin or dry between them.[5] The climate it waters is semi-arid central India, a land of fierce extremes — summer heat of forty degrees and more, cool nights in winter, and a monsoon that arrives in June and, in the space of four months, delivers some nine-tenths of the whole year's rain.[6] This is a country that has learned to store water against the long dry season, and Udayāditya was working squarely within that logic when, beside his new city and its new temple, he had a great reservoir excavated — the **Udayasamudra**, the "sea of Udaya," a tank to hold the monsoon and feed the town through the parched months.[7] City, temple, and lake were conceived together, a single royal foundation stamped with one man's name three times over.

The stone itself is local. The Malwa plateau here is built of Deccan trap and Vindhyan sandstone, and it is the sandstone — quarried red and pink and workable under the chisel — that gave the region its temples.[8] You can read the geology in the carving. Where Khajuraho's celebrated temples rise a few hundred kilometres to the north-east, the masons of Malwa worked their own red rock into something the early surveyors judged the equal of anything in India. The *Vidisha District Gazetteer*, surveying the monument with the cool prose of a government handbook, allows itself one unguarded verdict: the sculpture here, it says, is "finer than Khajuraho."[9]

## II. Why This Temple Matters

It is a large claim for a building that most travellers have never heard of, and it deserves unpacking. Why should an eleventh-century Śiva temple in a half-forgotten Malwa town hold our attention across a whole book?

The first answer is art-historical. To the archaeologists who catalogued the monuments of this region in the middle of the twentieth century, the Udayeśvara was simply the best thing they had. D. R. Patil, surveying the cultural heritage of the old Madhya Bharat state in 1952, returned to it again and again, and rendered his judgement without hedging: "In the long array of these sacred structures, the Udayeshwar temple stands supreme."[10] It was, in his account, the apogee of the Indo-Aryan — the Nāgara — temple tradition in central India, a building against which others were measured. When he wanted to praise the Siddheśvara temple at Nemawar in the Narmada valley, the highest compliment he could pay was to compare its tower to Udaypur's; when he reached the temples at Un, he called them work "matching that of Udaypur."[11] The Udayeśvara had become the yardstick.

The second answer is that the temple is a particularly pure and mature example of a specific architectural manner — the **Bhūmija** style — and one of the monuments that lets us understand that style at all. The word *bhūmija* means "born from the earth," and it is a name with several layers of meaning folded into it. It marks the style as native, a regional manner rooted in the soil of Malwa rather than imported. It hints at a "terrestrial" character, an earthly evolution set against styles imagined to have a divine origin. And, most concretely, it describes what the eye actually sees: those rows of miniature spires (*bhūmis* can mean "stories" or "levels") climbing the tower so that the whole thing appears to grow straight up out of the ground.[12] The Bhūmija manner was no provincial accident. One of the earliest texts to name it is the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, the great treatise on architecture compiled under the Paramāra king Bhoja — Udayāditya's illustrious predecessor — and by the time of our temple the style was so fully formed that it must have had a long earlier history. It was the Paramāras of Malwa who brought it to maturity, and the concentration of Bhūmija temples across Malwa, Rajasthan, and northern Maharashtra traces the reach of their cultural influence.[13]

The third answer is that the Udayeśvara is not merely beautiful but *legible*. A great temple is never only an art object; it is a religious building, a machine for making the invisible visible, and its design is a coded statement of belief. This one is unusually rich in such coding. It belongs to the world of **Śaiva Siddhānta**, the dominant theological school of medieval Śaivism — a systematic philosophy of Śiva as supreme reality — and its sculpture reads almost like that philosophy set in stone.[14] The walls carry a deliberate, ordered population of gods: the eight **Dikpālas**, the guardians of the directions, each set on the wall that faces the quarter of the world he rules — Indra to the east with his thunderbolt, Agni to the south-east on his ram, Yama the lord of death to the south, Varuṇa of the cosmic waters to the west on his crocodile mount.[15] Among and above them, Śiva appears in form after form: as the cosmic dancer **Naṭeśa**, caught in the violent *tāṇḍava* with one knee flung high; as **Tripurāntaka**, the warrior who destroyed three demon-cities with a single arrow; and — pointedly, for a king who fought hard to hold his throne — as **Mṛtyuñjaya**, the "Conqueror of Death," seated in meditation, wreathed in serpents, invoked to grant victory and to overcome the danger of death on the battlefield.[16] To walk around the temple is to be walked through a theology.

And the fourth answer is human and historical. The Udayeśvara has survived nine hundred turbulent years, and the marks of that survival are part of its meaning. It was begun, by the testimony of its own inscriptions, in 1059, and its victory flag was raised in 1080 — twenty-one years of labour by a royal workshop at the height of its powers.[17] It outlived the dynasty that built it. In the fourteenth century, when the armies of the Delhi Sultanate took Malwa, the rear of the complex was converted into a mosque, built partly from the stones of the temple's own dismantled outbuildings, and two Persian inscriptions still record the deed.[18] Sculptures were defaced; later legend laid further mutilation at the door of the emperor Aurangzeb.[19] Yet the main building came through, in the early surveyors' phrase, "without serious damage," and in the eighteenth century the Maratha Scindias of Gwalior restored Hindu worship, sheathing the ancient stone *liṅga* in a brass cover with a carved face — a gift recorded for a general of Mahadji Scindia in 1775.[20] The temple we walk into today is therefore a palimpsest: an eleventh-century Paramāra masterpiece, scarred by the fourteenth century, repaired in the eighteenth, and worshipped still.

## III. The Patron and His Moment

Behind the building stands a man and a dynasty. The **Paramāras** ruled Malwa from their capital at Dhar through the tenth, eleventh, and twelfth centuries, and they were among the great cultured houses of medieval India. The line ran through able and ambitious kings — Sīyaka II, Mūñja, and above all **Bhoja** (who reigned roughly in the first half of the eleventh century), the scholar-king whose name became a byword for learning, who endowed a temple of Sarasvatī at Dhara and under whose patronage that treatise on architecture, the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, was compiled.[21]

Udayāditya came after Bhoja, and he came to a kingdom in trouble. He ascended the throne around 1070 and inherited the hard work of consolidation, holding the Paramāra realm together against its enemies.[22] The temple's foundation inscription — the *Udayapur Praśasti* — remembers him in the heroic register of the age, casting the king as a second sun: "another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword, and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."[23] The sun-imagery is not idle court flattery. It is the same image that runs through the whole foundation — *udaya*, the rising sun, in the king's name, the city's name, the god's name, the reservoir's name. To build the Udayeśvara was to make a permanent claim: that under this king, after a hard season, Malwa had risen again into light and stability. During his reign, the sources tell us, the region knew peace and flourished as a leading centre of religion and culture, and Udayāditya marked that flourishing by founding a city and crowning it with this temple.[24]

This is one of the things that makes the Udayeśvara so rewarding to study: it is a monument whose patron, date, dynasty, faith, and even psychological motive are all within reach. Many great temples are anonymous. This one comes to us with a name, a praise-poem, a pair of dates, and a king who needed his god to be a conqueror of death.

## IV. How to Read the Building

Before we set out, it helps to have a rough map of the temple in the mind, because the chapters ahead will return to its parts by their proper names.

A North Indian temple of this kind is, at its core, a small dark room beneath a tall tower. The room is the **garbhagṛha** — literally the "womb-chamber," the sanctum, the holy of holies — and in it stands the *liṅga* of Śiva.[25] Over it rises the **śikhara**, the mountain-like tower. In front of the sanctum, here at Udaypur, comes a vestibule, the **antarāla**, and then a large pillared hall for worshippers, the **gūḍhamaṇḍapa** ("closed hall"), which throws out three projecting balcony-porches to east, north, and south.[26] The whole temple — *prāsāda* is the old word for such a lordly stone edifice — stands on a lofty platform, the **jagati**, which was once ringed by eight subsidiary shrines, several of them now broken.[27] On the same axis in front stands a separate low, flat-roofed hall, the **Vedī**, a stone pavilion enclosed by a balustrade and pierced with latticed windows, built, by old tradition and the report of the surveyor Alexander Cunningham, as a public hall for the recitation of the Vedas and the performance of sacrifices.[28]

The original way in was from the east, up a grand stairway flanked by over-life-size doorkeepers, the *pratīhāras* of Śiva; today, by the accidents of history, visitors enter from the west.[29] The plan beneath all this is itself a small marvel of geometry. Rather than dividing a square like a grid, the Bhūmija designer rotated a square around its own centre, pausing at fixed intervals; the spinning corners map out a circle, and that circle governs the line of the outer walls. The result is a plan that is at once star-shaped and circular — *saptaratha*, seven-faceted, on the ground, and *saptabhūmi*, seven-storeyed, in the air.[30] The faceted buttresses of the walls run unbroken up into the tower, where they become those climbing rows of miniature spires; the surveyors compared them to long strings of pearls hung from the summit.[31] It is a building conceived as a single upward gesture from earth to sky — which is, of course, exactly what the name promised.

## V. The Journey of This Book

This book is a single temple, examined slowly and from many sides. That is itself a deliberate choice, and one with a distinguished pedigree: the scholar whose decade-long study of the Udayeśvara underpins much of what follows, Anupa Pande, argued that a temple is precisely the kind of object where art, religion, and architecture intersect, and that its meaning lives in those intersections — so that to understand it you must be willing to read it as art object, as house of God, and as engineered building all at once.[32] That is the spirit of the chapters ahead, and they are arranged as a journey inward and downward, from the world the temple stood in to the vision it was built to express.

We begin, in the next chapter, with **history** — the long story of the Betwa country and the Paramāra dynasty, the rise of Udayāditya, and the moment of crisis and recovery into which the temple was born. From there we turn to the **patron** himself: the king of the praise-poem, the city-founder, the man who named a god after the rising sun and asked that god to conquer death.

Then we come to the stone. A chapter on **architecture** will take the Bhūmija style seriously on its own terms — the rotated plan, the seven facets and seven storeys, the soaring stacked spires, the mouldings of base and wall that the old craftsmen named with such loving precision. A chapter on **sculpture** will walk the outer walls deity by deity, reading the ordered crowd of guardians and goddesses and dancing Śivas, and pausing over the rare and the strange: a dancing Sarasvatī, a goddess seated on a corpse, a syncretic god who is Śiva and Viṣṇu and Sūrya and Brahmā at once.

With the imagery in hand, we can ask what it all *means*, and so a chapter on **philosophy** will set the temple within Śaiva Siddhānta, the metaphysics of Śiva that gives the whole programme its logic — the system the eleventh century itself tried to capture in texts like Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*. And finally we will turn to the **inscriptions** — the *Udayapur Praśasti* and the later Persian records of conquest and conversion — the written voices, Hindu and Muslim, royal and humble, that the building has gathered to itself across nine hundred years.

It is a great deal to ask of one temple. But the Udayeśvara, as we shall see, can carry it. It was built by a king who meant his stone to outlast him, to stand on the Malwa plateau as a permanent sunrise — and so it has.
