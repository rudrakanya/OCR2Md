# Chapter 7 — An Epigraphic Archive: Eight Centuries of Inscriptions

A great temple is not only a building. It is also a surface — vast acres of cut stone that, once a kingdom has decided the place matters, becomes a kind of public ledger. Kings record their gifts on it. Pilgrims scratch their names. Conquerors carve their victories. Restorers note their repairs. Across the centuries the stone keeps accumulating writing the way a riverbed accumulates silt, until the monument is no longer merely a thing seen but a thing *read* — a continuous text composed by hundreds of hands who never met one another, in languages that did not share an alphabet, professing faiths that warred over the very ground they stood on.

The Udayeśvara is such a surface. We have so far approached it as architecture and as theology — as the bhūmija masterpiece raised over the Malwa plateau in the late eleventh century, and as the seat of the Rising Lord whose name the temple carries. But the building has a second, quieter distinction that has only recently begun to receive its due. It is one of the most remarkable epigraphic archives in central India: a single monument bearing a continuous sequence of inscriptions in Sanskrit, Persian, and Hindi that runs across roughly eight centuries — from the temple's consecration in the year *saṃvat* 1137 (1080 CE) to its archaeological restoration in *saṃvat* 1985 (1928 CE).[1] Almost the entire span of north India's medieval and early-modern history can be read off these walls, if one knows where to look and which scripts to bring.

This chapter treats the inscriptions not one by one — Chapter 3 already gave the foundation eulogy, the *Udayapur Praśasti*, its proper hearing — but as a single accumulating corpus across time. We will watch the archive build itself: first the dense Sanskrit layer of the Paramāra and post-Paramāra centuries, then one extraordinary inscription that has gone almost unnoticed in the scholarly literature — the serpentine "scimitar of letters," a magico-political device unique to a pair of eleventh-century kings — and finally the Persian records that mark the moment when the conquerors arrived and a part of the temple became a mosque. The chapter closes, as the book closes, with the temple's long afterlife: defacement, repurposing, restoration, and a worship that has never quite ceased.

## The shape of the corpus

It is worth beginning with the simple, almost bureaucratic fact of *quantity*. The Udayeśvara is not a temple with an inscription or two; it is a temple swarming with them. The Gwalior State archaeology department, in a campaign of survey and copying running through the 1920s, transcribed and listed inscription after inscription from the complex, and the work was carried forward decades later by the epigraphy branch of the Archaeological Survey of India.[2] Only a fraction of this material has ever been formally edited and published — chiefly in the corpus volumes of H. V. Trivedi and in the studies of A. C. Mittal — which means that the temple's walls still hold a reserve of unread and half-read text, a working archive rather than a closed book.[3]

What survives, taken together, sketches a biography. The Paramāra inventory of inscriptions gathered from Udayapur alone shows the place returning again and again as a site of royal record across the dynasty's long arc. The founder Udayāditya is named on the great stone inscription dated *saṃvat* 1137 / 1080 CE — the consecration year — and on a second undated stone tied to the same moment.[4] His son and successor Naravarman left a record here dated *saṃvat* 1151 / 1094 CE, the year after his accession.[5] More than a century on, the temple was still attracting royal inscriptions: the Paramāra king Devapāla is recorded at Udayapur in *saṃvat* 1286 / 1229 CE and again a few years later, around 1232.[6] And remarkably late in the dynasty's twilight, Jayasiṃha II appears on a stone here dated *saṃvat* 1366 / 1309 CE — only a handful of years before the Paramāra line itself dissolved into the rising tide of the Delhi Sultanate.[7]

Set those dates in a row — 1080, 1094, 1229, 1232, 1309 — and a pattern emerges that is more than antiquarian. For well over two centuries, through the dynasty's high noon and into its decline, the Udayeśvara remained a *living* royal foundation: a place where successive kings chose to be seen, to record gifts and grants, to renew the bond between the throne and the Rising Lord. The temple was not a monument that the Paramāras built once and abandoned. It was an instrument they kept playing. When we read these inscriptions as a corpus rather than as isolated curiosities, this is the first thing the stone tells us — that the Udayeśvara was, for the whole life of the dynasty, the dynastic shrine par excellence, the Paramāra answer to the question of where a Śaiva king goes to make his kingship visible.

The languages and scripts deepen the picture. The earliest layer is Sanskrit, in the rectilinear Nāgarī of the late eleventh century — characters described by those who have studied them closely as well-formed and inch-high, cut in a rigidly upright style with verticals that finish in a sharp bend to the right, the local hand of Udayāditya's masons.[8] Over this medieval Sanskrit stratum lie the much later additions: Persian, in the Arabic script, recording the arrival of a wholly different polity; and Hindi, marking the temple's modern caretaking. Three languages, three scripts, three religious and political worlds, all incised on one building. That is the archive in miniature: not a harmony but a stratigraphy, each layer pressing down on the one before.

## A serpentine scimitar of letters

Among all this writing there is one inscription that deserves to be lifted out and held to the light, because it is unlike anything most readers will ever have imagined a temple could carry. It is not a praise-poem, not a grant, not a date. It is a *diagram* — a serpent, drawn out of letters.

The stone itself is unassuming: a rectangular slab of pinkish sandstone, about seventy centimetres high, built into the outer parapet wall — the *prākāra* — at the point where the temple's quadrangular courtyard projects southward.[9] Anyone who has studied the wall closely can tell that the slab is no longer where it began. Its flat, inscribed face is turned *inward*, against the masonry, while its decorative side — carved with an ornamental balustrade, a *vedikā* — faces out, exactly the reverse of every well-preserved stretch of the courtyard wall around it. The block plainly belonged to this parapet, but originally looked *into* the courtyard; at some point it was prised out, flipped, and reset the wrong way round — almost certainly during one of the twentieth-century restoration campaigns, when masons rebuilding the wall did not recognize what they were handling and set it back facing the wrong way.[10] The very accident of its survival is a small parable of the archive: a precious text saved by being mortared, blind, into a wall.

Turn the stone over in the mind's eye, to the inscribed face, and here is what you find. The centrepiece is a snake — but a snake whose entire body is built from the letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, each letter sitting in its own little compartment along the serpent's coiling length.[11] Read from the top down, the creature's geometrical "head" carries the fourteen vowels, marshalled in the classical order of short-and-long pairs — *a, ā; i, ī; u, ū; ṛ, ṝ; ḷ, ḹ* — rising to the complex vowels *e, ai* and the compounds *o, au*. Down the tubular "body" run the consonants: the four semivowels at the neck, then the five classes of stops gathered into a rhomboidal knot at the torso, then the three sibilants trailing down one leg. The whole series opens and closes with the voiced aspirate *ha*, exactly as the alphabet is arranged in the Śivasūtras that open Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, the foundational grammar of Sanskrit.[12] At the diagram's base sit the rarer breathed sounds; and at the very tail, looping up and down, are the grammatical *endings* — twenty-one terminations for nouns across all seven of their cases, and eighteen for verbs in the present tense, in both the active and the middle voice.[13] In other words: a complete machine of the language. Hold this one serpent in view and you hold the sounds of Sanskrit and the rules for inflecting them, all at once, coiled into a single image.

What is it *for*? The answer is given by two short lines of writing on the slab — a damaged but partly legible verse — and confirmed, crucially, by an identical diagram elsewhere. The verse on the Udayapur stone calls the diagram, in so many words, a *varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā*: a "serpentine scimitar of letters."[14] The compound is built to startle. *Varṇa* means both a *letter* of the alphabet and, in another register, the *order of society*; *nāga* is the serpent; *kṛpāṇikā* is a small curved sword, a scimitar. The grammar of the surviving verse runs as a command in the causative — roughly, *"may the protector cause this serpentine scimitar of letters to protect..."* — though precisely what is to be protected has flaked away with the stone.[15] The image is unmistakable all the same: the vowels form the snake's flaring hood, the consonants its coiled body, the grammatical endings its tail; and that same shape reads at once as a weapon — the hood as the curved blade, the knotted torso as the ornamented hilt, the winding tail as the sword-strap. A snake, an alphabet, and a sword, fused into one device. As the inscription's modern student puts it, the object presents "an unusual synthesis of aurality, visuality and efficacy" — sound, sight, and power braided together.[16]

For the meaning of that power we must travel west, to the great Mahākāla temple of Ujjain, the sacred heart of the Paramāra realm, where an identical diagram bearing the same name survives in a far better-preserved setting, embedded in a longer dedicatory inscription.[17] There the verses are explicit. They call the diagram a "serpent-scimitar of letters marked with Udayāditya's name" — *udayāditya-nāmāṅka-varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā* — and say it was composed, along with other "poetic gems," by a figure described as "the friend of talented poets," apparently King Naravarman himself.[18] The diagram, the Ujjain text declares, is an "ornament to be affixed on the chest of both poets and kings," and — most tellingly — a "unique magical sword," a *siddhāsiputrikā*, belonging to those two royal devotees of Śiva, Udayāditya and his son Naravarman, and intended *varṇasthityai*: "for the preservation of language and social order."[19]

That phrase is the key that unlocks the whole strange object. Scholars long assumed these letter-serpents were schoolroom aids — clever mnemonics for drilling Sanskrit into young boys.[20] The Ujjain verses show otherwise. The serpent-scimitar was a *device of kingship*. The pun in *varṇa* is doing real work: to "preserve the letters" is, in the same breath, to "preserve the social order," and the king who guards the alphabet thereby guards the proper arrangement of the world. Śiva, the Ujjain inscription elsewhere declares, is language itself personified; and the Paramāra kings, who held their crowns as Śiva's lieutenants, claimed the alphabet — the very substance of the god — as a magical weapon of their own.[21] To inscribe the serpent-scimitar on the wall of a royal Śiva temple was to make a single object out of grammar, magic, and sovereignty: a talisman that protected language, dynasty, and cosmic order in one coiled stroke.

This is why the device travelled. Versions of the serpentine scimitar attributed to Udayāditya are known not only from Ujjain but from Dhar, the Paramāra capital, and from the temple cluster at Un — and now, with this slab, from Udaypur itself.[22] (At Un the diagram is built into the Chaubārā Ḍerā temple, a stone's throw from another inscription that names the great king Bhoja, Udayāditya's celebrated predecessor.)[23] Together these copies form a small family of "serpentine scimitars of letters," all radiating from the same two kings in the last decades of the eleventh century. That Udaypur should possess one is exactly what we would expect. The town and its tank were founded and named for Udayāditya himself, around 1080; its temple was his temple. Of all the shrines in Malwa, this was precisely the place to plant the dynasty's grammatical sword — and so it was planted, where it stayed, flipped and forgotten, until a fieldworker turned the stone over in his imagination and read the snake.

## When the conquerors wrote

The Sanskrit archive is a record of a single civilization talking to itself across two centuries. The next layer is something harsher: the record of that civilization being overtaken.

By the early fourteenth century the Paramāras were gone, swallowed — as that very late Jayasiṃha II inscription of 1309 had all but foretold — by the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate across Malwa. And the conquest left its mark on the Udayeśvara in the most literal way imaginable. At the rear of the temple, on the western side of the platform, a mosque was raised — built, in the standard practice of the age, partly out of the temple's own dismantled stone.[24] Tradition and the local record date this work to the Tughluq period; the relevant order to dismantle and repurpose part of the complex is placed around 1325 CE, in the years of the early Delhi Sultanate, and the construction of the rear mosque to the late 1330s.[25] Sources differ on the exact attribution and date — the Madhya Bharat site directory assigns the Tughluq mosque to the years 1336–1338 CE (A.H. 737–739), under Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, while Pande's study frames the dismantling order more broadly within the early Sultanate's reach into Malwa.[26] What is not in dispute is the physical fact: a corner of a Śaiva temple converted into a Muslim house of prayer, its rear hall fitted with a *miḥrāb* — the niche that orients the faithful toward Mecca — cut into a building that had been raised for the Rising Lord.[27]

And here the archive changes language. Set into this converted portion of the complex are two Persian inscriptions, in the Arabic script, recording the new dispensation.[28] They are the third great stratum of the Udayeśvara's text: after the Sanskrit of the founders and the grammatical magic of their grammarians, the Persian of the conquerors. We must be candid about the limits of our sources here. The reference materials available for this chapter confirm the *existence* and the *placement* of the two Persian inscriptions — and confirm that they belong to the mosque-conversion of the rear of the temple — but they do not preserve a full translation of their contents. [GAP — the precise wording and full reading of the two Persian inscriptions are not given in the sources consulted; they are listed and located but not transcribed.] What we can say with confidence is structural rather than verbal: that at the very moment the temple stopped being purely a Paramāra monument and became contested ground, the writing on its walls switched scripts, and a new sovereignty signed its name in a new hand.

There is something almost unbearably eloquent in that switch of script alone. One does not need to read the Persian to grasp what it means. For two and a half centuries the only writing the Udayeśvara had carried was Sanskrit — the god's own language, in the kings' own hand. Then, abruptly, a second alphabet appears, running right to left, professing a different god and a different king. The serpent-scimitar had promised to *preserve the letters and the social order*; the Persian inscriptions are the documentary proof that the order it guarded had, in the end, given way. The two layers face each other across the same building like the two halves of a single, painful sentence.

## Afterlife: a permanent sunrise

What happened next is the part of the story that monuments rarely get to tell, because most do not survive to tell it. The Udayeśvara did.

The fourteenth-century mosque was not the last violence. A persistent local tradition holds that the temple's carvings were defaced again in the reign of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, late in the seventeenth century — the same emperor whose 1682 demolition of the Vijay Mandir at nearby Bhilsa is a matter of firmer record.[29] The tradition at Udaypur is exactly that — a tradition, carried in local memory and repeated in the descriptive literature rather than fixed by a dated inscription — and it should be reported as such; but the mutilation of the temple's images is plain enough on the stone, whatever the precise hand that did it.[30] For a long stretch, then, the Rising Lord rose over a wounded shrine: images battered, a corner given over to another faith, the great red-sandstone spire presiding over a complex that had passed out of the hands that built it.

Then the wheel turned again. In the eighteenth century the Marathas rose to power across Malwa, and with them came the Scindia (Sindhia) house of Gwalior — Hindu rulers who restored Hindu worship to the great shrines their predecessors' enemies had repurposed. At the Udayeśvara the restoration left a precise and touching memorial. In 1775 CE a general of Mahādjī Scindia named Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave the temple a new face for its god: a *liṅga* sheathed in a fitted cover of brass — a *mukhaliṅga*, the brass mask that gives the aniconic stone of Śiva a human face — installed in the sanctum where it remains to this day.[31] One should pause on the modesty and the meaning of that gift. After the dismantling order, after the mosque, after the defacement, the act that restored the temple to worship was not a grand rebuilding but a single, careful, gleaming face of brass, fitted by a soldier-donor over the ancient stone. The Rising Lord was given back his features. (Sources record the donor's name and rank in slightly varying forms — a general of Mahādjī Scindia, named in the Pande study as Khaṇḍerāo Appājī — and agree on the date of 1775 and the fact of the brass face; the minor variation in how the donor is styled is worth noting honestly.)[32]

The final stratum of the archive is the one that makes this whole chapter possible: the modern campaigns of survey, conservation, and repair. Under the Gwalior State, the archaeology department mounted a sustained effort through the 1920s — the very campaign, recorded in its annual reports for the years 1923–24 through 1928–29, that copied and listed the temple's inscriptions and reset its displaced stones, including, as we saw, the serpent-scimitar slab now mortared the wrong way round.[33] The work passed in time to the Archaeological Survey of India, whose own epigraphy branch and field surveys returned to the temple across the following decades, into the 1980s, to record and conserve.[34] These restorers, too, wrote on the building — in Hindi, the third living language of the archive — closing the eight-century sequence that had opened in *saṃvat* 1137 with a final layer dated to *saṃvat* 1985.[35] The men who saved the temple thereby became its last inscribers, their workmanlike notices the most recent silt in the riverbed.

And so the corpus is complete, or as complete as a living thing can be — for the Udayeśvara is not a ruin, not a museum, not a cleared archaeological site fenced against its own past. It is a temple still in worship. The brass-faced liṅga that Khaṇḍerāo Appājī gave it in 1775 is still attended; the doors still open; the Rising Lord still rises. Eight centuries of writing — Sanskrit praise and Sanskrit magic, Persian conquest, Hindi repair — run across the red sandstone like the rings of a great tree, each marking a year the monument lived through and survived.

It is fitting to end the whole book on the image its title has promised throughout. King Udayāditya took for his temple the name of the *Rising Lord* — Udayeśvara — and the foundation eulogy that opens this archive compared the king himself, with a poet's audacity, to "another sun, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his foes."[36] The metaphor was meant to last a reign. It has lasted, instead, the better part of a millennium. Conquerors came and signed their names in another script; emperors are said to have hacked at the god's face; dynasties fell and rose and fell. Through all of it the great bhūmija spire has stood on the Malwa plateau and caught the first light of every morning on its seven storeys of carved sandstone, a permanent sunrise fixed in stone above a temple that has never quite stopped being a temple. The kings who built it are dust, and the empires that fought over it are names in a gazetteer. The Rising Lord is still rising. That, in the end, is what the archive on his walls records: not the triumph of any one of the powers that wrote there, but the endurance of the stone they all wrote on.
