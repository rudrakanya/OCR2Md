# Chapter 3 — Raising the Udayeśvara: Patron, Date, and the Udayapur Praśasti

## I. A Foundation Stamped Three Times Over

Most cities grow. A few are decreed. Udayapur belongs to the second, rarer kind — a place that did not gather slowly at a crossroads or a ford, but was called into being by a single royal act and given, from the first, a single royal name.[1] Its founder was Udayāditya, the Paramāra king of Malwa, and what he founded on this stretch of the Vidisha plateau was not a temple alone but a complex of three things conceived together: a walled city, a great Śiva temple at its heart, and beside them a vast artificial lake to hold the monsoon and water the town through the long dry season.[2]

The three foundations share one name. The city is **Udayapura**, "the city of Udaya." The temple's god is **Udayeśvara**, the "Rising Lord." The reservoir is the **Udayasamudra**, the "sea of Udaya." And folded into all three is the king's own name — Udayāditya, in which *udaya* means "rising," as the sun rises, and *āditya* is the sun itself. To found this place was to sign it. A traveller arriving from the west would pass through ramparts of giant uncemented stone, drink from a lake that bore the king's name, and worship before a god who bore it too.[3] City, temple, and tank were a single gesture, stamped three times over with one man's identity.

We should pause on the lake, because the modern visitor, intent on the temple, can walk past it without grasping what it meant. This is semi-arid central India, a country of fierce monsoon rains followed by long parched months, where the upper Betwa runs hard in the wet season and thin or dry between. A king who built a city here had to feed it water, and the **Udayasamudra** — excavated beside the temple by Udayāditya's order — was the engine that made the foundation possible.[4] In the older Indian understanding of kingship, the excavation of a great tank was itself a sacred and meritorious act, a public benefaction set on the same moral plane as the building of a temple. Udayāditya did both at once, and named both for himself. The "sea of Udaya" was not an ornament beside the holy city; it was the condition of the holy city's life.

This chapter is about the making of that foundation — about the man who ordered it, the years of labour it consumed, the dates its own stones still carry, and the praise-poem that the eleventh century carved to explain him to posterity. The temple of the previous chapters has been a finished thing, a building to be read. Here we watch it being raised.

## II. The Patron: Udayāditya Paramāra

By the time Udayāditya came to the throne, the Paramāra house was old, illustrious, and in trouble. Its capital was Dhar; its greatest name was **Bhoja**, the scholar-king of the early eleventh century whose court became a byword for learning and under whose patronage the architectural treatise the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* — one of the earliest texts to name the very Bhūmija style in which the Udayeśvara would be built — was compiled.[5] But Bhoja's death was followed by hard years. The Paramāra kingdom, pressed by its neighbours, had nearly come apart, and it fell to Udayāditya to gather it together again.

He was, in the verdict of the sources, the king who restored Paramāra fortunes.[6] Anupa Pande, whose decade-long study of the temple stands behind much of this book, puts it plainly: Udayāditya "faced many challenges in consolidating his empire," and it was under him that Malwa recovered its footing and "witnessed political stability and flourished as a leading center of religious and cultural activities."[7] The recovery and the foundation belong together. A king does not found a new city and crown it with a masterpiece in a season of collapse; he does it when he has won enough peace to build. Udayapur is the monument of a recovery — a kingdom risen again, given permanent form in red sandstone.

Here, though, we meet the first of the difficulties that honest history cannot tidy away: the sources do not quite agree on when Udayāditya's reign began. Pande's study has him "ascend the throne in 1070 CE."[8] But D. R. Patil's survey of the cultural heritage of the old Madhya Bharat state, and the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* after it, place his reign earlier — Patil's chapters give dates of roughly 1058–1087, and the *Gazetteer*'s history simply names Udayāditya as the successor of Bhoja who "built the magnificent Nilkanthesvar temple at Udaypur."[9] The gap is real: an accession around 1059 against one around 1070, a difference of more than a decade. It is the kind of discrepancy that arises naturally from the nature of the evidence — regnal dates in this period are reconstructed from scattered inscriptions and genealogies, and reasonable scholars reach slightly different reconstructions. We need not resolve it to tell the story; we need only flag it. What matters is that the temple's *own* dates, as we shall see, are far firmer than its founder's accession, because the temple carved them into itself.

One further thing the sources let us glimpse is the king's temperament, or at least the public face of it. He was a Śaiva, a worshipper of Śiva, and the god he installed at the heart of his city was Śiva under a name of his own coining. But the temple's sculpture, read in the previous chapters, suggests a particular emphasis. Among the many forms of Śiva carved on the walls is **Mṛtyuñjaya**, the "Conqueror of Death," seated in meditation and wreathed in serpents, a form invoked — in Pande's reading of the iconography — to grant victory and to overcome the danger of death on the battlefield.[10] For a king who had spent his strength holding a contested throne, the choice is telling. The Udayeśvara is the temple of a fighter who wanted his god to be a conqueror, and who wanted, very specifically, not to die in battle.

## III. The Twenty-One-Year Workshop

We can date the building of the Udayeśvara with a precision that is rare for an eleventh-century Indian monument, and we can do it because the temple is, in part, its own archive. Its porches and walls carry inscriptions, and two dates emerge from them that bracket the whole enterprise.

Construction was begun, by the testimony of the temple's records, in **Vikrama Saṃvat 1116** — which is to say **A.D. 1059**. The Vikrama Saṃvat, or *saṃvat* for short, is the Indian calendar era most used in these inscriptions; it runs roughly fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era, so that subtracting about fifty-seven from a Vikrama year yields the Christian date. V.S. 1116 thus falls in A.D. 1059, the year the masons first broke ground.[11] And the work was crowned — its great victory-flag raised at last upon the summit of the tower — in **V.S. 1137**, A.D. **1080**.[12]

Between those two dates lies a span of twenty-one years: twenty-one years of a royal workshop labouring on a single building. That is the human fact behind the finished stone. The Udayeśvara was not thrown up in a hurry; it was the sustained project of more than two decades, long enough that a master who began the foundations as a young man might have seen the flag raised in his maturity, long enough for the carving that so impressed the early surveyors — sculpture the *Vidisha District Gazetteer* judged "finer than Khajuraho" — to be brought to its high finish.[13] When Chapter 1 noted the small human figure carved clambering up the north-west face of the spire, a flag-bearer frozen mid-ascent with the victory banner he means to plant at the very top, it was looking at the iconographic echo of this very event: the moment of completion, the *dhvaja* raised, the long labour done.[14]

It is worth dwelling for a moment on what such a workshop meant for the place around it. The later memory of Udayapur preserves a small, telling detail. Among the satellite structures of the town the *Gazetteer* records the **Pisanari-ka-Mandir** — the "grinding-woman's temple" — said to have been built by a poor woman from the earnings she made grinding flour for the temple's masons.[15] The story may be legend; but legends of this kind preserve a real social truth. A royal building project of twenty-one years was an economy as well as a monument. It drew labour, fed and paid it, and threw off enough surplus that even a flour-grinder at the bottom of the chain could, in the end, endow a small shrine of her own. The great temple sat at the centre of a web of lesser making.

The dating evidence deserves one note of caution and one of corroboration. The note of caution: the inscriptions are not all neatly dated, and the surviving epigraphic record at Udayapur is more tangled than a single pair of dates suggests. The corroboration is striking. The standard corpus of Paramāra inscriptions lists, for the reign of Udayāditya, two stone records at Udayapur itself — one assigned to A.D. 1080, and one explicitly dated **V.S. 1137 / A.D. 1080**.[16] The flag-raising date, in other words, is not a lone tradition; it is anchored in the catalogued epigraphy of the dynasty. The temple's own claim about when it was finished is confirmed from outside.

That same corpus lets us watch Udayapur go on speaking long after its founder was gone. The town that Udayāditya raised became a place where later Paramāra kings, too, left their inscriptions on stone: Udayāditya's successor **Naravarman** in V.S. 1151 / A.D. 1094, and a long sequence of later rulers — a Devapāla in the thirteenth century, a Jayasiṃha in the early fourteenth, V.S. 1366 / A.D. 1309 — each adding his own record to the town's accumulating stone.[17] A city founded as one king's signature became, across two and a half centuries, a dynastic place of record — proof, in stone, that the foundation took, that Udayapur lived on as something more than a single reign's vanity. The temple was a magnet for memory: kings came to it to be remembered, generation after generation, exactly as its founder must have hoped they would.

The fabric of the town bears out the same picture of a place that grew up around its great temple and kept growing. Besides the Pisanari-ka-Mandir, the *Gazetteer* records at Udayapur a cluster of further structures — a pillared hall of the eleventh century known as the **Barakhambi**, the "twelve-pillared"; the **Bijamandal** or *Ghadiyalan-ka-makan*, the "clockman's house," associated with the invocation of the Sun-god; a cavalry stepwell, the *Ghod-daud-ki-baodi*; and, from the later centuries of the town's life, a succession of mosques.[18] Not all of these are of the foundation moment, and several belong to the very different world of the Sultanate and Mughal centuries. But taken together they show what Udayāditya's act had set in motion: not a lone temple in an empty field, but a living town, walled and watered and worshipping, that went on building for centuries after the royal workshop had packed away its chisels.

## IV. The Udayapur Praśasti

If the dated inscriptions tell us *when* the temple was built, another inscription tells us, in the high rhetorical register of the age, *why* — or at least how the act of building was meant to be understood. This is the **Udayapur Praśasti**.

The word *praśasti* means "praise" — a praise-inscription, a panegyric, a poem of laudation cut in stone. The genre was a fixture of medieval Indian royal culture. When a king founded a temple or a city, or won a war, or wished to fix his glory for posterity, he commissioned a court poet to compose a praśasti: a Sanskrit poem, often of considerable length and learning, that set out his lineage, rehearsed his virtues and victories, and bound his name to the pious work he had performed. The praśasti is part genealogy, part eulogy, part dedication — and it is, for the modern historian, one of the most precious of all sources, because it is the voice in which a dynasty wished to be remembered, carved by the dynasty's own hand.[19]

The Udayapur Praśasti is exactly such a poem, set on the temple Udayāditya raised. Its governing image — and this is the thing to grasp about it — is the sun. We have already met the logic at work: the king's name is Udayāditya, the "rising sun"; his city, his god, and his lake all carry *udaya*, the rising, in their names. The praśasti takes that solar logic and makes it sing. It does not merely call the king a great warrior; it makes him a second sun, and it builds its central conceit out of the most ancient of Indian metaphors — light against darkness, the sun dispelling the night.

The single line that survives most vividly in the sources, and that Chapter 1 quoted in part, runs in full:

> "...another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword, and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."[20]

It repays slow reading, because every element of it is doing deliberate work. The king is "another sun" — a second *āditya*, which is of course his own name unfolded into a simile. His enemies are "dense darkness," the natural antithesis of the sun, and the conceit lets the poet praise the foes even as the king destroys them: they are "magnanimous," a touch of chivalry that magnifies the victor by dignifying the vanquished. The "rays issuing from his strong sword" fuse the two registers of the image at a stroke — the sword's flash *is* the sun's ray, martial and solar at once, so that the very act of war becomes an act of illumination. And the closing turn is the political point of the whole: by destroying the darkness, the king is "gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour." The sun that burns his enemies is the same sun that warms his subjects. This is a precise poetic statement of the ideal of kingship the temple was built to embody — the king as the source of light, whose violence against his foes is the very thing that gives his people peace and joy.

We should be careful about what such a poem can and cannot tell us. A praśasti is not a chronicle; it is an idealised, ornamental, frankly partisan portrait, composed to flatter and to last. Its description of Udayāditya as a world-conquering second sun is conventional hyperbole, the standard idiom in which any successful medieval king was praised, and we cannot read off from it a literal record of battles won.[21] But it is not therefore worthless. Read for what it *is* — a statement of royal self-presentation — the Udayapur Praśasti tells us a great deal. It tells us that Udayāditya wished to be remembered as a restorer, a bringer of light after darkness, which fits exactly the historical situation of a king who pulled the Paramāra kingdom back from the edge. It tells us that the solar imagery was no accident of nomenclature but a fully worked-out programme, a single idea — *udaya*, the rising — extended from the king's name through his city, his god, his lake, and into the poetry carved on his temple's walls. And it confirms, from the inside, what the architecture says from the outside: that this whole foundation was conceived as a sunrise made permanent, a kingdom's recovery frozen at the moment of dawn.

Here we must also be honest about the limits of our sources. The full text of the Udayapur Praśasti — its length, its complete genealogy of the Paramāra line, the particular victories and gifts it may name — is not preserved in the materials underlying this book; what survives to us here is the single, justly famous solar line and the framework of solar imagery around it. ## V. The Meaning of the Making

Step back from the dates and the poem, and the shape of the whole enterprise comes clear. Udayāditya did something with unusual coherence. He took a single idea — rising, the sunrise, his own name — and he built it out at every scale at once: into the name of a city, into the name of a god, into the name of a lake, into a praise-poem that made him a second sun, and into the very architecture of a temple whose Bhūmija tower was designed to look as though it grew straight up out of the earth toward the sky. The Udayeśvara is sometimes described, in this book and elsewhere, as a building conceived as a single upward gesture. The making of it, traced here, shows that the gesture went far beyond the stone. The temple is the largest and most lasting expression of a programme that ran through an entire royal foundation.

And the programme had a political edge that the praśasti makes explicit. This was the work of a king who had inherited a kingdom in trouble and brought it back; the imagery of darkness dispelled and light restored was not idle court flattery but a claim about what his reign had achieved. To found Udayapur — city, temple, and "sea of Udaya" together — and to crown it with twenty-one years of a royal workshop's labour, was to make that claim permanent. The dated stones tell us the labour ran from 1059 to 1080; the praśasti tells us what those two decades were *for*. They were for fixing, in the most durable medium available to an eleventh-century king, the memory of a kingdom that had risen again into the light.

It worked. The dynasty that built the temple did not outlast the medieval centuries; the city itself dwindled, in the end, to the modest walled village a traveller finds today. But the foundation held its meaning. Later Paramāra kings came back to Udayapur to carve their own records; later restorers, Maratha and modern, kept the Rising Lord in worship. Nine hundred years on, the sun the poet promised has not set. The temple still stands on the Malwa plateau exactly as its founder meant it to stand — a permanent sunrise, in red stone, carrying his name.
