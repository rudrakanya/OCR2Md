# Chapter 4 — The Bhūmija Vision: Plan and Architecture

## I. A Style Born from the Earth

Every great building belongs to a family. Before we can see what is singular in the Udayeśvara, we have to know the kin it springs from, and the name of that kin is a strange and beautiful one: **Bhūmija**. The word repays unpacking, because it is not merely a label that scholars later pinned to a group of temples — it is a statement, made by the medieval architects themselves, about what these buildings *were*.[1]

*Bhūmija* means, literally, "born from the earth," or "earth-produced." Read one way, the name is geographical: it marks a regional manner, the native style of Malwa, as against the temple modes of Gujarat, of Orissa, of the Deccan. Read a second way, it is almost a theological claim. The standard medieval taxonomy of temple forms held that the loftiest building-types had a divine or celestial origin — that the gods themselves had first dwelt in such structures, and that men merely copied the heavenly pattern. Against that company of sky-born forms, the Bhūmija stood as the terrestrially evolved type, the order that had grown up out of the ground of a particular country rather than descending ready-made from heaven.[2]

But there is a third reading, and it is the one that matters most for the eye. *Bhūmi* means a storey, a level, a tier — and the Bhūmija tower is, before anything else, a thing of stacked storeys. Its spire is composed of miniature spires, ranged in rows one above another, climbing in diminishing tiers from the ground to the summit. The whole *śikhara* — the tower — looks as though it is rising directly out of the earth, level upon level, a single growth pushing upward. *Bhūmija*: born from the earth, and built of storeys that climb from it. The name and the form say the same thing.[3] This is the deepest sense in which the temple at Udayapur is the temple of the Rising Lord. Its very style is a style of rising.

The Bhūmija order seems to have crystallised in Malwa, and the Paramāra kings brought it to its full maturity. By the second half of the eleventh century, when the Udayeśvara was rising, the style was so confidently established, so completely worked out, that it must have had a long development behind it — styles do not arrive fully formed. The textual evidence and the buildings agree on this. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, the architectural treatise compiled under the Paramāra king Bhoja, is among the earliest texts to speak of the Bhūmija mode; and the temples themselves cluster thickly in exactly the lands the Paramāras and their neighbours ruled — Malwa, Rajasthan, the northern Deccan of Maharashtra.[4] When the great art historian Stella Kramrisch came, in 1946, to define and classify this regional style, she reached for one building above all others to stand as its type-specimen, its classic example against which all the rest could be measured. That building was the temple at Udayapur.[5] What follows in this chapter is, in effect, a long look at why she chose it.

## II. The Square That Turns: A Plan Made of Rotation

To understand the Udayeśvara, begin not with the tower but with the ground — with the shape the walls trace on the earth. For it is here, in the plan, that the building announces its singular logic, and it is a logic unlike that of almost any temple a visitor will have seen before.

The standard way to design a North Indian temple plan — the manner art historians call **Nāgara** — begins with a square. The square is the sacred figure, the perfect and stable form; the inner sanctum is square, and the outer walls are generated from it by a series of projections and recesses, like a grid worked outward. The architect adds offsets to the faces of the square: a central projection on each side (the *bhadra*), then subsidiary projections flanking it, then the corner. Count the resulting vertical facets and you get the temple's "ratha" number — *tri-ratha* (three-faceted), *pañca-ratha* (five-faceted), *sapta-ratha* (seven-faceted). It is, in essence, a square enriched by stepping its sides in and out. The eye still reads a square underneath.[6]

The Bhūmija plan does something else entirely, and the difference is not of degree but of kind. Instead of subdividing a square like a grid, the Bhūmija architect *rotates* it. He takes a square and turns it about its own centre — the operation the texts call *parivartanā*, "turning around" — stopping the rotation at regular intervals and recording, at each stop, the position of the turning square's corners. As the square pivots, its four corners sweep out a circle. The points it leaves behind at each pause map the line that the exterior walls will follow. The result is a plan that is neither a simple square nor a simple circle but a marriage of the two: a figure that is, in the language of the sources, **stellate-cum-circular** — star-shaped where the corners thrust out, gathered onto a circle that governs the whole.[7]

Take a moment to feel what this means, because it is the secret engine of the entire design. A grid-built plan is fundamentally static: a square is a square, planted four-square on the earth, at rest. A plan generated by rotation is fundamentally *dynamic*: it carries, frozen into its geometry, the memory of a turning motion. The walls of the Udayeśvara do not simply stand; they seem to wheel, to have spun outward from the central point of the sanctum like rays thrown off a turning hub. The plan is a movement caught in stone. And it is the same movement, repeated upward, that will become the climbing storeys of the tower. Earth-plan and sky-tower are generated by one idea.

In the specific figures of the Udayeśvara, the rotation is stopped so as to yield a sanctum that is *sapta-ratha* — seven-faceted — on plan, and the elevation answers it with seven tiers, *saptabhūmi*, seven storeys, in the rise of the tower.[8] Seven on the ground, seven in the air: the building rhymes with itself from foundation to finial. This is why Chapter 1 could give, as the headline fact about the temple's shape, that its plan is "rotated" and *saptaratha*. Here we can see what lies behind that headline — that the rotation is not a decorative flourish but the generative act of the whole composition, the single geometric decision from which everything else unfolds.

One consequence of the design deserves to be stated plainly, because it governs how a worshipper meets the building. Temples of the Bhūmija class are, almost without exception, **nirandhara** — "without a passage." There is no enclosed ambulatory corridor running around the sanctum within the walls, no dim circuit for the devotee to pace around the god in the dark of the interior. The wall of the sanctum is, in effect, a single thickness; the faceted exterior we have been describing *is* the outer face of the sanctum wall itself.[9] This has a profound effect on the elevation, as we shall see: with no ambulatory to wrap, the wall can be thinner, the buttresses sharper, the whole exterior more vertical and more knife-edged. The plan's economy becomes the tower's energy.

## III. From the Ground Up: The Base and the Wall

If the plan is the building's secret, the elevation is its public argument — the upward sentence it makes to anyone who stands before it. The Udayeśvara makes that sentence in clearly marked stages, each with its own register of mouldings, and the discipline of reading them is the discipline of watching the building climb. We move from the bottom up, because that is the direction the whole design insists upon.

At the foot of the temple is the **pīṭha**, the plinth or base — the platform of mouldings on which the wall proper stands. At Udayapur it is built of five distinct horizontal courses, and they ascend in a calculated crescendo of ornament from plain to rich. The lowest course (*bhiṭṭa*) is severe and unadorned, the bedrock note. Above it a second *bhiṭṭa* breaks into carved leaf and garland work, capped by a "shoulder" course of lotus petals. Then comes the **jāḍyakumbha**, a bold moulding cut in the profile of an inverted cyma — a lotus-shaped swell, detailed with rosettes along its flanks and a fringe of little caitya-window motifs across its top. Above that a sharp knife-edged moulding, the **karṇaka**, carries an arrow-and-spiral band; and crowning the whole base runs the **grāsapaṭṭi**, a frieze of grinning gorgon-faces — *grāsa* or *kīrtimukha*, the "face of glory," the swallowing mask that Indian builders set at thresholds to devour evil.[10] Already, before the wall has even begun, the building has taught the eye its method: a vertical sequence of bands, each different, each more elaborate than the last, marching the gaze upward.

Above the plinth stands the **maṇḍovara** — the wall, the body of the temple between base and roofline. This is the zone where the building shows its gods to the world, and it too is read as a stack of horizontal members rising into a field of vertical ones. Its lower part is the **vedibandha**, the "altar-binding," a girdle of three principal mouldings: the *khura* (a hoof-shaped member, banded here with a row of cut diamonds), the **kumbha** (a swelling pot-shaped moulding, ornamented with tiny shrine-niches that house small deities, with its own diamond band and lotus-petal shoulder), and the *kalaśa* (a rounded torus, a "pitcher" moulding, hemmed between fillets and carrying the same arrow-and-spiral band we met below).[11] The names tell their own story — *khura*, hoof; *kumbha*, pot; *kalaśa*, pitcher — for the Indian moulding vocabulary is drawn from the ordinary furniture of ritual life, the vessels and altars of worship, stacked up into the structure of the divine house.

Then, above this girdle, the wall changes character utterly. The lower mouldings were horizontal, encircling bands; now the surface breaks into **the jaṅghā**, the upper wall, and here the design turns vertical with a vengeance. The faceted plan we traced on the ground now stands up into space as a row of tall, pillar-like buttresses — the projecting facets of the rotated square, risen into the wall as slender vertical shafts. Each is treated as a faceted pilaster, and each frames a deep, beautifully carved niche, a **khaṭṭaka**, in which a major god or goddess stands.[12] Between the buttresses fall deep shadowed recesses, so that the wall is no longer a flat skin but a corrugated, rhythmic alternation of jutting light and sunken dark — projection, recess, projection, recess, around the whole turning circuit of the building. (The deities who inhabit these niches — the guardians of the directions, the dancing forms of Śiva, the great goddesses — are the subject of the next chapter; here we are concerned only with the architecture that holds them.) At their tops the buttresses are crowned with capital-like mouldings, an ornamental fillet, a recess, and a projecting eave-moulding (*chādya*) that throws its line of shadow across the wall.[13]

Now comes the single most important fact about the elevation of the Udayeśvara, the fact that turns a fine wall into a Bhūmija masterpiece. **The vertical buttresses do not stop at the roofline.** The pillar-like shafts of the *jaṅghā* run straight up off the top of the wall and into the tower above, where they transform — without a break, as one continuous gesture — into the climbing rows of miniature spires that compose the *śikhara*. One member of the elevation, beginning at the foot of the wall, rises through the whole height of the building to the summit. Anupa Pande's account fixes the image precisely: the buttress-lines, continued upward, become rows of little spires that hang "one below the other," resembling "long pearl strings suspended from the spire."[14] Hold that picture, because it is the whole building in a phrase. A string of pearls is exactly what the eye sees on the great tower: vertical chains of small bright forms, threaded from sky to earth, each pearl a tiny spire, the whole chain hanging like a necklace down the curving flank of the *śikhara*. The wall and the tower are not two things joined; they are one continuous upward growth, and the pearl-strings are the thread that ties them.

## IV. The Climbing Tower: The Śikhara and Its Strings of Pearls

So we arrive at the tower itself — the **śikhara**, the great curvilinear spire that is the glory of the building and the signature of its style. To understand it we have to set it, for a moment, against its more famous cousins, because the Bhūmija *śikhara* is most easily seen by contrast.

The classic Nāgara tower of central India — the kind that crowns the celebrated temples of Khajuraho, the Chandella masterpieces a few days' travel to the north-east — works by *accumulation* and *clustering*. The Khajuraho spire of the developed type is a **śekharī** tower: a tall central spire wreathed about with a rising mass of half-spires and quarter-spires (*uraḥśṛṅgas* and *śṛṅgas*) clustered against its flanks, so that the whole mountainous form seems to swarm upward in a profusion of smaller peaks, a single summit attended by a teeming retinue of lesser ones. The effect is of organic abundance — a mountain breeding mountains, ridge crowding ridge, the eye almost overwhelmed by multiplicity.[15]

The Bhūmija *śikhara* achieves its height by a wholly different and, in its way, more austere logic. It is governed not by clustering but by *the grid* — by the disciplined, rational ordering of its parts into rows and columns. Down the centre of each of the four cardinal faces runs a **latā** — a flat vertical spine or band, a broad raised stripe climbing from the base of the tower to its shoulder. The four *latā*s, set to the four cardinal directions, divide the tower into four quadrants, and they are filled with a dense, exquisite lace-work of **gavākṣa** motifs — interlaced "cow's-eye" or caitya-arch ornaments, the honeycomb of little dormer-windows that is the most characteristic surface-texture of the Indian tower.[16]

It is the *quadrants between the latās* that give the Bhūmija tower its character and its name. Each quadrant is filled, not with clustered half-spires swarming at random, but with neat ranks of complete **miniature spires** — *kūṭastambhas*, "spire-pillars," each a tiny perfect temple-tower in its own right — arranged in orderly rows and columns, diminishing in size as they climb toward the summit. At the Udayeśvara each quadrant carries these miniature spires in a regular array: rows climbing vertically and ranks running horizontally across the curve of the face, a tidy multiplication of the same small form, growing smaller as it rises.[17] (The sources transmit the count of these ranks in slightly varying figures — Krishna Deva's description gives the Udayeśvara seven vertical rows and five horizontal ranks of miniature spires to each quadrant, while Pande's general account of the style gives ranges of three to five vertical and five to nine horizontal — the variation reflecting the difference between a count of this one temple and a description of the class as a whole.)[18]

Here, then, is the visual heart of the matter, and the deepest meaning of the word *Bhūmija*. Where the Khajuraho tower *clusters*, the Udayeśvara tower *stacks*. Its spire is built of storeys — *bhūmis* — of miniature spires, ranged level upon level in legible rows, and because the columns of those miniature spires line up vertically all the way down and continue, as we have seen, into the buttress-shafts of the wall below, the whole tower reads as a set of vertical chains running unbroken from summit to ground. These are the "strings of pearls": each vertical column of stacked miniature spires is a string, each spire a pearl, hanging the length of the tower's curving flank.[19] The Khajuraho spire makes you feel the swelling, organic mass of a holy mountain. The Bhūmija spire makes you feel a clean, rational, irresistible *ascent* — order rising, storey by counted storey, from the earth into the air. It is architecture as a single upward gesture, and the Udayeśvara is its supreme statement.

The summit completes the figure. The tower draws in at the **skandha**, the "shoulder," where the climbing miniature spires reach their smallest size, and the curve gathers toward the top. The star-shaped logic of the plan, carried all the way up, persists even here: each sub-quarter of the tower is finished with a five-pointed serrated edge, a little zig-zag stellate crenellation, and the same serrated rhythm is repeated in the corrugated rim of the heavy crowning member.[20] That crowning member is the **āmalaka** — the great fluted stone disc, ribbed like the *āmalaka* fruit (the myrobalan) from which it takes its name, that caps every North Indian tower and over which, finally, the pot-finial and the victory-flag are raised. And it is on the upper tower, on the north-west face just below the summit, that the medieval sculptors carved their most human touch: the small figure of a man climbing the spire, the flag-bearer frozen forever in the act of mounting to plant the *dhvaja*, the banner of completion, at the very top — the moment, met in earlier chapters, when twenty-one years of labour came to their end.[21]

On the eastern, front-facing side the tower does one thing more. There the central *latā* does not simply climb the face but thrusts forward, away from the body of the tower, into a great projecting frontal mass called the **śukanāsa** — the "parrot's-beak," an antefix or fronton that juts out over the vestibule like the prow of the spire, masking and announcing the front of the building. The Bhūmija *śukanāsa* is invariably prominent, and at Udayapur it is a vast, balanced composition of sculpture in its own right (its imagery, again, belongs to the next chapter). Architecturally, its role is to give the otherwise symmetrical, radial tower a *front* — to declare which way the building faces, which way the god looks, which way the worshipper must come.[22] The *śukanāsa* faces east, toward the rising sun, toward the original grand stairway and its guardian door-keepers; and so even the tower, for all the wheeling symmetry of its plan, turns its face to the dawn.

## V. The Hall, the Roof, and the Reach for the Sky

A great Indian temple is not a single tower but a sequence of spaces strung along an axis, each roofed in its own way, the roofs themselves forming a deliberate progression from low to high. The Udayeśvara's axis runs, from the sacred core outward: the **garbhagṛha**, the sanctum or "womb-chamber," square within, where the *liṅga* of the Rising Lord stands in its dark; the **antarāla**, the short vestibule that links sanctum to hall; the **gūḍhamaṇḍapa**, the great closed hall where worshippers gather, opened to the air on three sides by projecting balconied porches (**mukhamaṇḍapas**) to the east, north, and south; and, set apart on the same axis before the temple, the low flat-roofed **Vedī**, the open ritual hall. All of this rests on the **jagati**, the lofty platform-terrace, once ringed by a girdle of eight subsidiary shrines — the **aṣṭāyatana** complex — several of them now broken.[23] These were named in Chapter 1 as the building's parts; what concerns us here is how their *roofs* contribute to the single upward argument the architecture is making.

For the design is governed throughout by a rising silhouette. The closed hall, the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa*, does not carry a flat or a simple roof but a **saṃvaraṇā** — a "bell-roof," a pyramidal roof built up of stepped, receding tiers of mouldings, ring above ring, climbing to a small finial.[24] Set this beside the soaring *śikhara* over the sanctum, and the temple presents, in profile, a deliberate ascent: the low platform, the lower stepped pyramid of the hall, and behind it the great curvilinear tower springing far higher — a procession of summits, each taller than the last, leading the eye from the gathered space of the worshippers toward the secret core where the god sits beneath the climbing spire. The bell-roof is the foothill; the *śikhara* is the peak. To approach the temple along its axis is to walk, in stone, a graded ascent toward the sky.

Inside, the design turns its energy from height to depth. The great hall is carried on four massive central pillars, and these are themselves small lessons in the geometry the whole building loves: they shift, as they rise, from square bases through polygonal mid-sections to circular ring-capitals — the very progression from *caturaśra* to *vṛtta*, squared to circular, that governs the plan outside.[25] Above them the hall is closed by a spectacular domed ceiling of deep concentric rings carved with *kīrtimukhas* and lotus-work, descending at the centre to a great hanging pendant, the **padmaśīla**, a stone lotus-bud suspended from the apex.[26] The eye drawn up into this dome finds the same circle the plan traced on the ground, now turned overhead and gathered to a single point. Outside, rotation throws the walls into a wheeling star; inside, it draws the ceiling into a deepening spiral. The geometry that climbs the tower descends, inverted, from the dome.

## VI. The Theory of the Temple: Prāsādas in the Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra

We have been describing what the medieval builders made. It remains to ask what they *thought* — how they themselves understood the temple as a category of object — and for that we can turn to a text that the Paramāras themselves produced, a treatise attributed to the very king whose dynasty perfected the Bhūmija style. This is the **Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra**, the great compendium of architecture (*vāstuśāstra*) ascribed to the Paramāra monarch Bhoja — Udayāditya's illustrious predecessor — and it offers us, in its chapters on the **prāsāda**, the temple as the eleventh century's own theorists conceived it.[27]

The word *prāsāda* means a lofty building — a palace, a mansion, and, supremely, a temple, the house of a god. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* opens its treatment of the subject not with a builder's manual but with a myth of origins, and the myth is worth pausing over, because it reveals at a stroke how these architects ranked their forms. In the beginning, the text says, the creator-god Brahmā fashioned five **vimānas** — five celestial cars or aerial palaces — and gave them as the dwellings of the highest gods: Vairāja, Kailāśa, Puṣpaka, Manika, and Triviṣṭapa, made of gold and inlaid with gems, sailing through the skies.[28] From these primal five, the text says, all the manifold types of temple were derived; Brahmā created "many others," shaped severally for every god, and the *prāsādas* of the towns — built now in stone and baked brick rather than gold — are the earthly images of those heavenly originals.[29] Here, embedded in the myth, is precisely the hierarchy against which the *Bhūmija* defined itself: the grandest forms are sky-born, divine in origin, descended from Brahmā's celestial cars. The earth-born style stands deliberately apart from that lineage.

And there is a further detail in the myth that speaks directly to the Udayeśvara. The text glosses these primal heavenly cars as **saptabhūmi prāsādas** — "seven-storeyed palaces."[30] The seven storeys, the *saptabhūmi* elevation that the Udayeśvara's tower realises in its seven climbing tiers, is thus not an arbitrary number but a figure with deep resonance in the architectural imagination: the heavenly mansion is a seven-storeyed thing, and a temple built *saptabhūmi* is an earthly echo of that celestial seven-fold rise. The "born-from-the-earth" style and the "seven-storeyed" heavenly model meet, in the Udayeśvara, in a single building: terrestrial in its style, celestial in its sevenfold ascent.

The bulk of the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*'s treatment, however, is taken up not with myth but with a vast and meticulous classification. The text enumerates **sixty-four prāsādas**, sorted with patient geometric rigour according to the shape of their ground-plan: twenty-four are *caturaśra*, square in plan; ten are *vṛtta*, circular; ten *caturaśrāyata*, square-oblong or rectangular; ten *vṛttāyata*, round-and-oblong; and ten *aṣṭāśra*, octagonal.[31] Each named type — Rucaka, Citrakūṭa, Siṃhapañjara, Bhadra, Nandyāvarta, Svastika, Meru, Mandara, and on through the long roll of sixty-four — is defined by the most exacting prescriptions: how many parts to divide the field into, where to set the central shrine, how many pillars to the hall, how many porches and on which sides, how high the door, how the boundary-walls and the lattices and the eaves are to be disposed.[32] It is a system of breathtaking comprehensiveness, an attempt to map the entire possible universe of temple-forms onto an ordered grid of names and measures.

Two things in this classification illuminate the building at Udayapur. The first is its governing principle: the prāsādas are sorted, above all, by the *shape of the plan* — square, circular, oblong, octagonal. The whole theory rests on the conviction that a temple's identity is determined at the ground, by the figure its walls trace on the earth, exactly the conviction that makes the Udayeśvara's rotated, stellate-cum-circular plan the generative fact about it. The medieval theorist and the Bhūmija builder agree that everything begins with the shape on the ground. The second is the text's insistence that the form of the temple is *charged with consequence*. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* does not treat its sixty-four types as neutral options. A whole chapter is devoted to the auspicious and the inauspicious building — and its language is startlingly grave. A temple well-proportioned, with even flanks and matched pillars, "neither very lofty nor very small," solidly founded and rightly decorated, is "the bestower of beneficence for men," the augmenter of honour, and "the builder and the one arranging the building act... may attain to utmost propriety." But a temple ill-made — uneven, deficient in its parts, its members badly joined — is a "foreboder of fright, bondage and chagrin," and may even prove "the source of death for the owner."[33] One striking line warns that a temple built *too high* brings danger to the king, while one built too low sees his army "churned up," annihilated.[34]

This is the mental world in which the Udayeśvara was raised. To build a temple, for an eleventh-century Paramāra king, was not merely to commission a beautiful object; it was to perform an act of cosmic and political consequence, in which the exactness of the proportions, the rightness of the measures, the matching of the parts, bore directly on the welfare of the realm and the life of the king himself. We have seen, in the previous chapter, that Udayāditya raised this temple at the hinge of his reign, as a kingdom pulled back from collapse made permanent in stone. The theory in his predecessor's treatise tells us how seriously that act would have been taken. A king who had nearly lost his kingdom did not build carelessly. The discipline we have watched in the architecture — the rigour of the rotated plan, the legible order of the climbing tower, the precise crescendo of the mouldings — is the visible face of a conviction that getting the building *right* was a matter, quite literally, of the kingdom's life and death.

## VII. Seeing the Whole

Stand back, now, from the parts, and let the building resolve into the single thing it was designed to be.

It begins as a square that turns. From that one act of rotation about a fixed centre — the still point of the sanctum where the god will stand — the whole form unfolds. The turning square throws its corners out onto a circle and leaves a plan that is at once star and wheel, motion frozen into stone. That same wheeling, faceted geometry stands up off the ground into the buttresses of the wall, which carry the gods in their niches and then, refusing to stop at the roofline, run straight on up into the tower and become the climbing rows of miniature spires — the strings of pearls, hung level upon level, storey upon storey, *bhūmi* upon *bhūmi*, from the earth to the sky. The hall before the sanctum lifts its stepped bell-roof as a foothill to the tower's peak; the dome within draws the same circle overhead and lets it down as a hanging lotus; and at the summit, where the seven tiers gather to the shoulder and the great ribbed disc crowns the whole, a small carved man climbs the last of the way to plant the flag.

Every element pulls in one direction. The plan rises into the wall; the wall rises into the tower; the tower rises, seven-storeyed, toward the heaven of the seven-storeyed celestial cars; and the building's very name — *Bhūmija*, born of the earth and built of climbing storeys — declares the gesture that its every line performs. This is what the Bhūmija vision came to in its supreme example: not a mountain of clustered peaks like Khajuraho's, not a square enriched like the ordinary Nāgara shrine, but a single, disciplined, unbroken reach from the ground into the air. The Udayeśvara does not merely house the Rising Lord. In the logic of its design, from the turning square at its foundation to the flag at its crown, it *is* a rising — earth made to climb toward heaven, and held there, in red sandstone, for nine hundred years.
