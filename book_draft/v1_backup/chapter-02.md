# Chapter 2

## The Paramāras of Malwa — From Bhoja to Udayāditya

When Udayāditya raised his temple beside the Betwa, he was not building in a vacuum. He was the heir of a dynasty already three centuries old, the inheritor of a royal house whose name had once stood for the highest things a medieval Indian kingdom could offer — conquest, yes, but also poetry, learning, and the patient engineering of lakes and cities. To understand the man who built the Rising Lord, we must first understand the family he came from, the kingdom they made, and the crisis that nearly swallowed both before he took the throne. This is the story of the Paramāras of Malwa: how they rose, how they reached a golden noon under the scholar-king Bhoja, how they came close to ruin after his death, and how Udayāditya gathered up the broken pieces.

### A kingdom called Malwa

The land itself comes first. Malwa is the rolling plateau of west-central India, watered by the Betwa and the Narmadā and ringed by the Vindhya hills — a country of black cotton soil, of fertile fields and trading towns, that medieval poets liked to call by its older, more resonant name, **Avanti**. It was, by every account, rich. A thirteenth-century inscription set up far away on Mount Abu still sang its praises: "Glory to Avanti, the home of rich men, which protects the world by the heroism of its lords, cleanses it by the pure and brilliant life of its Brahmans... and which gladdens it through the fragrance arising from the dalliance of its passionate youths." [1] This was a kingdom that knew its own worth.

Two cities anchored it. The first was **Ujjain** (the Sanskrit Ujjayinī), ancient beyond memory, sacred to the great god Śiva in his form as Mahākāla, "Lord of Time," and home to one of the most venerated shrines in all of Hindu India. The second was **Dhārā** — modern Dhar — which the Paramāras made their working capital and which a Hoysala grant of 1117 remembered simply as the city "made prosperous by Bhoja." [2] From these twin seats the Paramāra kings styled themselves *Mālavapati*, "Lord of Malwa," and *Avantinaśa*, "Master of Avanti," and at their height their writ ran from the country around Kota and Bundi in the north to the Narmadā and the edge of Khandesh in the south, from Bhilsa and Sagar in the east to the river Mahī in the west. [3]

### Born of fire

Where did this family come from? The Paramāras themselves gave an answer that was less history than sacred poetry — and they told it with conviction. They claimed to be **Agnikula**, "fire-born": descended not from an ordinary ancestor but from a hero who sprang fully formed from a sacrificial fire-pit on Mount Abu (the *Arbudācala*), kindled by the sage Vasiṣṭha. In the legend, the sage's wish-granting cow had been stolen, and from his *yajña-kuṇḍa*, his fire-altar, leapt a champion to recover it — a warrior named for his deed, *Paramāra*, "the slayer of the foe." Other Rajput houses were sometimes drawn into the same fiery genealogy, but the Paramāras' claim was the strongest of them. One modern authority on the subject, Dasharatha Sharma, put it flatly: no dynasty other than the Paramāras should really be regarded as an Agnikula lineage. [4]

We need not believe the cow or the fire-pit to see why the story mattered. An origin myth like this did real work: it gave a ruling family a divine charter, tied it to the Brahmanical world of sacrifice and to the holy mountain of Abu, and set it apart from common kings. The Paramāras repeated it in their own praise-poems — most importantly in the great **Udaipur** *praśasti*, the eulogistic inscription that remains one of our chief sources for the whole dynasty's genealogy. [5]

Behind the myth, the verifiable line begins more modestly. The earliest king the records can name with any confidence is **Upendra**, placed around the opening of the ninth century. After him came a succession of rulers — Vairisiṃha, a Sīyaka, a Vākpati — who held Malwa as something closer to a provincial power than an empire, often in the shadow of greater neighbours. [6] The transformation from local dynasty to imperial house belongs to the tenth century, and to a king named Sīyaka.

### Sīyaka, Mūñja, and the making of an empire

**Sīyaka II** — also remembered as Sīyaka-Harṣa — is the king whom the dynasty's own poets credited with winning it imperial standing. As the Udaipur *praśasti* and later historians tell it, he "won imperial status for his family by the strength of his arms, and left to his successors the task of building up an ideal empire." [7] He campaigned in the north-west against the Hūṇas and clashed with the rising Chandellas to the east; out of his wars the Paramāras emerged for the first time as a genuine power on the Indian stage rather than a vassal of someone else's.

It was Sīyaka's adopted son and successor who first showed the world the peculiar Paramāra blend of sword and pen. **Vākpati Mūñja** — he is known by both names, and his inscriptions also load him with grand titles like Śrīvallabha and Pṛthvīvallabha, "Beloved of the Earth" — ruled from about 973 to 995 and became, in memory, the dynasty's first golden figure. [8] He was a warrior of furious ambition and a poet of real gift, and the two sides of him eventually collided.

Mūñja's great obsession was the south, and his great enemy the Cālukya king Tailapa of the Deccan. The tradition is almost epic in its rhythm: Mūñja is said to have marched against Tailapa and defeated him again and again — some sixteen times — until, on the seventeenth campaign, his luck ran out. Against the counsel of his minister Rudrāditya, who had warned him off these endless southern wars, he crossed once too often and was captured. [9] His elephant corps, the pride of his army — the chronicles claim he lost something like fourteen hundred and seventy-six elephants in his Deccan fighting — could not save him. [10] He was put to death in the Cālukya capital.

What the tradition lingers on is not the battle but the poet facing his end. Mūñja, led to execution, is remembered for a verse mourning not himself but the goddess of learning he was leaving behind: "Fortune will go to Govinda; glory and heroism to the house of the Hero; but when Mūñja has passed away, that storehouse of fame, Sarasvatī, will be without support." [11] This was not idle epitaph-making. Mūñja had genuinely been a patron of letters and a writer himself — verses ascribed to him survive only as quotations lovingly copied out by later anthologists and critics, who could not agree whether a given stanza was his or his predecessor's, so closely had the royal voice mingled with the poets'. [12] He built the great tank still called Mūñja Sāgar at Dhar, and gathered around him a court of luminaries — the poet Padmagupta, the dramatist Dhanañjaya, the scholar Halāyudha — who would outlast him. [13]

Mūñja's brother **Sindhurāja** succeeded him and held the kingdom together. He too led the elephants into Gujarat, and he too kept a poet at his elbow: Padmagupta wrote the *Navasāhasāṅkacarita*, a courtly epic, to celebrate Sindhurāja's marriage into a Nāga princely family. [14] But Sindhurāja's chief contribution to history was his son.

### Bhoja and the golden noon

With **Bhoja**, who came to the throne around the year 1000 and reigned roughly half a century to about 1055, the Paramāras reached their height — and Bhoja himself became something rarer than a successful king. He became a legend, the very type of the philosopher-prince, so famous that an everyday Hindi proverb still measures the distance between the great and the lowly by his name: *"Kahāṅ Rājā Bhoj, kahāṅ Gaṅgū Telī"* — "Where stands King Bhoja, and where the humble oil-presser?" [15]

Bhoja was a soldier; he practised archery, kept the great elephant army, and fought on several frontiers. [16] But it is for his mind and his works that he is remembered. The tradition credits him with an almost impossible literary output — some eighty-odd books, ranging across astronomy, medicine, poetics, grammar, architecture, religious philosophy, even the veterinary care of horses. Among the titles ascribed to him are the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* on architecture, the *Sarasvatīkaṇṭhābharaṇa* on poetics, the *Rājamārtaṇḍa* commentary on the Yoga aphorisms, and the *Rājamṛgāṅka* on astronomy and medicine. [17] Modern scholars rightly doubt that one man, however gifted, wrote all of these; many were probably composed by a stable of learned men working under his name and direction. Only one of them, the *Rājamṛgāṅka*, even carries a date of composition, the equivalent of 1042–43. [18] But that very ambiguity is the point. Under Bhoja the boundary between the king and his court of scholars had become so blurred that the whole intellectual achievement of Malwa could be filed under a single royal signature.

And what a court it was. The *Udaipur praśasti* simply calls him "the king of poets." [19] Mughal-era memory, in Abū'l-Faẓl's *Āʾīn-i Akbarī*, looked back across five centuries and reported that "Bhoja held wisdom in honour, the learned men were treated with distinction, and seekers after knowledge were encouraged by his support. Five hundred sages, the most erudite of the age, shone as the gathered wisdom of his court, and were entertained in a manner becoming their dignity and merit." [20] He handed out honorific titles to scholars — conferring "Vidyāpati," lord of learning, on the poet Bhāskarabhaṭṭa — and his name became a magnet drawing students and writers from neighbouring kingdoms to settle in Malwa, until later anthologists, unable even to keep their chronology straight, began heaping famous poets of every age into his entourage simply because that was where greatness was assumed to belong. [21] His benevolence reached beyond his own Brahmanical faith: under the influence of the Jaina poet Dhanapāla he is remembered as well-disposed toward the Jainas, a sign of the religious tolerance that helped make his Malwa a gathering-place rather than a battleground of creeds. [22]

Tradition even makes the Kamāl Maulā mosque at Dhar — its name now thoroughly Islamic — a college Bhoja built, and the claim is given weight by two remarkable charts found carved on its walls: the Sanskrit alphabet laid out along the winding body of a single serpent, its tail bearing the very endings that nouns and verbs take, and the rules of Sanskrit grammar coiled along two intertwined snakes — teaching-aids, set up for the pupils who once studied there, that let us glimpse the actual method by which children were taught their letters nearly a thousand years ago. Identical charts turn up at the Mahākāla temple in Ujjain and elsewhere, so the practice was no Dhar peculiarity but a whole pedagogy spread across the kingdom. [23]

The same restless energy went into stone and water. Bhoja rebuilt Dhārā into the splendid capital that bore his stamp for centuries. [24] South of modern Bhopal he is credited with founding the city of Bhojpur and damming a valley to create the **Bhojpur lake**, once among the very largest artificial lakes in India — a sheet of water held back by colossal masonry dams of mortarless stone fitted so tightly as to be watertight, an achievement of engineering that travellers still marvelled at when the lake had long since been drained. [25] A pretty legend says Bhoja built it to cure a deadly illness, on a physician's advice that he bathe in water gathered from three hundred and sixty-five streams; engineers scoured the Vindhyas, found a site fed by three hundred and fifty-nine springs, and diverted a river to make up the difference. [26] The story is fanciful; the dams were real.

Malwa under Bhoja, in short, was as close to an ideal Hindu state as its admirers could imagine — prosperous, orderly, devout, and luminous with learning. A couplet long associated with him captures the way later memory framed his whole reign: in his lifetime, *"Adya Dhārā sadādhārā"* — "Today Dhārā has a firm support"; on his death, the same words turned inside out — *"Adya Dhārā nirādhārā"* — "Today Dhārā is left without support." [27] The pun is untranslatable and the grief is not. With Bhoja gone, the support did indeed give way.

### The kingdom that nearly died

For Bhoja's death opened the floodgates. A kingdom that shines as a cultural beacon also gleams as a target, and Malwa's neighbours had long resented Paramāra power. [GAP — the specific events of the crisis immediately after Bhoja's death (c. 1055), including the often-cited combined assault on Malwa and the sack of Dhārā, are not narrated in the sources consulted for this chapter; the standard dynastic histories treat them at length and a fuller account belongs to them.]

What the sources before me do make unmistakable is the longer pattern into which that crisis fed. From the later eleventh century onward, Malwa was caught in a vice. To the west pressed the **Cālukyas of Gujarat** — the Caulukyas, or Solaṅkīs, of Anhilwar — whose kings warred against the Paramāras for generations; the Gujarat ruler Jayasiṃha would in time take Dhārā by storm and carry a Paramāra king captive to his own capital. [28] To the south, across the Narmadā, loomed the Deccan powers, first the Cālukyas of Kalyāṇī and later the **Yādavas of Devagiri**, whose armies raided Malwa again and again; their inscriptions boast, with grim repetition, of Yādava kings who were "a blast of the day of doom in extinguishing the lamps of the Mālavas." [29] And gathering on the northern horizon, though its full weight would not fall until the thirteenth century, was the new power of the Delhi Sultanate. The story of the dynasty's last century, as its historians tell it, becomes "one long series of tales of rapid deterioration and collapse," a kingdom whose nervous system was shattered by invasion after invasion until it finally broke down. [30]

That collapse was still generations in the future, the end-point of a long erosion driven by enemies on every side. [31] But the vice had already begun to close, and the Paramāra house had to find a king strong enough to hold the jaws apart. It found one in Udayāditya.

### Udayāditya, the steadier of the kingdom

He arrives in our records less as a conqueror than as a restorer — the man who set the kingdom back on its feet. The very texture of his reign, as it survives, is the texture of consolidation: coins, building, the patient reassertion of order.

Consider the coins. Gold pieces apparently struck in Udayāditya's reign have been recovered in the Central Provinces, modelled on the currency of the Kalachuri king Gāṅgeyadeva and bearing on the obverse the legend *Śrīmad-Udaya-deva*. [32] A king who issues good gold coinage is a king whose treasury and authority have steadied. Consider, too, the language his own inscriptions use about him: Udayāditya, together with his son and successor Naravarman, "declared that their swords were ever ready to protect *varṇa*" — to defend the very order of caste and society that a long bout of invasion and disorder had threatened. [33] This is the self-image of a restorer, a king who saw his task as the repair of a frayed social and political fabric.

He bound the dynasty back into the web of Indian royalty through marriage, giving a daughter to the Guhila king of Mewar, a connection that tied Malwa to one of the great houses of Rajputana. [34] And like every Paramāra worth the name, he built — not merely for use but for memory. Udayāditya founded a whole new city thirty miles north of Bhilsa and, in keeping with an old Paramāra habit, named it after himself: **Udayapur**, "the city of Udaya." [35] He is credited as well with a great tank, the Udayasamudra, now lost. [36] A charming folk-legend grew up around the city's founding — a tale of the king swallowing a grateful but treacherous snake, of a second snake guarding buried treasure, and of a quick-witted queen who overheard the serpents quarrelling and saved her husband — but the inscriptions are plain that the city was Udayāditya's deliberate foundation, not a hunter's lucky accident. [37]

And at Udayapur he raised the temple this book is about. The record is precise where it matters: the great Śiva temple was built by Udayāditya, and an inscription preserved at the site dates the work to the equivalent of 1059. [38] (The reader may notice that the older surveys call this shrine the Nīlakaṇṭheśvara temple, "Lord of the Blue Throat" — one of Śiva's many names — while the kingdom's other inscriptions glory in the god under a whole litany of titles, Mahākāla and Amareśvara and Siddhanātha among them; the name by which we know it best, and the meaning Chapter 1 has already drawn out of it, is the Udayeśvara, the Rising Lord. [39]) It is enough here to say that the temple was no isolated act of piety. It was the crowning work of a reign devoted to putting Malwa back together — a Paramāra king doing, in stone and ritual, exactly what his great ancestors Mūñja and Bhoja had done before the long night of invasions: declaring, through magnificence, that the dynasty endured.

For the Paramāras were, before all else, devout worshippers of Śiva. The list of kings who poured their wealth into his worship reads like a roll-call of the dynasty — Sīyaka-Harṣa, Vākpati, Bhoja, and now Udayāditya, who "built a temple to Śiva at Udayapur." [40] In raising the Rising Lord, Udayāditya was at once the heir of Bhoja's golden age and the answer to the crisis that had followed it: a king who had steadied a reeling kingdom and who marked his recovery by building one of the most beautiful temples in India.

The life infused into the dynasty by "the ever-memorable Mūñja and Bhoja" would, in the end, be blighted; the long defeat of the thirteenth century lay ahead, and the Paramāras would not escape it. [41] But that is a later chapter of the dynasty's history, and not the one that produced our temple. The moment that produced the temple was this one: a kingdom saved, a king named for the rising sun, and a hillside above the Betwa cleared for the work of the masons. To that work — the building of the Udayeśvara itself — we now turn.
