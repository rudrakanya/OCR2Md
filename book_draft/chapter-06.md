# Chapter 6 — Philosophy in Stone: Śaiva Siddhānta and Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*

## I. From the Measured Wall to the Meaning Within

The chapters before this one have looked hard at a building, and looked lovingly — they have measured its plan and named its mouldings; they have followed the Bhūmija tower as it rises out of the very ground in its serried, climbing rows of miniature spires; they have walked the long outer walls and counted the gods carved upon them, the dancing Naṭeśa, the meditating Mṛtyuñjaya, the guardians of the eight directions ranged each in his proper quarter, the fierce goddesses, and the great syncretic forms in which Śiva gathers Viṣṇu and the Sun and Brahmā into the single compass of his body. We have, in short, read the Udayeśvara as an object — as architecture, as sculpture, as stone that one may stand before and admire.

This chapter asks something else of us. Not *what* is carved, but *why* — and why arranged just so. For a temple of this kind was never meant to be only a beautiful thing. It was meant to be true. Beneath its design there lay a complete and rigorous account of what reality is, of how the soul is bound within it, and of how that soul may at last be freed — an account the building was raised to make visible to the eye. The scholar whose decade of patient study underlies so much of this book, Anupa Pande, titled her work not merely on the *art and architecture* of the Udayeśvara but on its *philosophy* — the philosophy of the **Śaiva Siddhānta**, the great medieval system of thought about Śiva.[1] To understand the temple as its makers understood it, we must learn something of that system. And here we have a rare and precise reason to do so, for the Paramāra dynasty that lifted this temple out of the earth produced, in the person of its most famous king, one of the recognised authorities on exactly this philosophy. Bhoja Paramāra — scholar-king, patron of the architectural treatise that first gave the Bhūmija style its name — also composed a treatise on Śaiva Siddhānta itself, the *Tattvaprakāśa*.[2]

So this is the interpretive heart of the book: the chapter in which the stone is at last disclosed as theology, and the dynasty's buildings and the dynasty's books are shown to be two utterances of a single vision. Yet we begin with a word of honesty about evidence. The exposition of Śaiva Siddhānta that follows rests on the settled scholarly account of that school — its doctrines are well attested across a great body of Sanskrit scripture and commentary — and on what the sources behind this book tell us of how that doctrine was understood and lived in eleventh-century Malwa. Where the sources are explicit, I follow them closely; where the system's finer technicalities run beyond what those sources spell out, I have said so plainly rather than invent. Philosophy is the one place in a book like this where a confident sentence can do real harm, and I have tried to earn each one.

## II. The Most Prosperous Faith of the Age

First, then, the setting. It is easy, looking back across nine centuries, to imagine medieval India as a single, undifferentiated "Hinduism." It was nothing of the kind. The religious world into which the Udayeśvara was born was a thronged and competitive landscape of sects, and in the eleventh century one of them rose above the rest. "Śaivism," writes the historian Mahesh Singh in his study of Bhoja and his times, "was the most prosperous sect of the age," patronised by kings and commoners alike, its power and influence mirrored in the multitude of Śiva temples then rising across the subcontinent.[3] To be a king in this period and a worshipper of Śiva was to stand squarely in the broad mainstream of the age.

The Paramāras of Malwa stood there without hesitation. They were, in Singh's words, "devout worshippers of Śiva" and "great builders of Śiva temples"; their land-grants open with benedictory verses in Śiva's honour, and their kings styled themselves *Parama-Māheśvara* — "supremely devoted to Maheśvara," to the Great Lord Śiva.[4] Their very heartland was Śaiva ground. Malwa's ancient capital, Ujjayinī, had been famous through the ages as the seat of **Mahākāla**, Śiva as "Great Time" — and Bhoja, the sources tell us, used to live in the vicinity of the Mahākāla temple, whose *liṅga* is counted to this day among the twelve great *Jyotirliṅgas*, the "shining liṅgas," of India.[5] So when Udayāditya, generations later, founded a new city and crowned it with a temple to Śiva under a name of his own coining — Udayeśvara, the Rising Lord — he was not making an idiosyncratic gesture. He was doing the most characteristic thing a Paramāra king could do.

But "Śaivism" is itself too broad a word, a single lamp held over many rooms. Within the worship of Śiva the medieval texts drew distinctions among several schools, and those distinctions matter for our temple. The sources behind this book name them. There were the **Pāśupatas**, the oldest of the organised Śaiva orders, founded (in the tradition) by the teacher Lakulīśa — ascetics who smeared themselves with ash and wore the matted hair of the god.[6] There were the more extreme orders — the **Kāpālikas** and **Kālāmukhas**, the "skull-bearers" and "black-faces," who courted the terrifying, *ghora*, aspect of the god in cremation-ground rites with skull-bowls and bone ornaments.[7] And there was, set apart from all of these, the school that gives this chapter its title: the **Śaiva Siddhānta**.

The Śaiva Siddhānta — the name means, roughly, "the established conclusion (*siddhānta*) of the Śaivas," the settled doctrine, the orthodoxy — was the great theological mainstream of the movement, the deep central channel through which the rest ran. Singh classes it among the schools whose authority rested upon the *Āgamas*, the body of scripture that Śaivas held to be revealed by Śiva himself, and he traces its remarkable institutional reach.[8] Its ascetic teachers ran in unbroken lineages of *rājagurus*, royal preceptors, who initiated kings into the faith and received, in return, extraordinary patronage — whole kingdoms, on occasion, were formally made over to a Śaiva-Siddhānta preceptor as a spiritual honorarium.[9] These lineages spread, Singh shows, from the Punjab in the north to the Tamil south, and they wielded "considerable influence in central India" — the Paramāras' own country.[10] One detail brings the kinship startlingly close: an early Paramāra king of Malwa, Sīyaka, is recorded as the royal disciple of a Śaiva-Siddhānta ascetic who had "heard the whole of Śaiva-siddhānta philosophy and was like a Śiva at Kailāsa."[11] The dynasty that built the Udayeśvara had Śaiva-Siddhānta teachers at its court generations before the temple was so much as imagined.

This, then, is the frame within which we must stand. The Udayeśvara is no generic Hindu temple but a particular one: a Śaiva temple, of the Śaiva-Siddhānta persuasion, raised by a dynasty steeped to the marrow in that exact tradition. To read its meaning we must now turn to what that tradition actually taught.

## III. The Lord, the Soul, and the Bonds

At the centre of the Śaiva Siddhānta stands a single supreme reality: **Śiva**. Not Śiva as one god among many — though the temple's walls, as we shall see, are crowded with many gods — but Śiva as the absolute, the ground of all being, the conscious source from which everything that exists proceeds and to which the liberated soul, at the end of its long pilgrimage, returns. The whole architecture of the system, like the whole architecture of the temple, is an attempt to give shape to that one conviction: that at the heart of things there is a single Lord.

The classical Śaiva Siddhānta organised its account of reality around three words — three words that an educated reader of the tradition would have carried always with him, like a master-key worn close at the breast. They are **pati**, **paśu**, and **pāśa**: the Lord, the bound soul, and the bonds. The three are best taken together, held in the hand at once, as a single picture of the universe and of the soul's predicament within it.

**Pati** means "lord," "master" — the very word an ordinary speaker would use for the master of a herd or the lord of a household. In the system it names Śiva: the Lord, supreme, eternal, all-knowing, all-powerful, himself never bound by anything at all, the conscious sovereign of the whole order of being. He is the goal toward which everything tends, the one reality that was never, for an instant, in chains.

**Paśu** means, literally, "beast," "cattle" — the animal that belongs to the herd and answers to the herdsman's call. It is a deliberately humbling word, and it names the individual soul: each one of us, the countless conscious selves, who are real and eternal and, in our deepest nature, akin to Śiva himself — and who are nonetheless, for now, *bound*. The soul is *paśu* because it stands to the Lord as the beast stands to its master: belonging to him, dependent upon him, yet for the present tethered and unfree. The image is not contemptuous so much as exact. We are, in the system's eyes, conscious beings of immense dignity who have forgotten our own freedom.

And **pāśa** — the word that completes the herding metaphor with a grim precision — means "noose," "tether," "fetter": the rope that binds the beast. The *pāśa* is the whole apparatus of bondage, all that holds the soul down in finitude and forgetfulness and the turning round of birth and death. The classical tradition analysed these bonds into several strands — most centrally a kind of innate, beginningless spiritual impurity that veils the soul's true infinitude, together with the binding force of *karman*, the moral residue of past action, and the material stuff of the cosmos that clothes the soul in body after body.[12]

The beauty of the scheme — and the reason it could organise a whole temple — lies in how cleanly it states the human situation and its remedy. Reality holds exactly three kinds of thing: one Lord, many souls, and the bonds that hold the souls. The Lord is forever free; the souls are presently bound; salvation is nothing but the loosening of the bond. And here the herding image turns from bleak to consoling, from prison to promise. A noose can be untied. The soul's bondage is not its nature but its condition — something added, something that may in principle be taken away — and when at last it is taken away, the *paśu* is revealed to have been, all along, of the very same nature as the *pati*. Liberation, in the Śaiva Siddhānta, is neither annihilation nor absorption into a featureless absolute; it is the soul, loosed of its fetters, coming home into its own infinite Śiva-nature in the presence of Śiva.[13] The whole point of the system is that the beast was always, secretly, more than a beast.

The reader will already feel how such a doctrine might shape a building. A temple founded upon the *pati–paśu–pāśa* scheme is a machine for staging exactly this drama: it sets the Lord at a still centre, surrounds him with the ordered ranks of lesser beings, and offers the worshipper a path inward and upward — from the bound and busy world of the outer walls toward the single free reality at the heart. We shall return to that. First we must add the dimension that turns the three categories into a cosmos: the *tattvas*.

## IV. The Ladder of Reality

If *pati*, *paśu*, and *pāśa* tell us *what kinds* of thing exist, the doctrine of the **tattvas** tells us *how reality is layered* — how it is stacked, level upon level, from the dense to the diaphanous. The word *tattva* means, literally, "that-ness," "the truth of a thing," and by extension a "reality-level" or "principle of existence." The Indian philosophical schools used the word to enumerate the basic constituents of the universe, the rungs by which the cosmos descends from the subtle to the gross — and the Śaiva Siddhānta inherited and expanded this scheme into one of its most characteristic doctrines: a graded hierarchy of reality, conventionally counted as thirty-six *tattvas*, running from the dense physical world at the bottom up to the divine at the top.

The lower reaches of this ladder the Śaivas shared with older Indian philosophy. At the very foot stand the five gross elements — earth, water, fire, air, and ether — the stuff of the visible world. Above them lie the subtle elements and the faculties of sense and action, the mind, the ego, and the discriminating intellect; and above those again, the principles of primordial matter and its qualities. This much is the common inheritance of the Indian schools, a map of the manifest cosmos rising from solid earth through ever-finer grades toward pure consciousness. What the Śaiva Siddhānta did — and this is its own distinctive contribution — was to crown the older ladder with a series of higher, properly Śaiva levels: the principles through which Śiva's pure consciousness contracts itself, veils its own infinitude, and steps down by slow degrees into the bound and limited condition of the individual soul. And at the summit of the whole hierarchy stands Śiva himself, the highest *tattva*, pure and infinite consciousness, the reality from which the entire descending order unfolds like light from a source.[14]

A reader meeting this for the first time may find the sheer enumeration daunting, and the detailed list need not detain us. The essential thing to grasp is the *shape* of the doctrine, for the shape is what the temple embodies. The *tattvas* describe reality as a single graded ascent: a continuous ladder from the most material to the most spiritual, with the dense physical world at the foot and Śiva, pure consciousness, at the crown. Existence is not a flat field but a hierarchy with a direction. To climb "up" the ladder of *tattvas* is to move from matter toward spirit, from the many toward the one, from bondage toward the Lord. The cosmos has, built into its very structure, the same upward orientation that the soul has in its thirst for release.

Hold that image — a layered reality, rising from earth to Śiva — and turn back, for a moment, to the building described in the earlier chapters of this book. A temple whose tower was conceived, in the Bhūmija manner, as rising *directly out of the earth* and climbing in story upon story (*bhūmi* upon *bhūmi*) toward a single crowning point; a temple that is *saptabhūmi*, "seven-storeyed," in elevation, its miniature spires diminishing as they ascend toward the summit. The correspondence is not a coincidence to be argued for. It is one idea, told twice — once as metaphysics, once as masonry. The ladder of the *tattvas* and the ascent of the *śikhara* are the selfsame ascent.

## V. Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa*: The Dynasty's Own Theology

We can now draw the link that gives this chapter its peculiar force. The Śaiva Siddhānta was not, for the Paramāras, an imported or merely inherited creed patronised from a courteous distance. One of them wrote it. The great Bhoja — Bhoja the scholar-king, under whose patronage the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, the architectural treatise that first names the Bhūmija style, was compiled — was himself an author of Śaiva-Siddhānta philosophy. Among the works credited to his pen, alongside the *Yuktikalpataru* on practical and economic matters, the *Rājamārtaṇḍa*, and the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* on architecture, there stands a treatise called the **Tattvaprakāśa** — the "Light on the Tattvas," the illumination of the reality-principles.[15]

Mahesh Singh puts the matter with welcome bluntness. Bhoja, he writes, "was not only a follower but also an exponent of Śaivism. One of his works, the *Tattvaprakāśa*, deals with Śaivism."[16] The word *exponent* is the one to fasten on, and to turn in the light. A follower worships; an exponent expounds — sets out the doctrine, argues it, teaches it. Bhoja did both. He styled himself *Parama-Māheśvara* on his copperplates and opened his grants with hymns to Śiva; and then he sat down and composed a treatise whose very title announces its subject — the *tattvas*, the reality-levels we have just been climbing.[17] The *Tattvaprakāśa* is, in fact, a recognised text of the Śaiva Siddhānta canon, a concise versified statement of the school's doctrine that later teachers thought worth a commentary; and that it should have issued from the hand of a reigning Paramāra emperor is one of the more remarkable facts in the whole intellectual history of medieval India.

This is the hinge of the entire chapter, so let me state it plainly. The dynasty that built the Udayeśvara was not merely a Śaiva dynasty in the loose sense that most kings of the age were Śaiva. It was a dynasty one of whose own kings was a published authority on the precise philosophy — Śaiva Siddhānta — that the temple embodies. The same royal house, within a few generations, produced a treatise that lights up the *tattvas* in words and a temple that raises them in stone. The book and the building are the dynasty's two testimonies to one faith.

We must, however, be careful not to overclaim, and here the limits of the sources must be honoured. Udayāditya, who actually built the Udayeśvara, was Bhoja's successor in the house, not Bhoja himself; the temple post-dates the king who wrote the *Tattvaprakāśa*. I have found in the sources behind this book no statement that Udayāditya read his predecessor's treatise, or that the temple was consciously designed as an illustration of it, and I will not pretend otherwise. [GAP — the sources consulted do not document a direct, deliberate link between the text of Bhoja's *Tattvaprakāśa* and the design of Udayāditya's temple.] What the sources *do* establish is the deeper and more interesting thing: that the *Tattvaprakāśa* and the Udayeśvara grow from a single soil — one royal Śaiva-Siddhānta culture, sustained across Paramāra reigns, in which a king could expound the *tattvas* in a book and a king could raise them in a tower, because both were doing, in their different media, the work of the same faith. The continuity is dynastic and cultural, and it is real. It does not need to be turned into a footnote-to-footnote dependence to be worth the telling.

## VI. The Temple as Cosmic Diagram

Return now, with the doctrine in hand, to the building — and watch it change before you. What looked, in the earlier chapters, like an inventory of architectural members and a catalogue of sculptures begins, read through Śaiva Siddhānta, to disclose an argument. The temple is a diagram of the very cosmos the doctrine describes: a model, in living stone, of reality as the Śaiva Siddhānta understood it. Three features carry the meaning.

**The sanctum as the still centre.** At the heart of the Udayeśvara, reached through vestibule and hall, lies the **garbhagṛha** — literally the "womb-chamber" — a small, square, dark, and almost wholly unornamented room. Within it stands the **Śivaliṅga**, the aniconic emblem of Śiva, the one image in the whole vast building that represents not a form of the god but the formless Lord himself.[18] Everything we have learned about the *pati* is gathered and concentrated here, in this one dim cell. The sanctum is the architectural statement of Śiva as supreme reality: the single still point at the centre of the whole structure, dark and plain because the absolute is beyond image and ornament, square and stable because it is the unmoving ground on which everything else comes to rest. The countless carved figures of the outer walls are all in motion — dancing, striding, fighting, riding their beasts; the liṅga in the dark does not move at all. It is the *pati* at the centre of the *paśus*, the one free reality around which the bound and busy world is ranged. To walk inward to the sanctum is to walk from multiplicity toward unity, from the many gods of the walls toward the one Lord at the core — a small pilgrimage taken in a few quiet steps.

**The ascent of the tower.** Above the sanctum rises the **śikhara**, the tower, and we have already seen what it figures. Built in the Bhūmija manner so as to seem to grow straight out of the earth, climbing in diminishing tiers of miniature spires toward a single crowning point, *saptabhūmi* — seven-storeyed — in its elevation, the tower is the ladder of the *tattvas* made vertical and visible. It carries the eye, and with the eye the mind, up the graded hierarchy of reality: from the earth at its foot, through the rising stories, toward the unity at the summit. If the sanctum states the doctrine of the Lord, the tower states the doctrine of the *tattvas* — the layered cosmos rising from matter to spirit. And the two are joined in the most literal way imaginable, for the tower stands directly over the sanctum: the ascent of reality rises, in the architecture exactly as in the theology, straight up out of the still centre where the Lord abides. The pearl-strings of miniature spires that the earlier chapters traced from ground to summit are the rungs of the cosmos, and they all climb toward the one point where Śiva is.

**The ordered ranks of deities.** Finally, the populous outer walls. Chapter 5 catalogued them: the guardians of the eight directions set each in his own quarter — Indra in the east, Agni in the south-east, Yama in the south, and so onward around the compass; the great forms of Śiva and the goddess; the syncretic images in which Śiva absorbs Viṣṇu, the Sun, and Brahmā into a single body.[19] Read as theology, this crowded host is neither decoration nor a random pantheon. It is the ordered world of bound beings — the *paśus* and the lesser divine powers — arrayed in their proper places around the central Lord. The directional guardians pin the cosmos to its quarters; the ranked gods fill out its hierarchy; and the syncretic forms, in which other great deities are shown as aspects gathered into Śiva, state in sculpture the very doctrine of the sanctum: that beneath all multiplicity there is one supreme reality, and that all the powers of the cosmos are, in the end, finally his. The walls are the manifest world in its order and its plurality; the sanctum is the one reality beneath it; the tower is the ascent that joins them. The whole building is the *pati*, the *paśu*, and the rising ladder of *tattvas*, set out in stone for anyone who knows how to read it.

There is even a textual warrant, drawn from the Paramāras' own architectural tradition, for treating a temple as a cosmic object rather than a mere shelter. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* — Bhoja's architectural treatise — opens its account of temple-types not with bricks and mortar but with a myth of origins: in the beginning, it says, the creator-god fashioned the celestial palaces of the gods, the heavenly cars that move through the sky, and the temples built on earth of stone and baked brick were made *in their image*, as the ornament of cities.[20] An earthly temple, in this understanding, is the replica of a divine archetype, a piece of the heavenly order brought down into the world of men. The treatise even classifies the dozens of named temple-forms by their geometry — square, circular, octagonal, oblong — as so many disciplined ways of giving the divine a stable shape; and it warns, in its chapter on the auspicious and the inauspicious, that a temple's proportions are no neutral matter, that a well-formed building brings beneficence to its builder and a malformed one brings ruin, disease, even death.[21] A temple, for the tradition that produced the Udayeśvara, was a thing whose very measurements were charged with cosmic consequence. To build one rightly was to align a patch of earth with the order of heaven.

## VII. Theology Made Visible

We can now gather up the threads. The Udayeśvara is a Śaiva temple, raised by a dynasty that was Śaiva to its marrow and that had kept Śaiva-Siddhānta preceptors at its court for generations. The philosophy it embodies — that of the Śaiva Siddhānta — holds that reality is composed of one supreme Lord (*pati*), many souls presently bound (*paśu*), and the fetters that bind them (*pāśa*); and that the cosmos is a graded hierarchy of reality-levels (*tattvas*) rising from dense matter at the foot to Śiva, pure consciousness, at the crown. That same dynasty produced, in the scholar-king Bhoja, an actual exponent of this philosophy, whose treatise the *Tattvaprakāśa* — the "Light on the Tattvas" — stands within the recognised canon of the school. And the temple, read with the doctrine in mind, turns out to be that doctrine in three dimensions: the still, dark, formless sanctum for the Lord; the seven-storeyed tower for the ascending ladder of the *tattvas*; the ranked and ordered deities of the walls for the bound multiplicity of the manifest world, with the syncretic forms declaring that all of it is, finally, Śiva's.

This is what it means to call the Udayeśvara philosophy in stone. The architecture and sculpture that the earlier chapters described as form and ornament are revealed, in the end, as theology made visible — a complete vision of God, soul, and cosmos, set out not in the words of a treatise but in the silent grammar of a building. The flag-bearer carved climbing the spire toward the summit; the worshipper walking inward through the hall toward the dark chamber where the liṅga stands; the eye drawn up the rising tiers of the tower toward the single point at the top — all of these are one movement, the movement at the centre of the whole faith: the movement of the bound soul toward the one free Lord. The Rising Lord's temple was built so that this ascent might be something a person could not merely think but walk, and stand inside, and see.

[16] Singh, *Bhoja Paramāra and His Times*: "Bhoja, I, the Paramāra ruler of Malwa, was not only a follower but also an exponent of Śaivism. One of his works, the Tattvaprakāśa deals with Śaivism."

[17] Singh, *Bhoja Paramāra and His Times*: Bhoja's land-grants open with verses in Śiva's honour (e.g. the Ujjain and Betma plates invoking Śiva as Vyomakeśa, "he whose hair is the sky"), and he is styled *Parama-Māheśvara*; the *Udaipur Praśasti* names him *Bhargabhakta*, "worshipper of Śiva." On the *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*'s separate chapter on the *liṅga*, including the *Lokapāla liṅgas*, see Singh, citing *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* ch. 70.

[18] On the *garbhagṛha*, the *antarāla* leading to it, and the Śivaliṅga as the primary deity (later encased in a brass *mukhaliṅga* cover gifted in 1775 CE), see Pande, ch. I, "The Sanctum (Garbhagṛha)."

[19] On the directional guardians (*Dikpālas*) ranged around the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* walls, the major forms of Śiva and the goddesses, and the syncretic images — Harihara (Śiva and Viṣṇu), Ardhanārīśvara, and the *Hariharārkāpitāmaha* combining Śiva, Viṣṇu, Sūrya, and Brahmā — see Pande, ch. I, "Iconography of the Outer Walls" and "The Śukanāsa."

[20] *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* of Bhoja Paramāra, ch. 49: the creator-god Brahmā first fashioned the *vimānas*, the heavenly palaces and aerial cars of the gods, and the temples (*prāsādas*) of like shapes were then built on earth "by (materials) such as stones and baked bricks… for the purpose of decoration or ornamentation of the towns." Cited from the translation of *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* chs. 49–50; the chapter is also reproduced in the appendix of translations in Pande's study.

[21] *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra*, chs. 49–50: the classification of temple-types by plan (square, circular, octagonal, oblong) and the account of auspicious and inauspicious forms — well-proportioned temples being "bestowers of beneficence" to builder and patron, while ill-formed ones are "the sources of death for the owner" and bring "disease, affliction and death." See also Pande, Author's Note, on sacred architecture as an attempt "to express the ineffable through material resources."
