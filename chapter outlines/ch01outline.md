## 1.2 Long memory *(Survey + Epigraphy · ~900 w)*

**Direction.** Present Udaypur as a witness to five regimes — a micro-archive where five political orders stand side by side within roughly one square kilometre. Name each, date each, and leave each unexplained: explaining them is what the rest of the book does.

**The five layers, in order.**

**1. Paramāra — the founding regime.** Model paragraph supplied by the editor, to be verified against the corpus and rewritten in the book's voice:

> The primary landscape was laid out under the Paramāra Rajputs. The foundational monument is the grand Bhūmija-style Śiva temple, begun in 1059 CE (VS 1116) and consecrated with a flagstaff in 1080 CE (VS 1137). Surrounding it are the remains of eight subsidiary shrines, the 1,400-metre fortification wall, and the 71-acre reservoir known as the Udayasamudra.

**2. Khaljī conquest and Tughluq rule.** Guiding points: after the fall of the central Paramāra state in 1305 CE (AH 705) under Alāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī, Udaypur retained a residual Paramāra administrative presence as late as 1310 CE. By 1336–1339 CE (AH 737 and 739) the Delhi Sultanate held direct control, building a mosque west of the temple precinct from reused temple material and leaving foundational Arabic and Persian epigraphs. Be clear that this Khilaji invasion was part of the conquest of the Indian subcontinent. 

**3. Mughal / local Sultanate.** Seventeenth-century additions: the Shahi Mahal and Masjid, 1616–1632.

**4. Maratha (Scindia).** Eighteenth-century Scindia repairs, anchored on the brass face-cover given to the *liṅga* in 1775 by Khanderao Appaji, general of Mahadji Scindia — patronage before "conservation" existed. **See the routing note below on the 1645 slab.**

**5. Present day.** The Archaeological Survey of India, under the Government of India, protects the Udaypur site; the adjacent hill falls under Forest Department jurisdiction. Two departments, one ground.

**Three checks before you draft this section.**
- **The 1645 slab.** The editor's note places the trilingual Qanungo Baoli slab (13 January 1645) with the Maratha layer. The canon timeline dates it to the Mughal era, and C16.5 treats it as a Mughal-town record with its Kāyastha builders. Draft it under layer 3 and carry the Maratha layer on the 1775 cover and the Scindia repairs. Flag the change in a drafting note so the editor can overrule.
- **"One square kilometre"** is an authorial approximation, not a surveyed figure — the Survey commits 1,400 m of wall enclosing 56 acres. Mark it as approximate in the text, or reframe by the surveyed circuit and the lanes below it.
- **The 71-acre Udayasamudra.** Verify this figure against the Survey before it enters the text, and reconcile it with the position taken in 1.4 that the tank can no longer be identified on the ground. Both can hold — a recorded extent and a lost location — provided the text says so plainly.

## 1.3 Epistemic discipline *(Scripture · ~900 w)*

**Direction.** State plainly that Udaypur was not an ancient Purāṇic site, but a deliberate eleventh-century medieval foundation. Add the clarification that Udaypur's place in the historical record has yet to be represented fully.

**Write it this way.**
- No text names Udayapura before the eleventh century. The Purāṇic register touching this landscape is regional — Avanti, the Vetravatī, Ujjayinī — never local.
- Frame the finding as a strength: a dated act of will carries more than an undated antiquity. A founder, a year and a reason beat a vague deep past.
- Say what the secondary literature has done with the site so far, and where it thins out.
- State the absence; leave it unfilled. Resist every temptation to supply a Purāṇic pedigree by association.

## 1.4 Naming the settlement (kasba) *(Epigraphy · ~750 w)*

**Direction — the royal naming act.** In 1059 CE King Udayāditya named the town (Udayapura), the temple (Udayeśvara) and the lake (Udayasamudra) together, as a single act of place-making.

**Model prose supplied by the editor.** Treat this as the target voice and argument. Verify every fact against the corpus, keep the footnote anchors live, and rewrite rather than paste:

> In the mid-11th century, the Paramāra dynasty was engulfed in political chaos, battered by a series of regional attacks that threatened to fracture the kingdom. It was in this crucible that a ruler named Udayāditya stepped forward to take ownership of the realm. Moving decisively to anchor the dynasty and restore stability, he established a new centre of Paramāra power in eastern Malwa. Here, beginning in 1059 CE, he executed a masterstroke of political and spatial engineering: he built a new capital from the ground up, and wove his identity into the very fabric of the landscape.
>
> In a single, sweeping act of creation, Udayāditya established a settlement, erected a towering temple to Śiva at its heart, and carved out a massive reservoir to sustain them both. He then bound this triad together with his own name: the town became Udayapura, the temple Udayeśvara, and the water Udayasamudra[^4].
>
> The symbolism of this act was literal. The name Udayāditya translates roughly to "Sun of Dawn," and a 12th-century *praśasti* captures exactly what this new capital meant to the people of Malwa. The inscription declares that just as the rising sun clears away the night's darkness, King Udayāditya swept away the shadows looming over the Paramāra dynasty, bringing a sudden, steadying light to the region. Two decades after he laid the foundations for this new dawn, a flagstaff was hoisted above the completed temple in 1080, the ceremonial capstone to a vision set in motion twenty-one years earlier[^5].
>
> It was a brilliantly self-sustaining monument. By tying his name to the fundamental elements of human survival and faith — shelter, worship, and water — Udayāditya built a legacy that required no court historians to guard it. He relied on something far more durable: the daily habits of ordinary people calling their home by its name.
>
> Today, nine centuries of war, weather, and neglect have scoured away nearly everything else this ruler sought to leave behind, but two pillars of his triad remain exactly where he placed them. The temple still stands substantially intact, and the town still carries his name. Only the tank, Udayasamudra, has been swallowed by time. While earlier generations traced its ruins just north-east of the settlement, no one can stand on the ground today and point to its waters with certainty[^13a].
>
> The town that survived is modern-day Udaypur. It shares its name — and nothing else — with the famous Rajasthani city of lakes built five centuries later by an entirely different dynasty. They are joined only by a shared Sanskrit root and the coincidence of modern spelling. The Udaypur of this book lies in the Vidisha district of Malwa — a place where a medieval king pulled his dynasty back from the brink of ruin, anchoring his victory in an act of naming so absolute that a thousand years of history could not wear it away[^13].

**Constraints.**
- Udayāditya founded the triad in 1059 as a **territorial lord**, eleven years before he became king. Keep the accession at c. 1070 and do not let the section imply he ruled Malwa in 1059.
- The *praśasti* reading is panegyric. Present it as the claim the inscription makes.
- The endowment mechanics — how the grant worked, who built what — belong to **C7**. This section reads the act of naming only.
- The Udaipur-of-Rajasthan correction lives here, as in the model above.

## House voice — this chapter has to be readable

C1 is the sample chapter. A reader decides here whether to continue. Write narrative non-fiction with a scholarly spine, not a survey report with citations attached.

**Do this.**
- **Open on an image, never a thesis.** The first paragraph puts the reader somewhere, with something to look at. The argument arrives once they are standing in it.
- **Let concrete nouns carry the weight.** Sandstone, scooter, drying grain, chisel mark, lintel, bore-well. Abstractions earn their place after the detail has done its work.
- **Vary the sentence.** Long sentences build a landscape or a lineage; short ones land a fact. A four-word sentence after a forty-word one is the chapter's main rhythmic tool.
- **Give dates dramatic work.** "Five years after the state that built it had ceased to exist, a clerk still dated a document in the old dynasty's name" beats "a Paramāra inscription of 1310 survives."
- **Keep the framing affirmative.** Say what stands, what survived, what was made.
- **Name people.** VMT and the residents he quotes, Udayāditya, the Kāyastha builders of the Baoli, Khanderao Appaji, the ASI custodian. Institutions act through people.
- **Use the ironies already in the material.** A mosque built from a temple's stone, standing while the temple stands. A protected temple beside an unprotected hill. A capital that became a village. State each once, flatly, and trust it.
- **Italicise Sanskrit and Persian terms on first use**, gloss them in half a clause, then use them plainly.

**Avoid this.**
- Heritage-brochure register: "nestled", "timeless", "the mists of time", "treasure trove", "steeped in history", "bears silent witness".
- Rhetorical questions stacked at section heads.
- Superlatives borrowed from sources — INTACH's "largest in the world" and its kin stay out of the text entirely.
- Present-tense drama applied to medieval events; present tense belongs to 1.1.
- Throat-clearing: "It is important to note that", "This chapter will examine".
- Adjective pile-ups standing in for one exact detail.

**Voice test.** Read any paragraph aloud. It should sound like a well-informed person telling you something they find genuinely interesting, with dates and sources at hand — never like a report, never like a tour guide.
