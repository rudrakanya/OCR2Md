## 3.1 Upper Rewa sandstone *(Survey · ~900 w)*

**Direction.** Establish the material: Upper Rewa (Vindhyan) sandstone as both the regional bedrock and the caprock of the local hill. Explain why the temple is red, and why it could be cut at all.

**Write it this way.**
- **Geological origin.** Place the Vindhyan sequence in time and process — sediment laid down, compacted, lifted, then stripped back by erosion until the hard Upper Rewa beds stood proud as caprock. The hill exists because this layer resisted what removed everything around it.
- **Physical characteristics.** Horizontal bedding planes and iron-oxide pigmentation, giving the stone its red-to-pink colour and its structural stability. The colour is chemistry, not patina: iron in the matrix, oxidised. Say this plainly and once.
- **Workability.** Why this particular sandstone allowed precision splitting along natural bedding planes, and what that permitted downstream — fine corbelling, and lace-carving (*jālī*) cut thin without shattering. Fine-grained, evenly bedded, soft enough to carve fresh from the quarry and hardening on exposure.
- **The chapter's thesis, stated here and carried throughout:** every later choice in the book — where the fort sits, how the temple is roofed, what the carvers could attempt — was settled by a decision the rock made first.

**Constraints.**
- The canon commits the temple to **red sandstone** (Hardy; Pande). The material description here and the temple description in C8/C12 must agree.
- Keep the fort's **fabric** — dry-stone, earthen rampart, no binding material, 1,400 m, 56 acres — for C11. Here you may say only that a landscape of split-able bedded stone makes dry-stone construction the obvious method.

## 3.2 Mesa, butte, arch *(Survey · ~900 w)*

**Direction.** Profile the topography as a tableland in the act of decaying. Three landforms, one process, caught at three stages.

**The committed profile.**

| **The mesa** | 567.66 m | the main hill-fort circuit |
| **The butte** | 582.31 m | taller than the mesa despite its far smaller surface area |
| **The natural arch** | — | named the **Third Eye** by INTACH |

**Write it this way.**
- **Lead with the sequence, not the list.** A plateau erodes into mesas; a mesa narrows into a butte; a butte is finally pierced and reduced to an arch and then to nothing. Udaypur's hill holds all three stages within sight of each other, which is what makes the landform legible.
- **Make the counter-intuitive figure land.** The butte stands higher while holding less ground — it is the older, better-defended remnant of a surface that once covered everything here. That single fact tells the reader what erosion has done.

## 3.3 What sandstone permits — and quarrying *(Monograph · ~850 w  +  Survey · ~850 w)*

**Note on structure.** The outline merges two subjects under 3.3, with two source tags and two word budgets. Draft them as two clearly separated movements under the one heading. **Recommended:** split them into **3.3 What sandstone permits** and **3.4 Quarrying**, with Forest and fauna becoming 3.5. Flag the choice in your hand-back note; the split is a heading change only and is reversible.

### Movement A — Quarrying technology *(Survey)*

**Direction.** The mechanics of extraction, described as a working method rather than a heritage curiosity.

- **Reading the stone first.** The quarryman finds the bedding plane and the joint set before he touches a tool. The rock is opened along surfaces it already possesses.
- **Wedge-hole drilling.** A line of holes cut along the chosen plane; wooden plugs driven in and wetted so that swelling timber does the splitting; the block parting along a face nobody cut.
- **Chisel work** to dress the block, and the sequence from quarry face to carved surface — roughed out at the quarry, finished at the site.
- **The live quarry by Bhujariya Talab.** The same beds worked by the same principle today. This is the chapter's strongest image: an eleventh-century technique still legible in a working face.

### Movement B — Geology as design constraint *(Monograph)*

**Direction.** How stone thickness and tensile strength dictated architecture.

- Sandstone is strong in compression and weak in tension. A beam of it spans only as far as its own weight allows.
- Hence **stone-beam lintels** over openings, and **corbelled vaults** — each course stepped inward until the gap closes — in place of the **true arch**, which the material and the building tradition both set aside.
- Hence also the carving: thin, even beds take deep undercutting and pierced *jālī* work, so ornament went where structure could spare it.
- End on the argument, stated once: these are not limitations the builders worked around. They are the grammar the building is written in.

**Boundaries.**
- The temple's form, plan, shrine count and image programme belong to **C8/C12**, with Pande as the authority. Here you explain only what the stone permitted.
- The unfinished hill **Naṭarāja** is C4's subject. If you cite it here as evidence of working method — chisel marks still visible on an abandoned piece — keep it to a sentence and describe it from the object: six arms, reclining on its back, unfinished, 8.2 × 4.2 m.
- **Illegal mining, dynamite and condition reporting belong to the Afterword.** C3 describes quarrying as a technology and a live trade. It makes no advocacy case.

## 3.4 Forest and fauna *(Survey · ~750 w)*

**Direction.** The dry-deciduous Vindhyan forest that covers the hill: what grows, what lives there, and who holds the land.

**Vegetation layers.** Dominated by **Van Tulsi**, **Neem**, **Bel**, **Palāśa** (Flame of the Forest) and stands of **Teak**. Describe the canopy as layered — teak and palāśa above, bel and neem through the middle, van tulsi and scrub at ground level — and tie the assemblage to the thin soil and the seasonal water regime C2 established.

**Fauna.** Vultures on the scarps, langur through the trees, bats in the caves. Note what the landform gives each: cliffs for nesting, cover for troops, cavities in the rock for roosts. The geology of 3.2 explains the ecology of 3.4.

**Tenure.** The hill holds **reserved-forest status**. State it as a land fact and stop — what protection covers and omits is C17's subject, and the collapse of the hill is the Afterword's.

**The forest as pharmacopoeia — see flag F1.** The strongest available link runs through the plants themselves. Bel (*Aegle marmelos*) is a Śaiva plant, its leaves offered to the *liṅga*; neem, bel and van tulsi are all standard materia medica. A forest of medicinal and ritual species surrounds a Śiva temple, and the temple's daily worship draws on it. Make that connection at the level the evidence supports.

**Close the chapter** on the single material: one rock cut into a fort, a temple and a quarry face, under one forest that supplies the worship the rock was cut for. Then hand forward — C4 asks who used this hill first.

## House voice — geology that reads

This chapter carries the highest risk of turning into a report. Write it as narrative non-fiction with a scholarly spine.

**Do this.**
- **Tell it as a process, in order.** Deposition, uplift, erosion, quarry, chisel, carving, worship. The chapter has a natural chronology of hundreds of millions of years narrowing to one worked face. Use it.
- **Open on an image, never a thesis.** Put the reader at a quarry face, a bedding plane, a split block — then explain what they are looking at.
- **Let concrete nouns carry the weight.** Wedge-hole, wet timber, joint, iron oxide, chisel mark, bel leaf, scarp. Abstractions arrive after the detail has done its work.
- **Vary the sentence.** Long ones to build the geological story, short ones to land a figure. 582.31 m deserves a short sentence.
- **Gloss every technical term in half a clause on first use** — bedding plane, corbel, caprock, mesa, butte, *jālī* — then use it plainly. Assume an intelligent reader with no geology.
- **Keep the framing affirmative.** Say what the stone permits, what it carries, what survives in it.
- **Use the ironies the material supplies.** The same beds worked today and in 1059. A hill that is disappearing while holding up a thousand-year-old building. A butte that stands highest because it has lost the most.

**Avoid this.**
- Textbook register: "It should be noted that the lithology comprises…"
- Heritage-brochure register: "nestled", "timeless", "the mists of time", "bears silent witness".
- Superlatives borrowed from the Survey.
- Adjective pile-ups standing in for one exact figure.
- Unsourced natural history. Every species, elevation and habit comes from the corpus.

**Voice test.** Read any paragraph aloud. It should sound like a well-informed person showing you a rock face and telling you why it matters — never like a survey report, never like a tour guide.

## Flags to resolve
