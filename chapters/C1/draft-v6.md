# C1 · Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

## 1.1 Arrival

This is the Udaypur of Vidisha district in Madhya Pradesh, a village that shares its name with the far larger Udaipur of Rajasthan and has a wholly separate history. [ed] The way in is worth describing exactly, because it is the first thing a visitor meets. [ed] The journalist Vijay Manohar Tiwari, who has walked the approach many times, takes the reader up "an extremely narrow lane, barely six or seven feet wide" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023]. The lane enters the settlement from the main road and climbs a slope for about three hundred metres, and along both sides of it stand decades-old shops of cloth, groceries and sweets, built from the stone of Udaypur itself, with barely any cement or brick in them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023].

At the top, the lane delivers you. "At the end of the lane a very old, huge stone doorway appears, on the other side of which is the Neelkantheshwar Mahadev temple, one of the finest monuments of this region from the era of the Paramara kings" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. That building is the subject of much of this book, and it answers to two names: the village and the surveys call it Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord, while the inscriptions and the scholarship call it Udayeśvara, after the king who built it [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. Tiwari notes that the lane is the only route to that rear doorway, because the temple's main east-facing door has been shut for as long as anyone can say [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. One of the most accomplished surviving buildings of eleventh-century central India is therefore reached from behind, through a working street of shops. [ed]

The same lane is the way into the rest of the town, and that is the more interesting thing about it. [ed] Tiwari reports that "these lanes lead into a settlement where countless stories of a thousand years are scattered at every step", and that on both sides of the temple precinct stand structures raised in the time of the Paramāra kings — old houses built from the arrangement of massive carved stones [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. He adds a reading of the town's shape: the lanes, he thinks, must once have been broad and straight, and narrowed over the centuries as building encroached on them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. Whether the medieval streets were in fact broader is not something the surviving record can settle, and the reading is Tiwari's. [ed]

The scale of what stands here can take a specialist by surprise. [ed] Tiwari describes bringing the historian Suresh Mishra to Udaypur for the first time: seeing the temple's architecture from a distance, Mishra had not imagined that a building of such magnificence stood so close to Bhopal; he went up to the sanctum, cleaned his spectacles, examined the inscriptions on the walls of the entrance, and fell silent [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00046].

How visitors use the place is its own kind of evidence. [ed] Since cameras became cheap and phones universal, Tiwari observes, people stop outside the temple for photographs and move on, and few go further into the lanes around it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Much of what the town holds lies outside the few minutes most visitors give it: the inscriptions its ancestors left on the walls, the stepwells and the tanks [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Tiwari has raised the condition of the approach repeatedly — with shopkeepers, with the three-tier panchayat that governs the village, and with leaders and government functionaries — and reports that it stood unchanged each time he returned [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025]. That is his assessment, and this book keeps it in his voice. [ed]

Then the arithmetic. [ed] By the 2011 census, Udaypur holds 6,383 people — 3,405 male and 2,978 female — in about 1,247 houses, with a literacy rate of 54.28 per cent [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. The survey carried out in 2022 by INTACH, the Indian National Trust for Art and Cultural Heritage, gives the village a total area of 1,966 hectares and repeats the same population and household figures [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162]. The country around it is agrarian and grows wheat, which goes to the large market at Ganj Basoda [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That is the ordinary description of an ordinary Indian village, and it is the reason this book exists. [ed] A settlement of six thousand carries a documentary and architectural record that would do credit to a district capital: a temple that the Archaeological Survey of India protects as a monument of national importance [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]; a reservoir dug by an eleventh-century king [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]; a Mughal-period mosque with a Persian foundation record, standing inside a Paramāra palace [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]; and, scattered through the lanes, the houses of the people who lived beside all of it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. The gap between the size of the place and the size of its record is this book's subject. [ed]

One thing this chapter will not do is pretend the visitor's experience is timeless. [ed] Tiwari's walk belongs to the early 2020s, and the town he describes — the closed east door, the narrow lane, the photographs taken from outside — is the town of that moment [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. What follows is an account of how it came to be what it is. [ed]

## 1.2 The inventory: five regimes in one small compass

Walk out of the temple's rear door and turn in any direction, and within a few minutes you cross five political regimes. [ed] They are not evenly represented: some left buildings, one left only an object, and the last left a pair of overlapping jurisdictions. [ed] The village's revenue boundary encloses 1,966 hectares [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162], but the monuments that carry this history stand close together, in and around the settlement core. [ed]

**First, the Paramāras.** The earliest layer is also the largest. Contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions on the temple's porches record that it was founded by the Paramāra king Udayāditya, that construction commenced in 1059 CE — Vikrama Saṃvat 1116, the era used in these records, which runs fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era — and that the flagstaff was hoisted in 1080 CE, V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The composite survey of Madhya Bharat's monuments, compiled when that state still existed, calls the building the absolute masterpiece of the *bhūmija* manner of temple architecture in India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Ganguly, reading the same epigraphic record, gives the foundation as saṃvat 1116, 1059 CE [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The king's work was not confined to the temple: INTACH records the Udaysagar tank as built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, and notes that a second tank, the Bhujariya, already existed before his time and may belong to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The eleventh-century foundation was laid on a site that was not empty [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

**Second, the Delhi Sultanate and its Tughluq officers.** This layer arrives through inscriptions and through demolition. [ed] INTACH reads an inscription from Udaypur as indicating that the Paramāra dynasty survived until 1310 CE, at least in the north-eastern part of Mālava — the region around Ujjain and Dhārā that the dynasty had ruled — and reads a later inscription as showing that the Delhi Sultanate had captured the area by 1338 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]. That is the survey's inference from two epigraphs rather than a statement either record makes on its own, and it is worth keeping in that register. [ed] Within the same span, someone built inside the temple compound: Muḥammad Tughluq damaged the attendant temples between 1336 and 1338 CE and erected a mosque at the back of the main temple from the material of what had been pulled down, an act recorded by two Persian inscriptions on the mosque itself [src: pande-udayesvara-00391], [src: patil-1952-00387]. D. R. Patil is precise about the dates — A.H. 737–39 in the Islamic calendar, that is 1336–38 CE — and equally precise about a misattribution: a popular legend gives the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. The compound had originally held eight minor attendant shrines, and the mosque was raised from the stone of a dismantled one [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]. The main temple escaped without serious damage [src: patil-1952-00387]. A later mosque, attributed to Sher Khan and dated 1488, stands outside the Moti Darwaza gate, from the century when Mālava was ruled by its own sultans [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

**Third, the Mughals.** The most complete seventeenth-century building in the village stands inside the eleventh-century palace, and INTACH's surveyors call it arguably the most elaborate mosque in the village for its area and the detailing of its architectural elements [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. The Shāhī Masjid sits at the south-west corner of Raja Udayāditya's palace, on a raised platform, built of sandstone with a pillared prayer hall and an open court; the survey fixes it by GPS and dates its completion to 1632 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. Its Persian inscription records the making: a *qāzī*, a judge, named Aulia commenced the mosque at Udaypur during the reign of Jahāngīr, and both the emperor and the qāzī died when the building was only half up, so that it was completed under Shāh Jahān, in a year the record gives as A.H. 1041 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]. Patil reports the same sequence with a slightly different reading of the Hijri year, A.H. 1042, both converted to 1632 CE, and notes the remains of a spacious mansion beside the mosque, probably the residence of the local governor [src: patil-1952-00388]. The composite survey lists mosque and palace together as the Shāhī Masjid and Mahal [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

**Fourth, the Marathas.** This regime left its mark not on a building but on the object at the centre of the oldest one. The *liṅga* in the sanctum — the aniconic form in which Śiva is worshipped — wears a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia, the Maratha ruler whose house then dominated this part of central India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Seven centuries after Udayāditya's flagstaff, an officer of a new power was still investing in the fabric of this temple, and the investment took the form of a covering for the god. [ed]

A second Maratha-era object has been reported to the author and is recorded here only as a claim awaiting verification. [ed] `[UNVERIFIED — NOT IN KB: a trilingual inscribed slab of 1645 CE at the Qanungo Baoli, cited as evidence of Maratha-era administration at Udaypur; awaiting source]` The sources assembled for this book hold no inscription of that date, no stepwell of that name and no trilingual slab; they do hold inscriptions at other Udaypur stepwells, which may or may not prove to be the same object under another name. [ed] The date would in any case need explaining, since it falls a century before the Maratha ascendancy that the 1775 plate belongs to. [ed] Until a source is produced, the Maratha layer in this chapter rests on that plate alone. [ed]

**Fifth, the modern state.** The regime that governs the site now is divided between two authorities. The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], and in INTACH's own grading, ASI-protected monuments of national importance occupy the highest heritage category [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]. The hill immediately above the village answers to a different department: it is a reserved forest area in the revenue records and in the working plan of the Forest Department [src: intach-geoheritage-00090]. Two jurisdictions, and a boundary between them that runs through the middle of a single historical landscape. [ed]

These are not the whole inventory. [ed] Patil's survey lists, in the same village, the Bijamandal or Ghadiyalan-ka-makan — the remains of a two-storeyed house contemporary with the temple, probably the dwelling of its time-keeper, carrying an unfinished Sanskrit inscription that opens in praise of the Sun-god; the Bara-khambi, the surviving pillared hall of a ruined eleventh-century temple on the village outskirts, ringed with raised seats with backrests under a ceiling of massive stone slabs; and the Pisnari-ka-mandir, an old temple popularly believed to have been built by a woman out of what she earned grinding flour for the workmen on the great temple [src: patil-1952-00388]. The last of those is a tradition, and Patil reports it as one [src: patil-1952-00388]. Together they describe something the five regimes can obscure: a working town, with a time-keeper, a pillared hall and a flour-grinder's shrine. [ed]

## 1.3 What "long memory" means

It is tempting to give a town like this a deep antiquity, and the temptation should be resisted. [ed] The phrase *long memory* in this chapter's title describes a record, not an age. [ed]

Begin with what the Purāṇic material actually says. [ed] The Skanda-Purāṇa, in the volume consulted for this book, is a text of sacred geography: it names holy rivers in long lists, and among them are the Carmaṇvatī, the Gomatī, the Godāvarī and the Daśārṇa [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Elsewhere it lists the rivers that come into confluence with Gaṅgā, and the Vetravatī — the Betwa, the river of this region — is among them [src: skanda-xiii-00301]. This is the register in which the region enters scripture: as water, as a named tract of sacred country. [ed]

What the text does not contain is this town. [ed] The whole of the Skanda-Purāṇa material assembled for this book — 713 passages — was searched for *Udayapura*, *Udaypur*, *Udayeśvara* and उदयपुर, and the search returned nothing: not one passage names the town, the temple or the king. [ed] A negative result from one volume has to be stated for what it is. [ed] It does not prove that no Sanskrit text anywhere names Udayapura; it establishes that the Purāṇic material this book has consulted reaches this region as landscape — the Vetravatī, the Daśārṇa — and not as a settlement of that name [src: skanda-xiii-00301], [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Anyone who wishes to argue that Udaypur is a Purāṇic site will have to produce the passage. [ed]

What can be said positively is better evidenced. [ed] The town's earliest secure date is 1059 CE, and it comes from the building's own inscriptions rather than from any later tradition [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That claim has to be made precisely, because the evidence is not silent about what came before. [ed] INTACH's survey states that Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the ninth century, while its prominence came in the period of Udayāditya [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. The same survey dates the Bhujariya tank, which stood before the king arrived, to perhaps the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. So there was something here before 1059: a settlement, a water body, presumably fields and the people who worked them. [ed] What there is no evidence for is a place called Udayapura. [ed] The claim of this chapter is therefore about naming and foundation, not about occupation: the site was inhabited; the town in the form that has come down to us was made in the eleventh century, and named then. [ed]

That is what *long memory* means here. [ed] Not that Udaypur is old beyond reckoning, but that a small place has been continuously written about for nine centuries — in Sanskrit on its porches in the eleventh [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; in the inscriptions that, as INTACH reads them, carry the Paramāras to 1310 and the Sultanate to 1338 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]; in Persian on a mosque wall in the fourteenth [src: patil-1952-00387]; on another mosque in the fifteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]; in Persian again in the seventeenth [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]; in a Sanskrit inscription on a house near the temple, left unfinished [src: patil-1952-00388]; in a Maratha general's donation in the eighteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; and, in the present century, in survey reports that fix structures by GPS and census returns that count households [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293], [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. No single institution kept this record. [ed] It accumulated because successive powers found the same small place worth writing on. [ed]

There is a discipline in this that the rest of the book will need. [ed] A place with a nine-hundred-year record attracts stories that outrun the evidence, and this temple has attracted more than its share: Ganguly records the tradition that Muḥammad Tughluq ordered the temple blown up and that the priests defeated the gunpowder by running water over it, and a second tradition that Aurangzeb ordered its destruction and was dissuaded by a dream [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Both are worth having, and neither is a record of what happened. [ed]

## 1.4 The name

Udayāditya did something at Udaypur that founders did not always trouble to do: he signed it. [ed]

The town carries his name. INTACH states the matter directly: the place is named after King Udayāditya, the Paramāra ruler of the Mālava region of central India [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. So does the temple — Udayeśvara, the lord of Udaya — which the porch inscriptions record as his foundation [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. So does the water: the Udaysagar tank, built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, the same year in which the temple's construction commenced [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Of the three namings, two can be dated, and they fall in the same year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

There is a complication in the temple's name, and it is instructive. [ed] The building is known by two names: Udayeśvara, and Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. One belongs to the king, the other to the god. [ed] Tiwari's account and the INTACH surveys use the god's name, which is what the village calls it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024], [src: intach-geoheritage-00089]; the king's name is the one that survives in the inscriptions and the scholarship [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. A name given from above and a name used from below have sat on the same building for nine centuries, and the town has quietly preferred the god's. [ed]

The naming also had something to be measured against. [ed] The village already held an older tank, the Bhujariya, which INTACH dates to perhaps the eighth or ninth century and which carries no royal name at all; the architectural detail of the two is, in the survey's assessment, very different [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. One reservoir had simply been there. The other was made, dated and named in a single year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. That contrast is the difference between a place that accumulates and a place that is founded. [ed]

Two cautions belong with this. [ed] The first is that no source consulted for this book states that town, temple and tank were named together, in one act, as a deliberate programme; what the sources give is three separately attested namings that share a king, a name and, for two of them, a year [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Reading them as a single act of place-making is this book's interpretation, and the reader is entitled to know that. [ed]

The second caution is chronological, and it matters more than it looks. [ed] This book adopts an accession around 1070 for Udayāditya's rule over Mālava, for reasons set out below — which means that in 1059, when the temple was begun and the tank dug, he was on Ganguly's surmise not yet the paramount king [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Tiwari, walking the site, reads the walled town with the king's palace inside it as evidence that Bhoja's heirs lived here — Bhoja being the most celebrated of the Paramāra rulers, Udayāditya's predecessor in the dynasty — and identifies the place as named after that same Udayāditya [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00078]. His reading of the palace is an inference from the standing remains rather than a documented fact. [ed] What the evidence supports is narrower, and stranger, than a king naming a capital: on Ganguly's surmise, a man not yet sovereign founded a town, a temple and a reservoir, and attached his own name to all three [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Whatever he was at the time, he was building as though he expected to be remembered. [ed]

## 1.5 Stance: how this book handles its evidence

A book about a place with this much documentation has to declare its method, because the documentation is of several incompatible kinds. [ed]

**Architecture and epigraphy are kept apart from folklore.** Both belong in the book; neither does the other's work. [ed] The Tughluq mosque behind the temple is dated by two Persian inscriptions on the building to A.H. 737–39, that is 1336–38 CE [src: patil-1952-00387]. The tradition that Aurangzeb ordered the temple destroyed, and the tradition that gunpowder was defeated by priests pouring water, are recorded by Ganguly as traditions [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Patil, writing in 1952, had already noticed the collision and resolved it: a popular legend attributes the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. This book follows that practice. Where a story and an inscription disagree, the inscription has the better claim on what happened, and the story becomes evidence of something else — of how the town explained itself to itself. [ed]

**Inscriptions are read as claims, not as reports.** A royal inscription is a *praśasti*, a panegyric composed to be believed, and its purpose is to establish something. [ed] That a sixteenth-century inscription at the temple calls it the most beautiful in India is evidence of how the building was regarded, not a verdict this book endorses [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The foundation inscriptions on the porches are the securest dates in this chapter precisely because they record administrative acts — a commencement, the hoisting of a flagstaff — rather than a virtue [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

**Two smaller rules govern how sources reach the page.** [ed] The first concerns quotation. Parts of the material assembled for this book are not transcriptions but restatements produced by software, and parts come from scans whose lines are cropped at the binding; both can support a fact, and neither may be quoted as a source's own words. In this chapter the rule bites only on Tiwari, and there it is satisfied: his sentences are quoted directly because his text has been verified against a readable original [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023], [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. The second concerns solitude. Many statements here rest on a single source — the population figures on the census as INTACH reports it [src: intach-geoheritage-00133], the Mughal mosque's sequence on one Persian record as two surveys read it [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110], [src: patil-1952-00388]. A claim carried by one source is not thereby wrong, but it is differently held than one carried by several, and this book attributes rather than asserts wherever that is so. When a name is attached to a statement — INTACH, Patil, Ganguly, Tiwari — the attribution is doing work. [ed]

**Three dates anchor everything that follows.** Two are firm. [ed]

*1059 CE, the foundation.* The porch inscriptions record construction commencing in V.S. 1116 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; Ganguly gives the same year from the epigraphic record [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]; INTACH dates the Udaysagar tank to it [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Three readings converge — though it should be said that they converge on one inscription, not on three independent witnesses [src: ganguly-paramara-00562].

*1080 CE, the flagstaff.* The same porch inscriptions record the hoisting of the *dhvaja* in V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Twenty-one years separate the two dates.

*c. 1070 CE, the accession.* Here the book adopts a date it cannot prove, and says so. [ed] The difficulty is epigraphic. Ganguly notes that the Panhera inscription, dated V.S. 1116 — 1059 CE — was issued by a feudatory of Jayasiṃha, which places the paramount throne with Jayasiṃha in the very year a Udaypur record shows Udayāditya ruling [src: ganguly-paramara-00262], [src: ganguly-paramara-00284]. Ganguly's own way out is a surmise, and he offers it as one: that Udayāditya held his paternal territory and built at Udaypur in 1059 without yet being sovereign of Mālava [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. The sources divide accordingly. INTACH's numismatic section gives the reign as 1070–1093 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075]; another passage in the same INTACH volume gives c. 1059–93 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]; Patil gives 1058–1087 [src: patil-1952-00095]; Ganguly gives 1059–1086 [src: ganguly-paramara-00568].

This book's formulation is that **Udayāditya ruled Mālava from about 1070 until the early 1090s, and was already active at Udaypur by 1059.** The second half of that sentence is true on every reading in the evidence; the first follows only one of them, and is adopted because Ganguly argues that it reconciles the Udaypur record with the Panhera inscription, rather than because the sources agree [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075], [src: ganguly-paramara-00262]. A reader who prefers Patil's 1058 or Ganguly's 1059 can follow every argument in this book with the accession moved earlier; nothing later depends on the later date except the description of what Udayāditya was in 1059, and this chapter has already said that he may not yet have been king. [ed]

That is the method in miniature. [ed] Where the evidence is firm, this book asserts. Where it divides, the division is shown, along with which way the book has gone and why. Where the record is silent — as it is on the Purāṇic naming of this town, and on whether the three namings were one act — the book says so rather than filling the gap with something plausible. [ed]
