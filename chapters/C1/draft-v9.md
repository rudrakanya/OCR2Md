# C1 · Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

## 1.1 Arrival

A lane six or seven feet wide turns off the main road at Udaypur and climbs a slope for about three hundred metres, past shops of cloth, groceries and sweets built from the stone of the place itself, with barely any cement or brick in them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023]. At the top it ends at a very old, huge stone doorway, and behind the doorway stands the absolute masterpiece of the *bhūmija* manner of temple architecture in India [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The village around it holds some six thousand people [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. The lane is the only way to that doorway, because the temple's main east-facing door has been shut for as long as anyone can say [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024].

The building answers to two names. The village calls it Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord; the inscriptions and the scholarship call it Udayeśvara, after the king who built it [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. By either name it is one of the finest monuments this region kept from the era of the Paramāra kings [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. One of the most accomplished surviving buildings of eleventh-century central India is reached from behind, through a working street of shops. [ed]

The same lane is the way into the rest of the town. [ed] The lanes beyond it lead into a settlement where countless stories of a thousand years lie scattered at every step, and on both sides of the temple precinct stand structures raised in the time of the Paramāra kings — old houses built from the arrangement of massive carved stones [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. Those lanes may once have been broad and straight, narrowing over the centuries as building encroached on them; no surviving record settles whether the medieval streets were wider [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029].

The scale of what stands here can take a specialist by surprise. [ed] The historian Suresh Mishra, coming to Udaypur for the first time, had not imagined that a building of such magnificence stood so close to Bhopal; he went up to the sanctum, cleaned his spectacles, examined the inscriptions on the walls of the entrance, and fell silent [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00046].

Most visitors give the place less time. [ed] Since cameras became cheap and phones universal, people stop outside the temple for photographs and move on, and few go further into the lanes around it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Much of what the town holds lies outside those few minutes: the inscriptions its ancestors left on the walls, the stepwells and the tanks [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. The condition of the approach has been raised again and again — with the shopkeepers, with the three-tier panchayat that governs the village, and with leaders and government functionaries — and it has stood unchanged [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025].

Udaypur lies in Basoda tehsil, on the east bank of the Betwa, fifteen kilometres from the sub-district headquarters at Ganj Basoda and sixty from Vidisha, the district town; the nearest railway station is at Ganj Basoda, ninety-three kilometres out from the Bhopal junction [src: intach-geoheritage-00132]. The village stands amid the eastern spurs of the Vindhyachal range, in a district of undulating country that occupies the basin of the Betwa [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00043].

By the 2011 census, Udaypur holds 6,383 people — 3,405 male and 2,978 female — in about 1,247 houses, with a literacy rate of 54.28 per cent [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. A heritage survey carried out in 2022 gives the village a total area of 1,966 hectares and repeats the same population and household figures [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162]. The country around it is agrarian and grows wheat, which goes to the large market at Ganj Basoda [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That is the ordinary description of an ordinary Indian village. [ed] What is not ordinary is the record it carries. [ed] A settlement of six thousand has a documentary and architectural inheritance that would do credit to a district capital: a temple that the Archaeological Survey of India protects as a monument of national importance, which puts it at the top of the heritage grading [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]; a reservoir dug by an eleventh-century king [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]; a Mughal-period mosque with a Persian foundation record, standing inside a Paramāra palace [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]; and, scattered through the lanes, the houses of the people who lived beside all of it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. Few villages of that size have so much written about them. [ed]

This is the town of the early 2020s: the closed east door, the narrow lane, the photographs taken from outside [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. It has not always looked like this. [ed]

## 1.2 The inventory: five regimes in one small compass

Walk out of the temple's rear door and turn in any direction, and within a few minutes you cross five political regimes. [ed] They are not evenly represented: some left buildings, one left only an object, and the last left a pair of overlapping jurisdictions. [ed] The village's revenue boundary encloses 1,966 hectares [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162], but the monuments that carry this history stand close together, in and around the settlement core. [ed]

The earliest layer is also the largest, and it is the Paramāras'. [ed] Contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions on the temple's porches record that it was founded by the Paramāra king Udayāditya, that construction commenced in 1059 CE — Vikrama Saṃvat 1116, the era used in these records, which runs fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era — and that the flagstaff was hoisted in 1080 CE, V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The same epigraphic record gives the foundation as saṃvat 1116, 1059 CE [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The king's work was not confined to the temple: the Udaysagar tank was built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, and a second tank, the Bhujariya, already existed before his time and may belong to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The eleventh-century foundation was laid on a site that was not empty [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

The Delhi Sultanate and its Tughluq officers arrive through inscriptions and through demolition. [ed] An inscription from Udaypur has been read as indicating that the Paramāra dynasty survived until 1310 CE, at least in the north-eastern part of Mālava — the region around Ujjain and Dhārā that the dynasty had ruled — and a later inscription as showing that the Delhi Sultanate had captured the area by 1338 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]. That is an inference from two epigraphs rather than a statement either record makes on its own. [ed] Within the same span, someone built inside the temple compound: Muḥammad Tughluq damaged the attendant temples between 1336 and 1338 CE and erected a mosque at the back of the main temple from the material of what had been pulled down, an act recorded by two Persian inscriptions on the mosque itself [src: pande-udayesvara-00391], [src: patil-1952-00387]. The dates are exact — A.H. 737–39 in the Islamic calendar, that is 1336–38 CE — and so is the correction of a long-standing misattribution: a popular legend gives the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. The compound had originally held eight minor attendant shrines, and the mosque was raised from the stone of a dismantled one [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]. The main temple escaped without serious damage [src: patil-1952-00387]. A later mosque, attributed to Sher Khan and dated 1488, stands outside the Moti Darwaza gate, from the century when Mālava was ruled by its own sultans [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

The Mughal century left the most complete seventeenth-century building in the village, and it stands inside the eleventh-century palace: for its area and the detailing of its architectural elements, arguably the most elaborate mosque here [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. The Shāhī Masjid sits at the south-west corner of Raja Udayāditya's palace, on a raised platform, built of sandstone with a pillared prayer hall and an open court, fixed now by GPS and dated in its completion to 1632 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. Its Persian inscription records the making: a *qāzī*, a judge, named Aulia commenced the mosque at Udaypur during the reign of Jahāngīr, and both the emperor and the qāzī died when the building was only half up, so that it was completed under Shāh Jahān, in a year the record gives as A.H. 1041 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]. A second reading of the Hijri year makes it A.H. 1042; both convert to 1632 CE, and beside the mosque stand the remains of a spacious mansion, probably the residence of the local governor [src: patil-1952-00388]. Mosque and palace are listed together as the Shāhī Masjid and Mahal [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

The Marathas left their mark not on a building but on the object at the centre of the oldest one. [ed] The *liṅga* in the sanctum — the aniconic form in which Śiva is worshipped — wears a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia, the Maratha ruler whose house then dominated this part of central India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Seven centuries after Udayāditya's flagstaff, an officer of a new power was still investing in the fabric of this temple, and the investment took the form of a covering for the god. [ed]

A second Maratha-era object has been reported at Udaypur: a trilingual inscribed slab of 1645 CE at the Qanungo Baoli, said to record Maratha-era administration in the town. [ed] `[UNVERIFIED — the 1645 Qanungo Baoli slab: reported, but not confirmed by any source consulted; awaiting a source]` No inscription of that date, no stepwell of that name and no trilingual slab has been traced, though inscriptions are recorded at other Udaypur stepwells, one of which may prove to be the same object under another name. [ed] The date would in any case need explaining, since it falls a century before the Maratha ascendancy to which the 1775 plate belongs. [ed] Until it is confirmed, the Maratha layer at Udaypur rests on that brass plate alone. [ed]

The regime that governs the site now is divided between two authorities. [ed] The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], and ASI-protected monuments of national importance occupy the highest heritage category in use [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]. The hill immediately above the village answers to a different department: it is a reserved forest area in the revenue records and in the working plan of the Forest Department [src: intach-geoheritage-00090]. Two jurisdictions, and a boundary between them that runs through the middle of a single historical landscape. [ed]

The five regimes are not the whole inventory. [ed] The same village holds the Bijamandal or Ghadiyalan-ka-makan — the remains of a two-storeyed house contemporary with the temple, probably the dwelling of its time-keeper, carrying an unfinished Sanskrit inscription that opens in praise of the Sun-god; the Bara-khambi, the surviving pillared hall of a ruined eleventh-century temple on the village outskirts, ringed with raised seats with backrests under a ceiling of massive stone slabs; and the Pisnari-ka-mandir, an old temple popularly believed to have been built by a woman out of what she earned grinding flour for the workmen on the great temple [src: patil-1952-00388]. The last of those is a tradition, and it is recorded as one [src: patil-1952-00388]. Together they describe something the five regimes can obscure: a working town, with a time-keeper, a pillared hall and a flour-grinder's shrine. [ed]

## 1.3 What is actually old here

Udaypur looks older than it is. [ed]

The Purāṇic material reaches this country as landscape. [ed] The Skanda-Purāṇa names holy rivers in long lists, and among them are the Carmaṇvatī, the Gomatī, the Godāvarī and the Daśārṇa [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Elsewhere it lists the rivers that come into confluence with Gaṅgā, and the Vetravatī — the Betwa, the river of this region — is among them [src: skanda-xiii-00301]. That is the register in which the region enters scripture: as water, as a named tract of sacred country. [ed]

What the text does not contain is the town. [ed] Across the 713 passages of the volume that covers this stretch of country, neither Udayapura nor Udaypur, neither Udayeśvara nor उदयपुर, appears once: not the town, not the temple, not the king. [ed] One volume cannot speak for the whole of Sanskrit literature, and a Purāṇic Udaypur would have to be found in some other text. [ed] In this one the region enters scripture as landscape — the Vetravatī, the Daśārṇa — and not as a settlement of that name [src: skanda-xiii-00301], [src: skanda-xiii-00207].

The district around it is another matter. [ed] Its past is a hoary one, rich in archaeology and history, referred to in the Epics, the Purāṇas and in Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical literature, and the symbols usually named for it are the Heliodoros pillar at Besnagar, the Udayagiri caves of the Gupta period and the temples at Gyaraspur, Udayapur and Badoh [src: vidisha-gazetteer-1979-00001]. Udaypur belongs on that list by its buildings rather than by its antiquity. [ed]

The earliest secure date for the town is 1059 CE, and it comes from the building's own inscriptions rather than from any later tradition [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The site was not empty before that. Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the ninth century, though its prominence came in the period of Udayāditya [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], and the Bhujariya tank, which stood before the king arrived, may belong to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. So there was something here before 1059: a settlement, a water body, presumably fields and the people who worked them. [ed] What there is no evidence for is a place called Udayapura. [ed] The eleventh century did not bring occupation to this site. [ed] It brought a name and a foundation. [ed]

The town's long memory is therefore a matter of record rather than of age. [ed] A small place has been written about continuously for nine centuries — in Sanskrit on its porches in the eleventh [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; in the inscriptions that have been read as carrying the Paramāras to 1310 and the Sultanate to 1338 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]; in Persian on a mosque wall in the fourteenth [src: patil-1952-00387]; on another mosque in the fifteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]; in Persian again in the seventeenth [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]; in a Sanskrit inscription on a house near the temple, left unfinished [src: patil-1952-00388]; in a Maratha general's donation in the eighteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; and, in the present century, in survey reports that fix structures by GPS and census returns that count households [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293], [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. No single institution kept this record. [ed] It accumulated because successive powers found the same small place worth writing on. [ed]

A nine-hundred-year record also attracts stories that outrun it. [ed] Tradition holds that Muḥammad Tughluq ordered the temple blown up and that the priests defeated the gunpowder by running water over it, and a second tradition that Aurangzeb ordered its destruction and was dissuaded by a dream [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Both are worth having, and neither is a record of what happened. [ed]

## 1.4 The name

Udayāditya did something at Udaypur that founders did not always trouble to do: he signed it. [ed]

The town carries his name: the place is named after King Udayāditya, the Paramāra ruler of the Mālava region of central India [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. So does the temple — Udayeśvara, the lord of Udaya — which the porch inscriptions record as his foundation [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. So does the water: the Udaysagar tank, built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, the same year in which the temple's construction commenced [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Of the three namings, two can be dated, and they fall in the same year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

The temple's own name is doubled, and the doubling is instructive. [ed] The building is known as Udayeśvara and as Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. One name belongs to the king, the other to the god. [ed] The god's name is the one the village uses, and the surveys follow it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024], [src: intach-geoheritage-00089]; the king's name is the one that survives in the inscriptions and the scholarship [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. A name given from above and a name used from below have sat on the same building for nine centuries, and the town has quietly preferred the god's. [ed]

The naming had something to measure itself against. [ed] The village already held an older tank, the Bhujariya, dated to perhaps the eighth or ninth century and carrying no royal name at all; the architectural detail of the two is very different [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. One reservoir had simply been there. The other was made, dated and named in a single year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. That contrast is the difference between a place that accumulates and a place that is founded. [ed]

No record says that town, temple and tank were named together, in one act; what survives is three namings that share a king, a name and, for two of them, a year [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Taken together they amount to a single act of place-making. [ed]

The chronology complicates it. [ed] Udayāditya's accession to the throne of Mālava probably falls around 1070, so that in 1059, when the temple was begun and the tank dug, he was probably not yet the paramount king [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. The walled town, square in plan and ringed by walls of equal length with the king's palace inside it, suggests that Bhoja's heirs lived here — Bhoja being the most celebrated of the Paramāra rulers, Udayāditya's predecessor in the dynasty — and the town takes its name from that same Udayāditya [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00078]. What the records leave is narrower, and stranger, than a king naming a capital: a man probably not yet sovereign founded a town, a temple and a reservoir, and attached his own name to all three [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Whatever he was at the time, he was building as though he expected to be remembered. [ed]

## 1.5 Records, legends and three dates

Udaypur is documented by several incompatible kinds of record, and they do not all answer the same question. [ed]

Architecture and epigraphy are one kind of evidence; folklore is another. [ed] The Tughluq mosque behind the temple is dated by two Persian inscriptions on the building to A.H. 737–39, that is 1336–38 CE [src: patil-1952-00387]. The tradition that Aurangzeb ordered the temple destroyed, and the tradition that gunpowder was defeated by priests pouring water over it, survive as traditions and nothing more [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The collision between them was noticed and settled as long ago as 1952: a popular legend attributes the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. Where a story and an inscription disagree about what happened, the inscription is the better witness — and the story becomes evidence of something else, of how the town explained itself to itself. [ed]

Inscriptions carry their own bias. [ed] A royal inscription is a *praśasti*, a panegyric composed to be believed, and its purpose is to establish something. [ed] A sixteenth-century inscription at the temple calls it the most beautiful in India, which records how the building was regarded rather than settling what it was [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The foundation inscriptions on the porches are the securest dates at Udaypur precisely because they record administrative acts — a commencement, the hoisting of a flagstaff — rather than a virtue [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Some of what is known rests on a single witness: the population figures on one census return [src: intach-geoheritage-00133], the Mughal mosque's sequence on one Persian record that two surveys read slightly differently [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110], [src: patil-1952-00388].

Three dates anchor the town's history. Two of them are firm. [ed]

*1059 CE, the foundation.* The porch inscriptions record construction commencing in V.S. 1116 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; the same year comes out of the epigraphic record independently [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]; the Udaysagar tank is dated to it [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Three readings converge — though they converge on one inscription, not on three independent witnesses [src: ganguly-paramara-00562].

*1080 CE, the flagstaff.* The same porch inscriptions record the hoisting of the *dhvaja* in V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Twenty-one years separate the two dates.

*The accession.* The third date is disputed, and the two positions rest on different kinds of record. [ed] At issue is a single question: in 1059, when the temple was begun, was Udayāditya already the sovereign of Mālava, or a prince building in his own territory? [ed]

The case for an early accession, in 1058 or 1059, begins with the building itself. [ed] The porch inscriptions show Udayāditya founding a temple, and the same year shows him digging a reservoir, on the scale and in the manner of a king [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The chronologies follow. One gives the reign as 1058–1087, passing straight from the joint attack by the Chalukya prince of Gujarat and the Kalachuri king of the Chedis, in which the king lost his life, to the revival of the family's fortunes under Udayāditya, with no interval named [src: patil-1952-00095]. The Un inscription is assigned to a reign of 1059–1086 [src: ganguly-paramara-00568], and another chronology gives c. 1059–93 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. Circumstance supports them. When Mālava was overrun by the combined Cauḷukya and Karṇāṭa forces and Jayasiṃha lost his life in battle, Udayāditya proved the saviour of the Paramāra kingdom and recovered it entirely; no other prince of the house of Dhārā was strong enough to hold the reins of government in that situation [src: ganguly-paramara-00284]. A kingdom in that condition does not wait a decade for a king. [ed]

Against all of that stands a record of the same year pointing the other way. [ed] The Panhera inscription, dated V.S. 1116, was issued by Mandalika, a feudatory of Jayasiṃha, which appears to place the paramount throne with Jayasiṃha in the very year the Udaypur record shows Udayāditya building [src: ganguly-paramara-00262]. The manner of the accession tells against an early date as well. Udayāditya is said to have obtained Mālava by defeating the Gurjara Karṇa, with cavalry supplied by another ruler [src: ganguly-paramara-00279], and Karṇa had probably not yet assumed kingly power in 1059 [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. A kingdom won from Karṇa cannot have been won before Karṇa was in a position to lose it. [ed] A fourth reckoning, working from the coinage rather than from the narrative sources, gives the reign as 1070–1093 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075].

What the two cases share is more than what divides them. [ed] Both have Udayāditya building at Udaypur in 1059 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; both have him ruling at Dhārā a generation later, where an inscription of 1077 from Shikarpur boasts of a great terror struck into the king of Dhārā [src: ganguly-paramara-00293], and a Jhalrapatan inscription executed in Sam 1143, 1086 CE, appears to belong to his victorious reign [src: ganguly-paramara-00290]. The disagreement is confined to the decade in which ruling his paternal territory became ruling Mālava. [ed]

One reconciliation, offered as a surmise rather than a finding, is that Udayāditya held his paternal territory and built at Udaypur in 1059 without yet being sovereign of Mālava [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Its merit is that it leaves both records standing — Jayasiṃha paramount in 1059, Udayāditya building at Udaypur in the same year — where the early dating has to explain the Panhera inscription away, and a strict late dating has to explain away a prince who builds like a king. [ed] On that reading Udayāditya ruled Mālava from about 1070 until the early 1090s [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075], and was already active at Udaypur by 1059 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

It is a reconciliation rather than a proof, and the earlier dating remains defensible: move the accession back to 1058 or 1059 and nothing in the town's history changes except the title Udayāditya held in the year he began to build. [ed] What is not in doubt is the sequence on the ground: a foundation in 1059, a flagstaff twenty-one years later, and a small place that has been written on ever since. [ed]
