# C1 · Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

## 1.1 Arrival

The way in is a disappointment, and that is the first thing worth saying about Udaypur. [ed] The journalist Vijay Manohar Tiwari, who has walked it many times, describes the approach exactly: "The third corner of that desolate three-way junction of Udaipur takes you into an extremely narrow lane, barely six or seven feet wide" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023]. The lane enters the settlement from the main road and climbs a slope for about three hundred metres, and along both sides of it stand decades-old shops of cloth, groceries and sweets, built from the stone of Udaypur itself, with barely any cement or brick in them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023].

At the top, the lane delivers you. "At the end of the lane a very old, huge stone doorway appears, on the other side of which is the Neelkantheshwar Mahadev temple, one of the finest monuments of this region from the era of the Paramara kings" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. Tiwari notes that this suffocating lane is the only route to that rear doorway, because the temple's main east-facing door has been shut for as long as anyone can say [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. A visitor arrives at one of the great buildings of eleventh-century India through its back door, past a sweet shop. [ed]

Tiwari's own verdict on the arrangement is not neutral, and he does not pretend otherwise: seeing the temple's architecture through that lane, he writes, "it felt as though a glittering diamond had been placed on a heap of garbage, and no one had any care for it" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025]. He had made the complaint before, to shopkeepers, to the three-tier panchayat, to leaders and government functionaries, and returned each time having given them a piece of his mind, with nothing changed [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025]. The judgement is his, and this book keeps it in his voice rather than adopting it. [ed]

What the visitors do is its own kind of evidence. [ed] Since cameras became cheap and phones universal, Tiwari observes, people stop outside the temple for photographs, pay little attention to the architecture, and leave Udaypur without delay [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. "No one stops. Hardly anyone ever peers into the surrounding lanes" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Nobody asks after the crumbling buildings, or reads the inscriptions the town's ancestors left on the walls, or walks up to the stepwells and tanks [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028].

That indifference is the more striking because of what the lanes hold. Tiwari reports that "these lanes lead into a settlement where countless stories of a thousand years are scattered at every step", and that on both sides of the temple precinct stand structures raised in the time of the Paramāra kings — old houses built from the arrangement of massive carved stones [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. He adds a judgement about the shape of the place: the narrow lanes, he thinks, must once have been broad, straight and clean, and shrank over the centuries under illegal encroachment [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. Whether the medieval streets were in fact broader is not something the KB can settle, and the reading is Tiwari's. [ed]

Now the arithmetic. [ed] By the 2011 census, Udaypur holds 6,383 people — 3,405 male and 2,978 female — in about 1,247 houses, with a literacy rate of 54.28 per cent [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. INTACH's 2022 survey gives the village a total area of 1,966 hectares and repeats the same population and household figures [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162]. This is a village, in other words, of the ordinary Indian sort: six thousand people, a Tuesday market's worth of shops, wheat in the fields around it.

And that is the problem this book exists to explain. [ed] A settlement of six thousand carries a documentary and architectural record that would do credit to a district capital: a temple that the Archaeological Survey of India protects as a monument of national importance [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]; a tank dug by an eleventh-century king [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]; a Mughal mosque with a Persian foundation record inside a Paramāra palace [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]; and, scattered through the lanes, the houses of people who lived beside all of it. [ed] The gap between the size of the place and the size of its record is the subject. [ed]

One thing this chapter will not do is pretend the visitor's experience is timeless. [ed] Tiwari's walk belongs to the early 2020s, and the town he describes — the shut east door, the encroached lane, the photographs taken from outside — is the town of that moment, not of every moment [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. What follows is an attempt to say how it came to be like this. [ed]

## 1.2 The inventory: five regimes inside one square kilometre

Walk out of the temple's rear door and turn in any direction, and within a few hundred metres you cross five political regimes. [ed] Most Indian towns of this size hold one or two. [ed] Udaypur holds a sequence, each leaving something built, and each of those things still standing where it was put. [ed]

**The Paramāras.** The earliest layer is also the largest. Contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions on the temple's porches record that it was founded by the Paramāra king Udayāditya, that construction commenced in 1059 CE (V.S. 1116), and that the flagstaff was hoisted in 1080 CE (V.S. 1137) [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Ganguly, reading the same epigraphic record, gives the foundation as saṃvat 1116, 1059 CE [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The king's work was not confined to the temple: INTACH records the Udaysagar tank as built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, and notes that a second tank, the Bhujariya, already existed before his time and may belong to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The eleventh-century foundation, in other words, was laid on a site that was not empty.

**The Delhi Sultanate.** The second layer arrives through inscriptions rather than buildings. INTACH's survey reads an inscription from Udaypur as indicating that the Paramāra dynasty survived until 1310 CE, at least in the north-eastern part of Mālava, and a later inscription as showing that the Delhi Sultanate had captured the area by 1338 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]. That reading is the survey's inference from two epigraphs rather than a statement any single record makes, and it is worth keeping in that register: what the stones support is a dynasty persisting locally into the fourteenth century and a new power in place within thirty years. [ed]

**The Tughluqs.** Between those two dates, someone built inside the temple compound. Muḥammad Tughluq damaged the attendant temples between 1336 and 1338 CE and erected a mosque at the back of the main temple, using the material from what had been demolished; two Persian inscriptions on the mosque record its construction [src: pande-udayesvara-00391], [src: patil-1952-00387]. D. R. Patil is precise about the dates — A.H. 737–39, that is A.D. 1336–38 — and equally precise about a misattribution: a popular legend gives the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. The composite survey of Madhya Bharat's monuments records the same episode from the fabric: the compound originally housed eight minor attendant shrines, and the mosque was built from the stone of a dismantled one [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]. The main temple itself escaped without serious damage [src: patil-1952-00387].

**The Mughals.** The third structure is the most complete piece of seventeenth-century building in the village, and it stands inside the eleventh-century palace. The Shāhī Masjid sits at the south-west corner of Raja Udayāditya's palace, on a raised platform, built of sandstone with a pillared prayer hall and an open court; INTACH fixes it by GPS and dates its completion to 1632 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. Its Persian inscription tells the story of its making: one Qazi Aulia commenced the mosque at Udaypur during the reign of Jahāngīr, and both the emperor and the Qazi died when the building was only half up, so that it was finished at the accession of Shāh Jahān in 1632 CE (A.H. 1041) [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]. Patil records the same sequence with a slightly different reading of the Hijri year, A.H. 1042, and notes the remains of a spacious mansion beside the mosque, probably the residence of the local governor [src: patil-1952-00388]. The composite survey lists mosque and palace together, and adds an earlier one — Sher Khan's Mosque of 1488 — outside the Moti Darwaza gate [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

**The Marathas.** The fourth regime left its mark not on a building but on the object at the centre of the oldest one. The liṅga in the sanctum wears a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Seven centuries after Udayāditya's flagstaff, a Maratha officer was still investing in the fabric of this temple, and the investment took the form of a covering for the god rather than a wall or a gate. [ed]

A second Maratha-era object has been reported to the author and is recorded here only as a claim awaiting verification. [ed] `[UNVERIFIED — NOT IN KB: a trilingual inscribed slab of 1645 CE at the Qanungo Baoli, cited as evidence of Maratha-era administration at Udaypur; awaiting source]` The knowledge base holds no inscription of that date, no stepwell of that name, and no trilingual slab; it does hold inscriptions at other Udaypur stepwells, which may or may not prove to be the same object under another name. [ed] Until a source is produced, the Maratha layer in this chapter rests on the 1775 plate alone. [ed]

**The modern state.** The fifth regime is the one that governs the site now, and it is divided. The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], and in INTACH's own grading, ASI-protected monuments of national importance occupy the highest heritage category [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]. The hill immediately above the village, however, answers to a different authority: it is a reserved forest area in the revenue records and in the working plan of the Forest Department [src: intach-geoheritage-00090]. One square kilometre, two jurisdictions, and a boundary between them that runs through the middle of the historical landscape. [ed]

## 1.3 What "long memory" means

It is tempting to give a town like this a deep antiquity, and the temptation should be resisted. [ed] The phrase "long memory" in this chapter's title describes a record, not an age, and the distinction matters enough to be demonstrated rather than asserted. [ed]

Begin with what the Purāṇic material actually says. [ed] The Skanda-Purāṇa, in the volume held in this corpus, is a text of sacred geography: it names holy rivers in long lists, and among them are the Carmaṇvatī, the Gomatī, the Godāvarī and the Daśārṇa [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Elsewhere it lists the rivers that come into confluence with Gaṅgā, and the Vetravatī — the Betwa, the river of this region — is among them [src: skanda-xiii-00301]. This is the register in which the region appears in scripture: as water, as a named tract of sacred country. [ed]

What the text does not contain is this town. [ed] The whole of the Skanda-Purāṇa material in the knowledge base — 713 passages — was searched for *Udayapura*, *Udaypur*, *Udayeśvara* and उदयपुर. The search returns nothing: not one passage names the town, the temple or the king. [ed] That is a negative result, and negatives from a single corpus must be stated carefully: it establishes that this Purāṇic volume does not name Udayapura, not that no Sanskrit text anywhere does. [ed] But it is enough to dispose of a particular kind of claim. [ed] The scriptural register that reaches this region reaches it as landscape — the Vetravatī, the Daśārṇa — and not as a settlement with this name [src: skanda-xiii-00301], [src: skanda-xiii-00207].

The conclusion follows, and it should be stated plainly. [ed] Udaypur is not an ancient Purāṇic site that happens to hold a medieval temple. [ed] It is an eleventh-century foundation: a town, a temple and a tank whose earliest secure date is 1059 CE, recorded by the building's own inscriptions [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That claim has to be made precisely, because the evidence is not silent about what came before. [ed] INTACH's survey states that Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the ninth century, while its prominence came in the period of Udayāditya [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. The same survey dates the Bhujariya tank, which stood before the king arrived, as possibly of the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. So there was something here — a settlement, a water body, presumably fields and people. [ed] What there was not, on the evidence available, was a place called Udayapura. [ed] The claim of this chapter is about naming and foundation, not about occupation: the site was inhabited; the town, in the form that has come down to us, was made in the eleventh century and named then. [ed]

This is what "long memory" means here. [ed] Not that Udaypur is old beyond reckoning, but that a small place has been continuously recorded — in Sanskrit on its porches in the eleventh century [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], in Persian on a mosque wall in the fourteenth [src: patil-1952-00387], in Persian again in the seventeenth [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110], in a Maratha general's donation in the eighteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], and in survey reports and census returns in the twenty-first [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. The memory is long because the recording never stopped, not because the town is older than it is. [ed]

There is a discipline in this that the rest of the book will need. [ed] A place with a thousand-year record attracts stories that outrun the evidence, and the Udayeśvara has attracted more than its share — Ganguly records the tradition that Muḥammad Tughluq ordered the temple blown up with gunpowder and that the priests defeated the charge by running water over it, and a second tradition that Aurangzeb ordered its destruction and was dissuaded by a dream [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. These are worth having. [ed] They are not worth confusing with the two Persian inscriptions on the mosque, which say who built what, and when [src: patil-1952-00387].

## 1.4 The name

Udayāditya did something at Udaypur that kings did not always trouble to do: he signed it. [ed]

The town carries his name. INTACH states the matter directly: the city is named after King Udayāditya, the Paramāra ruler of the Mālava region [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. So does the temple — Udayeśvara, the lord of Udaya — which the porch inscriptions record as his foundation [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. So does the water: the Udaysagar tank, built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, the same year in which the temple's construction commenced [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

Three things, then, bearing one name, and the two dates that can be checked fall in the same year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Tiwari, walking the site, reads the walled town with the king's palace inside it as evidence that Bhoja's heirs lived here, and identifies the place as named after that same Udayāditya [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00078]. His reading of the palace is an inference from the standing remains rather than a documented fact, and it is offered here as his. [ed]

The pattern is worth naming, with a caution attached. [ed] No source in this knowledge base states that town, temple and tank were named together, in one act, as a deliberate programme; what the sources give is three separately attested namings that share a king, a name and — for two of the three — a year [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Reading them as a single act of place-making is this book's interpretation, and the reader is entitled to know that. [ed]

It is a reasonable interpretation. [ed] A king who builds a temple, digs a reservoir and settles a town in the same years, and attaches his own name to all three, is doing something more deliberate than construction. [ed] The town was not named for a deity already resident, nor for a feature of the land, nor for an older settlement absorbed into it; on the evidence of the inscriptions it took the king's name at the moment of its making [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. Whatever stood here in the ninth century [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], what was founded in the eleventh was called after the man who founded it. [ed]

## Stance: how this book handles its evidence

A book about a place with this much documentation has to declare its method, because the documentation is of several incompatible kinds. [ed]

**Architecture and epigraphy are kept apart from folklore.** Both belong in the book; neither is allowed to do the other's work. [ed] The Tughluq mosque behind the temple is dated by two Persian inscriptions on the building to A.H. 737–39, that is 1336–38 CE [src: patil-1952-00387]. The tradition that Aurangzeb ordered the temple destroyed, and the tradition that gunpowder was defeated by priests pouring water, are recorded by Ganguly as traditions [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Patil, writing seventy years ago, had already noticed the collision and resolved it the right way: a popular legend attributes the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. This book follows that practice throughout. Where a story and an inscription disagree, the inscription decides what happened and the story becomes evidence of something else — of how the town explained itself to itself. [ed]

**Inscriptions are read as claims, not as reports.** A royal inscription is a *praśasti*, a panegyric composed to be believed, and its purpose is to establish something. [ed] That a sixteenth-century inscription at the temple calls it the most beautiful in India is evidence of how the building was regarded, not a verdict this book endorses [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The foundation inscriptions on the porches are the securest dates in the chapter precisely because they record an administrative act — commencement, hoisting of a flagstaff — rather than a virtue [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

**Three dates anchor everything that follows.** Two of them are firm. [ed]

*1059 CE, the foundation.* The porch inscriptions record construction commencing in V.S. 1116 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; Ganguly gives the same year from the epigraphic record [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]; INTACH dates the Udaysagar tank to it [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Three independent works and the building's own stone agree.

*1080 CE, the flagstaff.* The same porch inscriptions record the hoisting of the dhvaja in V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Between the two dates lie twenty-one years of building.

*c. 1070 CE, the accession.* Here the book adopts a date it cannot prove, and says so. [ed] This chapter's working formulation is that **Udayāditya ruled Mālava c. 1070–93, and was already active at Udaypur by 1059** — which is true on every reading in the evidence. The difficulty is real: a Udaypur inscription shows him ruling in 1059, while the Panhera inscription of that same year names Jayasiṃha as sovereign, so the two cannot both describe a paramount throne. The sources divide accordingly. INTACH's numismatic section gives his reign as 1070–1093 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075]; another passage in the same INTACH volume gives c. 1059–93 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]; Patil gives 1058–1087 [src: patil-1952-00095]; Ganguly gives 1059–1086 [src: ganguly-paramara-00568]. The book adopts the later accession without suppressing the earlier attestation, and a reader who prefers 1059 can follow the argument with the dates swapped. [ed]

That is the method in miniature. [ed] Where the evidence is firm, the book asserts. Where it divides, the book shows the division and states which way it has gone and why. Where the record is silent — as it is on the Purāṇic naming of this town, and as it is on whether the three namings were one act — the book says so rather than filling the gap with something plausible. [ed]

The chapters that follow take the layers in turn: the valley and its stone, the dynasty that rose in Mālava, the king, the temple he built, the words cut into it, and the town that has gone on living around the whole of it for nine hundred years. [ed]
