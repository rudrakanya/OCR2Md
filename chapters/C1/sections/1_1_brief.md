> The third corner of that desolate three-way junction of Udaipur takes you into an extremely narrow lane, barely six or seven feet wide. This single crooked and extremely narrow lane, entering the settlement from the main road, goes upward along a slope. This is a painful path to reach the greatest identity of a Udaipur that, in its own time, was magnificent and more than a thousand years old. On both sides of this roughly three-hundred-metre-long lane are decades-old shops made of stone — of cloth, groceries, sweets. These shops are made of the stones found in Udaipur itself, in which the use of cement and bricks was next to nil. In many old and closed houses, doors of the old era, of stone itself, are clearly visible. Here everything was of stone alone. It was stones alone that once wrote, in detail, the story of Udaipur. With the passing centuries, Udaipur must have kept spreading and sprawling here and there over its old plan. Broad and orderly roads turned into disorderly narrow lanes. The picture kept changing. Centuries had passed.

> I had come here many times before this too, and each time this wretched lane taunted me. Seeing, through the lane, the incomparable architecture of the temple, it felt as though a glittering diamond had been placed on a heap of garbage, and no one had any care for it. From ordinary people to the traders of the lane, from the representatives of the three-tier panchayat to the other leaders and government functionaries — I used to return only after giving them a piece of my mind. This lost lustre pricked no one's eyes. Everyone's work was going on carefree. The world's business was going on as usual. The tears that had flowed from Udaipur's eyes too had dried up long ago. > But history was terribly restless to turn a corner in Udaipur.
