# C1 · Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

## 1.1 Arrival

This is the Udaypur of Vidisha district in Madhya Pradesh, a village that shares its name with the far larger Udaipur of Rajasthan and has a wholly separate history. [ed] The journalist Vijay Manohar Tiwari, who has walked the approach many times, describes it as "an extremely narrow lane, barely six or seven feet wide" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023]. The lane enters the settlement from the main road and climbs a slope for about three hundred metres, and along both sides of it stand decades-old shops of cloth, groceries and sweets, built from the stone of Udaypur itself, with barely any cement or brick in them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023].

At the top, the lane delivers you. "At the end of the lane a very old, huge stone doorway appears, on the other side of which is the Neelkantheshwar Mahadev temple, one of the finest monuments of this region from the era of the Paramara kings" [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. The building answers to two names: the village and the surveys call it Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord, while the inscriptions and the scholarship call it Udayeśvara, after the king who built it [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. The lane is the only route to that rear doorway, because the temple's main east-facing door has been shut for as long as anyone can say [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. One of the most accomplished surviving buildings of eleventh-century central India is reached from behind, through a working street of shops. [ed]

The same lane is the way into the rest of the town. [ed] "These lanes lead into a settlement where countless stories of a thousand years are scattered at every step", Tiwari writes, and on both sides of the temple precinct stand structures raised in the time of the Paramāra kings — old houses built from the arrangement of massive carved stones [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. He reads the shape of the place as evidence of change: the lanes, he thinks, must once have been broad and straight, and narrowed over the centuries as building encroached on them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. No surviving record settles whether the medieval streets were broader, and the reading is his. [ed]

The scale of what stands here can take a specialist by surprise. [ed] Tiwari describes bringing the historian Suresh Mishra to Udaypur for the first time: seeing the temple's architecture from a distance, Mishra had not imagined that a building of such magnificence stood so close to Bhopal; he went up to the sanctum, cleaned his spectacles, examined the inscriptions on the walls of the entrance, and fell silent [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00046].

Most visitors give the place less time. [ed] Since cameras became cheap and phones universal, Tiwari observes, people stop outside the temple for photographs and move on, and few go further into the lanes around it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Much of what the town holds lies outside those few minutes: the inscriptions its ancestors left on the walls, the stepwells and the tanks [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Tiwari has raised the condition of the approach repeatedly — with shopkeepers, with the three-tier panchayat that governs the village, and with leaders and government functionaries — and reports that it stood unchanged each time he returned [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025].

Udaypur lies in Basoda tehsil, on the east bank of the Betwa, fifteen kilometres from the sub-district headquarters at Ganj Basoda and sixty from Vidisha, the district town; the nearest railway station is at Ganj Basoda, ninety-three kilometres out from the Bhopal junction [src: intach-geoheritage-00132]. The village stands amid the eastern spurs of the Vindhyachal range, in a district of undulating country that occupies the basin of the Betwa [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00043].

Then the arithmetic. [ed] By the 2011 census, Udaypur holds 6,383 people — 3,405 male and 2,978 female — in about 1,247 houses, with a literacy rate of 54.28 per cent [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. The survey carried out in 2022 by INTACH, the Indian National Trust for Art and Cultural Heritage, gives the village a total area of 1,966 hectares and repeats the same population and household figures [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162]. The country around it is agrarian and grows wheat, which goes to the large market at Ganj Basoda [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That is the ordinary description of an ordinary Indian village. [ed] What is not ordinary is the record it carries. [ed] A settlement of six thousand has a documentary and architectural inheritance that would do credit to a district capital: a temple that the Archaeological Survey of India protects as a monument of national importance [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]; a reservoir dug by an eleventh-century king [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]; a Mughal-period mosque with a Persian foundation record, standing inside a Paramāra palace [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]; and, scattered through the lanes, the houses of the people who lived beside all of it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. Few villages of that size have so much written about them. [ed]

Tiwari's walk belongs to the early 2020s, and the town he describes — the closed east door, the narrow lane, the photographs taken from outside — is the town of that moment [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. It has not always looked like this. [ed]

## 1.2 The inventory: five regimes in one small compass

Walk out of the temple's rear door and turn in any direction, and within a few minutes you cross five political regimes. [ed] They are not evenly represented: some left buildings, one left only an object, and the last left a pair of overlapping jurisdictions. [ed] The village's revenue boundary encloses 1,966 hectares [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162], but the monuments that carry this history stand close together, in and around the settlement core. [ed]

**First, the Paramāras.** The earliest layer is also the largest. Contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions on the temple's porches record that it was founded by the Paramāra king Udayāditya, that construction commenced in 1059 CE — Vikrama Saṃvat 1116, the era used in these records, which runs fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era — and that the flagstaff was hoisted in 1080 CE, V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The composite survey of Madhya Bharat's monuments, compiled when that state still existed, calls the building the absolute masterpiece of the *bhūmija* manner of temple architecture in India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Ganguly, reading the same epigraphic record, gives the foundation as saṃvat 1116, 1059 CE [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The king's work was not confined to the temple: INTACH records the Udaysagar tank as built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, and notes that a second tank, the Bhujariya, already existed before his time and may belong to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The eleventh-century foundation was laid on a site that was not empty [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

**Second, the Delhi Sultanate and its Tughluq officers.** This layer arrives through inscriptions and through demolition. [ed] INTACH reads an inscription from Udaypur as indicating that the Paramāra dynasty survived until 1310 CE, at least in the north-eastern part of Mālava — the region around Ujjain and Dhārā that the dynasty had ruled — and reads a later inscription as showing that the Delhi Sultanate had captured the area by 1338 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]. That is the survey's inference from two epigraphs rather than a statement either record makes on its own. [ed] Within the same span, someone built inside the temple compound: Muḥammad Tughluq damaged the attendant temples between 1336 and 1338 CE and erected a mosque at the back of the main temple from the material of what had been pulled down, an act recorded by two Persian inscriptions on the mosque itself [src: pande-udayesvara-00391], [src: patil-1952-00387]. D. R. Patil is precise about the dates — A.H. 737–39 in the Islamic calendar, that is 1336–38 CE — and equally precise about a misattribution: a popular legend gives the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. The compound had originally held eight minor attendant shrines, and the mosque was raised from the stone of a dismantled one [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]. The main temple escaped without serious damage [src: patil-1952-00387]. A later mosque, attributed to Sher Khan and dated 1488, stands outside the Moti Darwaza gate, from the century when Mālava was ruled by its own sultans [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

**Third, the Mughals.** The most complete seventeenth-century building in the village stands inside the eleventh-century palace, and INTACH's surveyors call it arguably the most elaborate mosque in the village for its area and the detailing of its architectural elements [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. The Shāhī Masjid sits at the south-west corner of Raja Udayāditya's palace, on a raised platform, built of sandstone with a pillared prayer hall and an open court; the survey fixes it by GPS and dates its completion to 1632 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. Its Persian inscription records the making: a *qāzī*, a judge, named Aulia commenced the mosque at Udaypur during the reign of Jahāngīr, and both the emperor and the qāzī died when the building was only half up, so that it was completed under Shāh Jahān, in a year the record gives as A.H. 1041 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]. Patil reports the same sequence with a slightly different reading of the Hijri year, A.H. 1042, both converted to 1632 CE, and notes the remains of a spacious mansion beside the mosque, probably the residence of the local governor [src: patil-1952-00388]. The composite survey lists mosque and palace together as the Shāhī Masjid and Mahal [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

**Fourth, the Marathas.** This regime left its mark not on a building but on the object at the centre of the oldest one. The *liṅga* in the sanctum — the aniconic form in which Śiva is worshipped — wears a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia, the Maratha ruler whose house then dominated this part of central India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Seven centuries after Udayāditya's flagstaff, an officer of a new power was still investing in the fabric of this temple, and the investment took the form of a covering for the god. [ed]

A second Maratha-era object has been reported at Udaypur: a trilingual inscribed slab of 1645 CE at the Qanungo Baoli, said to record Maratha-era administration in the town. [ed] `[UNVERIFIED — NOT IN KB: the 1645 Qanungo Baoli slab; no source in the knowledge base supports it; awaiting source]` The report is unconfirmed: no inscription of that date, no stepwell of that name and no trilingual slab has been traced, though inscriptions are recorded at other Udaypur stepwells, one of which may prove to be the same object under another name. [ed] The date would in any case need explaining, since it falls a century before the Maratha ascendancy to which the 1775 plate belongs. [ed] Until it is confirmed, the Maratha layer at Udaypur rests on that brass plate alone. [ed]

**Fifth, the modern state.** The regime that governs the site now is divided between two authorities. The temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], and in INTACH's own grading, ASI-protected monuments of national importance occupy the highest heritage category [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]. The hill immediately above the village answers to a different department: it is a reserved forest area in the revenue records and in the working plan of the Forest Department [src: intach-geoheritage-00090]. Two jurisdictions, and a boundary between them that runs through the middle of a single historical landscape. [ed]

These are not the whole inventory. [ed] Patil's survey lists, in the same village, the Bijamandal or Ghadiyalan-ka-makan — the remains of a two-storeyed house contemporary with the temple, probably the dwelling of its time-keeper, carrying an unfinished Sanskrit inscription that opens in praise of the Sun-god; the Bara-khambi, the surviving pillared hall of a ruined eleventh-century temple on the village outskirts, ringed with raised seats with backrests under a ceiling of massive stone slabs; and the Pisnari-ka-mandir, an old temple popularly believed to have been built by a woman out of what she earned grinding flour for the workmen on the great temple [src: patil-1952-00388]. The last of those is a tradition, and Patil reports it as one [src: patil-1952-00388]. Together they describe something the five regimes can obscure: a working town, with a time-keeper, a pillared hall and a flour-grinder's shrine. [ed]

## 1.3 What is actually old here

It is tempting to give a town like this a deep antiquity. [ed] The temptation is worth resisting, because the record says something more precise. [ed]

The Purāṇic material reaches this country as landscape. [ed] The Skanda-Purāṇa names holy rivers in long lists, and among them are the Carmaṇvatī, the Gomatī, the Godāvarī and the Daśārṇa [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Elsewhere it lists the rivers that come into confluence with Gaṅgā, and the Vetravatī — the Betwa, the river of this region — is among them [src: skanda-xiii-00301]. That is the register in which the region enters scripture: as water, as a named tract of sacred country. [ed]

What the text does not contain is the town. [ed] Across the 713 passages of the volume that covers this stretch of country, neither Udayapura nor Udaypur, neither Udayeśvara nor उदयपुर, appears once: not the town, not the temple, not the king. [ed] One volume cannot speak for the whole of Sanskrit literature, and a Purāṇic Udaypur would have to be shown from some other text. [ed] What this one establishes is that the region enters scripture as landscape — the Vetravatī, the Daśārṇa — and not as a settlement of that name [src: skanda-xiii-00301], [src: skanda-xiii-00207].

The district around it is another matter. [ed] The Vidisha gazetteer opens on a past it calls hoary, rich in archaeology and history, referred to in the Epics, the Purāṇas and in Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical literature, and it names the Heliodoros pillar at Besnagar, the Udayagiri caves of the Gupta period and the temples at Gyaraspur, Udayapur and Badoh as the symbols of that inheritance [src: vidisha-gazetteer-1979-00001]. Udaypur belongs on that list by its buildings rather than by its antiquity. [ed]

The earliest secure date for the town is 1059 CE, and it comes from the building's own inscriptions rather than from any later tradition [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

That has to be put precisely, because the evidence is not silent about what came before. [ed] INTACH's survey states that Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the ninth century, while its prominence came in the period of Udayāditya [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. The same survey dates the Bhujariya tank, which stood before the king arrived, to perhaps the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. So there was something here before 1059: a settlement, a water body, presumably fields and the people who worked them. [ed] What there is no evidence for is a place called Udayapura. [ed] The eleventh century did not bring occupation to this site. [ed] It brought a name and a foundation. [ed]

The town's long memory is therefore a matter of record rather than of age. [ed] A small place has been written about continuously for nine centuries — in Sanskrit on its porches in the eleventh [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; in the inscriptions that, as INTACH reads them, carry the Paramāras to 1310 and the Sultanate to 1338 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]; in Persian on a mosque wall in the fourteenth [src: patil-1952-00387]; on another mosque in the fifteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]; in Persian again in the seventeenth [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]; in a Sanskrit inscription on a house near the temple, left unfinished [src: patil-1952-00388]; in a Maratha general's donation in the eighteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; and, in the present century, in survey reports that fix structures by GPS and census returns that count households [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293], [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. No single institution kept this record. [ed] It accumulated because successive powers found the same small place worth writing on. [ed]

A nine-hundred-year record also attracts stories that outrun it. [ed] Ganguly records the tradition that Muḥammad Tughluq ordered the temple blown up and that the priests defeated the gunpowder by running water over it, and a second tradition that Aurangzeb ordered its destruction and was dissuaded by a dream [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Both are worth having, and neither is a record of what happened. [ed]

## 1.4 The name

Udayāditya did something at Udaypur that founders did not always trouble to do: he signed it. [ed]

The town carries his name. INTACH states the matter directly: the place is named after King Udayāditya, the Paramāra ruler of the Mālava region of central India [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. So does the temple — Udayeśvara, the lord of Udaya — which the porch inscriptions record as his foundation [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. So does the water: the Udaysagar tank, built by Udayāditya in 1059 CE, the same year in which the temple's construction commenced [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Of the three namings, two can be dated, and they fall in the same year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

There is a complication in the temple's name, and it is instructive. [ed] The building is known by two names: Udayeśvara, and Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. One belongs to the king, the other to the god. [ed] Tiwari's account and the INTACH surveys use the god's name, which is what the village calls it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024], [src: intach-geoheritage-00089]; the king's name is the one that survives in the inscriptions and the scholarship [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. A name given from above and a name used from below have sat on the same building for nine centuries, and the town has quietly preferred the god's. [ed]

The naming had something to be measured against. [ed] The village already held an older tank, the Bhujariya, which INTACH dates to perhaps the eighth or ninth century and which carries no royal name at all; the architectural detail of the two is, in the survey's assessment, very different [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. One reservoir had simply been there. The other was made, dated and named in a single year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. That contrast is the difference between a place that accumulates and a place that is founded. [ed]

Two cautions belong with this. [ed] No source states that town, temple and tank were named together, in one act, as a deliberate programme; what survives is three separately attested namings that share a king, a name and, for two of them, a year [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Read together they look like a single act of place-making, but that reading is an inference from the pattern rather than a claim any record makes. [ed]

The second caution is chronological, and it matters more than it looks. [ed] Udayāditya's accession to the throne of Mālava probably falls around 1070, which means that in 1059, when the temple was begun and the tank dug, he was on Ganguly's surmise not yet the paramount king [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Tiwari, walking the site, reads the walled town with the king's palace inside it as evidence that Bhoja's heirs lived here — Bhoja being the most celebrated of the Paramāra rulers, Udayāditya's predecessor in the dynasty — and identifies the place as named after that same Udayāditya [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00078]. His reading of the palace is an inference from the standing remains rather than a documented fact. [ed] The evidence supports something narrower, and stranger, than a king naming a capital: on Ganguly's surmise, a man not yet sovereign founded a town, a temple and a reservoir, and attached his own name to all three [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Whatever he was at the time, he was building as though he expected to be remembered. [ed]

## 1.5 The evidence, and three dates

Udaypur is documented by several incompatible kinds of record, and they do not all answer the same question. [ed]

**Architecture and epigraphy are one kind of evidence; folklore is another.** The Tughluq mosque behind the temple is dated by two Persian inscriptions on the building to A.H. 737–39, that is 1336–38 CE [src: patil-1952-00387]. The tradition that Aurangzeb ordered the temple destroyed, and the tradition that gunpowder was defeated by priests pouring water over it, are recorded by Ganguly as traditions [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Patil, writing in 1952, noticed the collision and resolved it: a popular legend attributes the damage to Aurangzeb, but the historical evidence points to Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. Where a story and an inscription disagree about what happened, the inscription is the better witness — and the story becomes evidence of something else, of how the town explained itself to itself. [ed]

**Inscriptions are claims, not reports.** [ed] A royal inscription is a *praśasti*, a panegyric composed to be believed, and its purpose is to establish something. [ed] A sixteenth-century inscription at the temple calls it the most beautiful in India, which records how the building was regarded rather than settling what it was [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The foundation inscriptions on the porches are the securest dates at Udaypur precisely because they record administrative acts — a commencement, the hoisting of a flagstaff — rather than a virtue [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

**Much of what is known about the town rests on a single source.** [ed] The population figures come from the census as INTACH reports it [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]; the Mughal mosque's sequence comes from one Persian record, as two surveys read it [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110], [src: patil-1952-00388]. A claim carried by one source is not thereby wrong, but it is held differently from one carried by several, and the name attached to such a statement — INTACH, Patil, Ganguly, Tiwari — is doing work. [ed]

Three dates anchor the town's history. Two of them are firm. [ed]

*1059 CE, the foundation.* The porch inscriptions record construction commencing in V.S. 1116 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; Ganguly gives the same year from the epigraphic record [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]; INTACH dates the Udaysagar tank to it [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Three readings converge — though they converge on one inscription, not on three independent witnesses [src: ganguly-paramara-00562].

*1080 CE, the flagstaff.* The same porch inscriptions record the hoisting of the *dhvaja* in V.S. 1137 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Twenty-one years separate the two dates.

*The accession.* The third date is disputed, and the dispute deserves setting out in full, because the two positions rest on different kinds of record and each has something solid under it. [ed] At issue is a single question: in 1059, when the temple was begun, was Udayāditya already the sovereign of Mālava, or a prince building in his own territory? [ed]

**The case for an early accession, 1058 or 1059.** Its strongest evidence is the building itself. [ed] The porch inscriptions show Udayāditya founding a temple, and INTACH shows him digging a reservoir, in the same year, on the scale and in the manner of a king [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The chronologies follow. Patil dates the reign 1058–1087, and his summary passes straight from the joint attack by the Chalukya prince of Gujarat and the Kalachuri king of the Chedis, in which the king lost his life, to the revival of the family's fortunes under Udayāditya, with no interval named [src: patil-1952-00095]. Ganguly, writing of the Un inscription, calls him simply "the Paramara Udayaditya (1059-1086 A. D.)" [src: ganguly-paramara-00568], and one passage of the INTACH survey gives c. 1059–93 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. Circumstance supports them. Ganguly records that when Mālava was overrun by the combined Cauḷukya and Karṇāṭa forces and Jayasiṃha lost his life in battle, Udayāditya proved the saviour of the Paramāra kingdom and recovered it entirely, there being no other prince of the house of Dhārā strong enough to hold the reins of government in that situation [src: ganguly-paramara-00284]. A kingdom in that condition does not wait a decade for a king. [ed]

**The case for an accession around 1070.** Against all of that stands a record of the same year, 1059, pointing the other way. [ed] Ganguly notes that the Panhera inscription, dated V.S. 1116, was issued by Mandalika, a feudatory of Jayasiṃha — which places the paramount throne with Jayasiṃha in the very year the Udaypur record shows Udayāditya building [src: ganguly-paramara-00262]. The manner of the accession tells against an early date as well. Ganguly reports that Udayāditya obtained Mālava by defeating the Gurjara Karṇa, with cavalry supplied by another ruler [src: ganguly-paramara-00279], and notes that Karṇa had not yet assumed kingly power in 1059 [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. A kingdom won from Karṇa cannot have been won before Karṇa was in a position to lose it. [ed] INTACH's numismatic section, working from the coinage rather than from the narrative sources, gives the reign as 1070–1093 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075].

What the two cases share is more than what divides them. [ed] Both have Udayāditya building at Udaypur in 1059 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; both have him ruling at Dhārā a generation later, where Ganguly reads a Shikarpur inscription of 1077 that boasts of a great terror struck into the king of Dhārā as one of the reverses of his reign [src: ganguly-paramara-00293], and records a Jhalrapatan inscription executed in Sam 1143, 1086 CE, in his victorious reign [src: ganguly-paramara-00290]. The disagreement is confined to the decade in which ruling his paternal territory became ruling Mālava. [ed]

Ganguly's own way through is a surmise, and he offers it as one: that Udayāditya held his paternal territory and built at Udaypur in 1059 without yet being sovereign of Mālava [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Its merit is that it leaves both records standing — Jayasiṃha paramount in 1059, Udayāditya building at Udaypur in the same year — where the early dating has to explain the Panhera inscription away, and a strict late dating has to explain away a prince who builds like a king. [ed] On that reading Udayāditya ruled Mālava from about 1070 until the early 1090s [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075], and was already active at Udaypur by 1059 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

It is a reconciliation rather than a proof, and the earlier dating remains defensible: move the accession back to Patil's 1058 or Ganguly's 1059 and nothing in the town's history changes except the title Udayāditya held in the year he began to build. [ed] What is not in doubt is the sequence on the ground: a foundation in 1059, a flagstaff twenty-one years later, and a small place that has been written on ever since. [ed]
