# C1 · Udaypur: A Small Town with a Long Memory

## 1.1 Through the Back Door: An Arrival at Udaypur

At the top of a slope in Udaypur, three hundred metres up from the main road, a stone doorway stands at the end of a street of shops — very old, and very large [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023], [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. Behind it is the absolute masterpiece of the *bhūmija* manner of temple architecture in India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The village around it holds some six thousand people [src: intach-geoheritage-00133].

The doorway is at the back. The temple's main door faces east and has been shut for as long as anyone here can say, so the way in has long been the way round [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024]. Until a few years ago that way was a lane barely six or seven feet wide, climbing between shops of cloth and groceries and sweets, decades old, built — like nearly everything here — out of the stone of the place itself, with hardly any cement or brick in them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00023]. `[UNVERIFIED — editor-supplied: the lane has since been widened, after the account this description rests on; awaiting a source]` The approach has since been widened; the shut east door has not changed. [ed] Nothing about it prepares a visitor for what stands at the end, and the building is not what anyone expects to find this close to Bhopal [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00046].

It answers to two names. In the village it is Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord; in the inscriptions and in the scholarship it is Udayeśvara, after the king who built it [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. By either name it is one of the finest things this region kept from the era of the Paramāra kings [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024].

The street does not stop at the temple. It opens into the rest of the town, and the rest of the town is where a thousand years lie scattered at every step: on both sides of the temple precinct stand houses raised in Paramāra times, built from the arrangement of massive carved stones [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029]. The lanes between them may once have been broad and straight, narrowing century by century as building crept into them, though no surviving record settles what width the medieval streets had [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029].

Few visitors go that far. Since cameras became cheap and phones universal, people stop outside the temple, take their photographs and move on, and the lanes around it stay empty of them [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. What the town holds lies outside those few minutes: the inscriptions its ancestors cut into the walls, the stepwells, the tanks [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. Complaints about the approach went to the shopkeepers, to the three-tier panchayat that governs the village, and to leaders and government functionaries, and for years nothing changed [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00025].

Udaypur lies in Basoda tehsil, on the east bank of the Betwa, fifteen kilometres from the sub-district headquarters at Ganj Basoda and sixty from Vidisha, the district town; the nearest railway station is Ganj Basoda, ninety-three kilometres out from the Bhopal junction [src: intach-geoheritage-00132]. The rivers that matter here are the Kewtan and the Betwa, and the towns of any size are Ganj Basoda and Bareth [src: intach-geoheritage-00132]. It stands among the eastern spurs of the Vindhyachal range, in a district of undulating country that occupies the basin of the Betwa [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00043]. The land around it is agrarian and grows wheat, which goes to the large market at Ganj Basoda [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

The 2011 census counted 6,383 people here, 3,405 male and 2,978 female, in about 1,247 houses; 54.28 per cent of them are literate — 60.70 per cent of the men, 46.94 per cent of the women [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. The village occupies 1,966 hectares [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162].

That is the ordinary description of an ordinary Indian village, and everything else about Udaypur contradicts it. A settlement of six thousand carries a documentary and architectural inheritance that would do credit to a district capital: a temple the Archaeological Survey of India protects as a monument of national importance, the highest grade this country's heritage listing gives [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]; a reservoir dug by an eleventh-century king [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]; a Mughal mosque with a Persian foundation record, standing inside a Paramāra palace [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]; and, scattered through the lanes, the houses of the people who lived beside all of it [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00029].

This is the town as the early 2020s found it: the closed east door, the lane still narrow, the photographs taken from outside [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00028]. It has not always looked like this.

## 1.2 Five Regimes in One Small Compass: An Inventory

Walk out of the temple's rear door and turn in any direction, and within a few minutes you cross five political regimes. [ed] They are not evenly represented. Some left buildings, one left a single object, and the last left a pair of overlapping jurisdictions. The village's revenue boundary encloses 1,966 hectares [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00162], but almost everything that carries this history stands within a few minutes' walk of the temple. A thousand years of Indian history is compressed into a walk of that length, and that compression is what makes Udaypur worth the journey. [ed]

---

The earliest layer is the largest, and it is the Paramāras'. Sanskrit inscriptions cut into the temple's porches while the work was going on record that the Paramāra king Udayāditya founded it, that construction commenced in V.S. 1116 (1059 CE) — the Vikrama era of these records runs fifty-seven years ahead of the Common Era — and that the flagstaff was hoisted in V.S. 1137 (1080 CE) [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The same epigraphic record, read independently, gives the foundation as V.S. 1116 (1059 CE) [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The king's work did not stop at the temple: the Udaysagar tank was dug in the same year, 1059, and a second tank, the Bhujariya, was already there before him and may go back to the eighth or ninth century [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The eleventh-century foundation was laid on ground that was not empty [src: intach-geoheritage-00092].

What he raised is still there to be walked round. [ed] The temple is built entirely of red sandstone and stands in a spacious square courtyard, walled low and fitted with stone seating [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Its plan is a square *garbhagṛha*, a central *sabhāmaṇḍapa*, and three entrance porches facing north, south and east — the east one being the door that is now shut [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. The outer walls are covered with multi-figured relief, Brahmā and Viṣṇu and Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya and Durgā among them, the Śaiva forms predominating; the assembly hall carries a low pyramidal roof, and over the sanctum stands a perfectly balanced seven-storeyed *śikhara* [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. High on it a carved human figure climbs the pinnacles — the architect, or the royal builder himself [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

---

The Delhi Sultanate arrives through inscriptions and through demolition. [ed] An inscription at Udaypur points to the Paramāra dynasty surviving until 1310 CE, at least in the north-eastern part of Malwa — the country around Ujjain and Dhar that the dynasty had ruled — and a later one shows the Delhi Sultanate holding the area by 1338 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]. How the country passed from Paramāra to Sultanate hands in the twenty-eight years between is not something the Udaypur evidence records. [ed]

Between those two dates somebody built inside the temple compound. [ed] Muḥammad Tughluq damaged the attendant temples between 1336 and 1338 CE and raised a mosque at the back of the main temple out of the material of what he had pulled down; two Persian inscriptions on the mosque record the act [src: pande-udayesvara-00391], [src: patil-1952-00387]. The Islamic dates are exact, A.H. 737–39, and they matter, because a popular legend gives the damage to Aurangzeb — three centuries after the stone was actually cut [src: patil-1952-00387]. The compound had held eight minor attendant shrines; the mosque was built from the stone of a dismantled one [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]. The main temple came through without serious damage [src: patil-1952-00387]. It is one of the few temples in Malwa that escaped complete demolition [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. A later mosque, attributed to Sher Khan and dated 1488, stands outside the Moti Darwaza gate, from the century when Malwa answered to its own sultans [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

---

The Mughal century put its most complete building inside the eleventh-century palace. [ed] For its area and for the detailing of its architectural elements, the Shāhī Masjid is arguably the most elaborate mosque in the village [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. It sits at the south-west corner of Raja Udayāditya's palace, on a raised platform, sandstone, with a pillared prayer hall and an open court, on a hundredth of a hectare that is government land today, and it was finished in 1632 CE [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293]. Its Persian inscription records how that came about: a *qāzī*, a judge, named Aulia began the mosque at Udaypur in the reign of Jahāngīr, and both the emperor and the qāzī died with the building half up, so that it was completed under Shāh Jahān, in a year the record gives as A.H. 1041 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]. 

A second reading of the Hijri year makes it A.H. 1042; both convert to 1632. [ed] Beside the mosque stand the remains of a spacious mansion, probably the residence of the local governor [src: patil-1952-00388]. Mosque and palace are catalogued together, the Shāhī Masjid and Mahal, and the stone *jālīs* in them are fine work [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085].

---

The Marathas left no building here at all. What they left is on the object at the centre of the oldest one: the *liṅga* in the sanctum — the aniconic form in which Śiva is worshipped — wears a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia, whose house then dominated this part of central India [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Seven centuries after Udayāditya's flagstaff went up, an officer of an entirely new power was still spending money on this temple, and what he bought was a covering for the god. [ed]

A second Maratha-era object has been reported at Udaypur: a trilingual inscribed slab of 1645 CE at the Qanungo Baoli, said to record Maratha-era administration in the town. [ed] `[UNVERIFIED — editor-supplied: no ingested source carries a slab of that date or description; see the handoff note; awaiting a source]` No inscription of 1645 has been traced anywhere in the record. [ed] 

What the record does hold sits beside a stepwell of very nearly that name. [ed] An inscription found near the Kanongo Baoli, now fixed to the boundary wall of the Badi Mata Mandir east of the village by the Moti Darwaza, runs to six lines, the first two in Nastaliq and the rest in Hindi; it carries a verse of the Qur'an and records the presence at Udaypur of Ibrahim Lodhi, son of Sikandar Lodhi, and it is dated V.S. 1578 (1522 CE) [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00100]. That is a Sultanate record and not a Maratha one, and it stands a century and a quarter earlier than the slab reported. [ed] Until that slab is produced, the Maratha layer at Udaypur rests on the brass plate alone. [ed]

---

The regime that governs the site now is split between two authorities. The temple belongs to the Archaeological Survey of India [src: intach-geoheritage-00089], and ASI-protected monuments of national importance sit at the top of the heritage grading [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00017]. The hill immediately above the village answers to a different department: it is reserved forest, in the revenue records and in the working plan of the Forest Department [src: intach-geoheritage-00090]. Two jurisdictions, and a boundary between them running through the middle of one historical landscape.

Five regimes do not exhaust the village. It also holds the Bijamandal, the Ghadiyalan-ka-makan — what is left of a two-storeyed house contemporary with the temple, probably the dwelling of its time-keeper, carrying an unfinished Sanskrit inscription that opens in praise of the Sun-god; the Bara-khambi, the pillared hall of a ruined eleventh-century temple on the outskirts, ringed with raised seats with backrests under a ceiling of massive stone slabs; and the Pisnari-ka-mandir, an old temple that the village believes was built by a woman out of what she earned grinding flour for the workmen on the great temple [src: patil-1952-00388]. That last is a story the town tells, and it is recorded as one [src: patil-1952-00388]. 

Together the three describe what the five regimes obscure: a working town, with a time-keeper, a hall and a flour-grinder's shrine. [ed] Regimes are what inscriptions record, because recording regimes is what inscriptions are for. [ed] The town they were cut in has to be read out of the buildings nobody troubled to date. [ed]

## 1.3 Rivers Without a Town: The Age of Udaypur

Udaypur looks older than it is.

The Purāṇic material reaches this country as landscape rather than as settlement. The Skanda-Purāṇa names holy rivers in long lists, and among them are the Carmaṇvatī, the Gomatī, the Godāvarī and the Daśārṇa [src: skanda-xiii-00207]. Elsewhere it lists the rivers that come into confluence with Gaṅgā, and the Vetravatī — the Betwa, the river of this region — is one of them [src: skanda-xiii-00301]. That is the register in which this country enters scripture: as water, as a named tract of sacred ground.

What the text does not contain is the town. The volume that covers this stretch runs to 713 passages, and the town appears in none of them — not under its Sanskrit name, not under its Hindi one — and neither does the temple, nor the king. [ed] A Purāṇic Udaypur would have to be found in some other text. In this one the region is the Vetravatī and the Daśārṇa, and nothing is said of a settlement of that name [src: skanda-xiii-00301], [src: skanda-xiii-00207].

The district is another matter. Vidisha's past is a hoary one, rich in archaeology and history, and it is referred to in the Epics, the Purāṇas and in Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical literature; the Heliodoros pillar at Besnagar, the Udayagiri caves of the Gupta period, the temples at Gyaraspur, Udayapur and Badoh are the monuments usually named for it [src: vidisha-gazetteer-1979-00001]. Udaypur belongs on that list by its buildings rather than by its antiquity.

The earliest secure date for the town is 1059 CE, and it comes from the building's own inscriptions rather than from any later tradition [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The ground was not empty before that. Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the ninth century, though its prominence came with Udayāditya [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], and the Bhujariya tank, already standing when he arrived, may belong to the eighth or ninth [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. 

So there was something here before 1059: a settlement, a body of water, fields and the people who worked them. [ed] What there is no evidence for is a place called Udayapura. The eleventh century did not bring occupation to this site. [ed] It brought a name, and a foundation. A foundation is a different kind of event from a settlement: it has a date, a patron and a purpose, and at Udaypur all three survive. [ed]

The long memory of the place is therefore a matter of record rather than of age. [ed] A small town has been written about continuously for nine centuries: in Sanskrit on its porches in the eleventh [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; in the inscriptions that point to the Paramāras surviving to 1310 and the Sultanate holding the country by 1338 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00126]; in Persian on a mosque wall in the fourteenth [src: patil-1952-00387]; on another mosque in the fifteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00085]; in Persian again in the seventeenth [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00110]; in a Sanskrit inscription on a house by the temple, left unfinished [src: patil-1952-00388]; in a Maratha general's donation in the eighteenth [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; and in this century in survey reports that fix structures by GPS and census returns that count households [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00293], [src: intach-geoheritage-00133]. 

The temple is itself the archive: its inscriptions run in a continuous sequence in Sanskrit, Persian and Hindi across eight centuries, from the consecration in V.S. 1137 (1080 CE) to the archaeological restoration in V.S. 1985 (1928 CE) [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. No single institution kept this record. It accumulated because one power after another found the same small place worth writing on. That is what a civilisational record looks like from underneath: not a plan, but an accumulation nobody supervised. [ed]

Nine centuries of record also attract stories that outrun it. [ed] One has Muḥammad Tughluq ordering the temple blown up and the priests defeating the gunpowder by running water over it; another has Aurangzeb ordering its destruction and a dream dissuading him [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. Both are worth having. Neither is a record of what happened.

## 1.4 The King Who Signed His Work: Town, Temple and Tank

Udayāditya did something at Udaypur that founders did not always trouble to do. He signed it.

The town carries his name: Udaypur is named for the Paramāra king Udayāditya, ruler of the Malwa country of central India [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127]. So does the temple — Udayeśvara, the lord of Udaya — which the porch inscriptions record as his foundation [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. So does the water: the Udaysagar tank, dug in 1059 CE, the year the temple's construction commenced [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Of the three namings, two carry dates, and the dates are the same [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

The temple's own name is doubled, and the doubling is instructive. [ed] It is Udayeśvara and it is Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, the blue-throated lord [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: singh-serpentine-00002]. One name belongs to the king, the other to the god. [ed] The god's name is the one the village uses, and the surveys follow the village [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00024], [src: intach-geoheritage-00089]; the king's name is the one the inscriptions kept [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. A name given from above and a name used from below have sat on the same building for nine hundred years, and the town has quietly preferred the god's.

The naming had something to measure itself against. The village already held the older tank, the Bhujariya, dated to perhaps the eighth or ninth century, carrying no royal name at all and built to a very different architectural detail [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. One reservoir had simply been there. The other was made, dated and named inside a single year [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. That is the difference between a place that accumulates and a place that is founded. [ed]

No record says that town, temple and tank were named together, in one act. [ed] What survives is three namings that share a king, a name and, for two of them, a year [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127], [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Taken together they amount to a single act of place-making. [ed]

The chronology complicates that reading. [ed] Udayāditya's accession to the throne of Malwa probably falls around 1070, which would mean that in 1059, with the temple begun and the tank dug, he was not yet the paramount king [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. The town itself argues otherwise: square in plan, ringed by walls of equal length, with the king's palace inside them, it suggests a place where Bhoja's heirs lived — Bhoja being the most celebrated of the Paramāra rulers and Udayāditya's predecessor in the dynasty — and it takes its name from that same Udayāditya [src: tiwari-jhk-en-00078]. What the records leave is narrower than a king naming a capital, and stranger: a man probably not yet sovereign founded a town, a temple and a reservoir, and put his own name on all three [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092], [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. Whatever he was in 1059, he was building as though he expected to be remembered. [ed]

## 1.5 What the Stone Fixes: Three Dates and a Disputed Accession

The temple's dates are cut into its porches. [ed] The mosque's are cut into the mosque. Everything else at Udaypur has to be argued. The difficulty is not that the evidence is thin — for a village of six thousand it is extravagant. [ed] It is that the different kinds of evidence answer different questions, and the questions a reader most wants answered are the ones the stone is least interested in. [ed]

Two stories have attached themselves to the temple, and both are about its destruction: that Muḥammad Tughluq ordered it blown up and the priests defeated the gunpowder by pouring water over it, and that Aurangzeb ordered it destroyed and a dream dissuaded him [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The mosque behind the temple carries two Persian inscriptions, and they date the damage to A.H. 737–39, that is 1336–38 CE, under Muḥammad Tughluq [src: patil-1952-00387]. The legend of Aurangzeb is three centuries adrift of the stone. Where a story and an inscription disagree about what happened, the inscription is the better witness; the story becomes evidence of something else, of how the town explained itself to itself. [ed]

Inscriptions carry their own bias. A royal inscription is a *praśasti*, a panegyric composed to be believed, written to establish something. [ed] A sixteenth-century inscription at the temple calls it the most beautiful in India, which tells us how the building was regarded and nothing about what it was [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]. The foundation inscriptions on the porches are the securest dates here precisely because what they record is administrative — a commencement, the hoisting of a flagstaff — rather than a virtue [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084].

Three dates anchor the town's history. Two of them are firm.

The first is 1059 CE, the foundation. The porch inscriptions record construction commencing in V.S. 1116 (1059 CE) [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]; the same year comes out of the epigraphic record read independently [src: ganguly-paramara-00562]; the Udaysagar tank is dated to it [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. Three readings give 1059, and all three rest on the same inscription [src: ganguly-paramara-00562].

The second is 1080 CE, the flagstaff, recorded on those same porches as the hoisting of the *dhvaja* in V.S. 1137 (1080 CE) [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Twenty-one years separate the two.

The third is the accession, and it cannot be fixed. The question is simple enough to state: in 1059, when the temple was begun, was Udayāditya already sovereign of Malwa, or a prince building like one? [ed]

The building argues that he was king. The porch inscriptions show him founding a temple and, in the same year, digging a reservoir, on the scale and in the manner of a king [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084], [src: intach-geoheritage-00092]. The circumstances argue it too. Malwa had been overrun by the combined Cauḷukya and Karṇāṭa forces, Jayasiṃha had lost his life in battle, and Udayāditya proved the saviour of the Paramāra kingdom and recovered it entirely; no other prince of the house of Dhar was strong enough to hold the reins of government [src: ganguly-paramara-00284]. A kingdom in that condition does not wait a decade for a king. [ed] 

The chronologies follow the building: one gives the reign as 1058–1087 and passes straight from the attack in which the king died — the Chalukya prince of Gujarat and the Kalachuri king of the Chedis, together — to the revival of the family's fortunes under Udayāditya, with no interval named [src: patil-1952-00095]; the Un inscription is assigned to a reign of 1059–1086 [src: ganguly-paramara-00568]; another chronology gives c. 1059–93 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00127].

A record of the same year points the other way. [ed] The Panhera inscription is dated V.S. 1116 (1059 CE), and it was issued by Mandalika, a feudatory of Jayasiṃha, which appears to put the paramount throne with Jayasiṃha in the very year the Udaypur record has Udayāditya building [src: ganguly-paramara-00262]. The manner of the accession tells against an early date as well. Udayāditya obtained Malwa by defeating the Gurjara Karṇa, with cavalry supplied by another ruler [src: ganguly-paramara-00279], and Karṇa had probably not yet assumed kingly power in 1059 [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. A kingdom won from Karṇa cannot have been won before Karṇa was in a position to lose it. [ed] The coinage gives a reign of 1070–1093 [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075].

What the two sides share is larger than what divides them. Both have Udayāditya building at Udaypur in 1059 [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Both have him ruling at Dhar a generation later: an inscription of 1077 from Shikarpur boasts of the great terror struck into the king of Dhar [src: ganguly-paramara-00293], and a Jhalrapatan inscription executed in V.S. 1143 (1086 CE) appears to belong to his victorious reign [src: ganguly-paramara-00290]. The disagreement is confined to the decade in which ruling his paternal territory became ruling Malwa.

The reading that leaves every record standing is that Udayāditya may have held his paternal territory and built at Udaypur in 1059 without yet being sovereign of Malwa [src: ganguly-paramara-00274]. On that reading Jayasiṃha is paramount in 1059, as the Panhera inscription has him, and Udayāditya is at Udaypur in the same year, as his own porches have him; he comes to Malwa about 1070 and holds it into the early 1090s [src: intach-2022-udaypur-00075], having been active at Udaypur for a decade before [src: patil-composite-inscriptions-00084]. Move the accession back to 1058 or 1059 and nothing in the town's history changes except the title Udayāditya held in the year he began to build. [ed]

What is not in doubt is the sequence on the ground: a foundation in 1059, a flagstaff twenty-one years later, and a small place that has been written on ever since. [ed] Nine hundred years of it, in Sanskrit, in Persian and in Hindi, in a village of six thousand people. [ed]
