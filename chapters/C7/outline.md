# Outline — C7 Udayeśvara in Stone: Architecture, Sculpture and Iconography

*Drafted from `research.md` (4,199 bucketed chunks; 123 in the dossier after four targeted top-ups). Editor approves before any prose is written.*

## Through-line

The Udayeśvara is usually introduced as a masterpiece of a style — the finest surviving *bhūmija* temple, built by Udayāditya Paramāra between 1059 and 1080. This chapter argues that the style is the least interesting thing about it. What the building actually offers is an unusually complete statement, in stone, of a particular Śaiva theology and a particular royal ambition: a plan you can walk, a spire built out of multiplied miniature shrines, a wall population arranged by doctrine rather than decoration, and a hillside of unfinished and humble work around it that shows what the same workshop did when it was not building for a king. The chapter moves from the ground up and then outward, ending on the temple's own habit of recording itself — which carries the reader into C8.

## Opening (250–400 words)

The building as it stands: red sandstone quarried from the hill close by, the courtyard with its dwarf wall and stone seats, the spire visible before anything else. Establish the two dates from the temple's own porches.

## Sections

### Section 1 — A plan you can walk  ·  sub-theme: plan, courtyard, porches

The temple as a thing with a route through it: courtyard, dwarf compound wall with stone seating, three porches facing north, south and east, the maṇḍapa, the sanctum with its liṅga and its eighteenth-century brass face. The navadevī lintel.
Evidence: `patil-composite-inscriptions-00084`, `intach-2022-udaypur-00240`, `intach-2022-udaypur-00181`, `pande-udayesvara-00355`
Gaps: no measured dimensions for the temple itself in the dossier — do not give a height.

### Section 2 — The seven-storeyed spire  ·  sub-theme: śikhara, latās, kūṭastambhas

What *bhūmija* means, built rather than defined: four latās, seven bhūmis of five kūṭastambhas in each sub-quarter, the śūrasenaka medallions, the serrated five-pointed edge, the skandha and āmalaka. Why the plan is called stellate and saptaratha, and what the Un temples show by comparison.
Evidence: `pande-udayesvara-00166`, `adhikari-2013-un-00065`, `deva-1969-temples-00241`, `patil-composite-inscriptions-00043`
Gaps: Hardy's reading of the Bhūmija mode is in the KB but thin in this bucket; attribute carefully or leave out.

### Section 3 — The wall and its people  ·  sub-theme: vedībandha, jaṅghā, khattakas, images

The wall read upward as a sequence of mouldings, then as a population: khattikā niches in the kumbha, khattakas in the jaṅghā, buttresses like pillars, the cardinal images — Cāmuṇḍā, Naṭarāja, Andhakāntaka — and the mutilation that makes several unreadable.
Evidence: `pande-udayesvara-00161`, `adhikari-2013-un-00390`, `adhikari-2013-un-00389`, `pande-udayesvara-00280`, `pande-udayesvara-00282`, `pande-udayesvara-00220`

### Section 4 — A theology you can walk around  ·  sub-theme: Śaiva Siddhānta, Śakti on the left

Pande's reading that the sculptural programme follows Śaiva Siddhānta, with Śakti on the left (north) half. Presented as her reading, because the KB labels it inference. Bhoja's own *Tattvaprakāśa* as the dynasty's doctrinal context.
Evidence: `pande-udayesvara-00327`, `pande-udayesvara-00560`, `adhikari-2013-un-00389`

### Section 5 — Outside the walls: a colossus and a flour-grinder  ·  sub-theme: the wider site

The Ravan Tol Naṭarāja, carved from a single rock and never finished into a temple; the two published measurements disagree and the chapter says so. The Pisanhārī kā Mandir, which local telling ascribes to a woman who ground flour — the same craft tradition working for someone who was not a king.
Evidence: `intach-2022-udaypur-00159`, `intach-2022-udaypur-00276`, `pande-udayesvara-00408`, `intach-geoheritage-00491`

### Section 6 — What the stone has survived  ·  sub-theme: inscriptions, damage, the mosque

More than sixty inscriptions; the serpentine slab set into the parapet wall; the mosque on the west side and the gunpowder tradition Ganguly reports; what survives and what does not.
Evidence: `intach-2022-udaypur-00240`, `singh-serpentine-00003`, `singh-serpentine-00002`, `ganguly-paramara-00562`, `pande-udayesvara-00402`

## Bridge out (150–250 words)

The temple records itself: from the foundation inscription to a twentieth-century restoration note. That habit is the subject of C8.
