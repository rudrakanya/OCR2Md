# C7 · Udayeśvara in Stone: Architecture, Sculpture and Iconography

The hill above Udaypur is the quarry, and the temple below it is the same colour [1]. An early description, quoted by Anupa Pande in her monograph on the building, put it plainly: "It is built entirely of a hard fine-grained red sandstone, which is quarried in the hill close by" [1]. Nothing was carted in from a prestigious distance. [ed] The stone of the spire and the stone of the hillside are one material, worked by people who could see the source of it from where they stood. [ed]

What they made from it is, by the reckoning of the surveys that have described it, the largest temple in the *bhūmija* manner that the Paramāras developed in Mālava [2]. Two Sanskrit inscriptions on its porches supply the dates the building gives about itself: construction commenced in 1059 CE (V.S. 1116), and the flagstaff was hoisted in 1080 CE (V.S. 1137) [3]. INTACH's surveyors, reading the same two inscriptions, record the construction as running from 1059 to 1080 [4]. D. C. Ganguly, working from the epigraphic record, gives the foundation as saṃvat 1116, 1059 CE, and adds that a sixteenth-century inscription at the site calls it the most beautiful temple in India [5]. Saarthak Singh, writing from his own fieldwork, dates the temple "around 1080", taking the consecration rather than the commencement as the moment worth naming [6]. The difference is one of emphasis rather than of fact: on any reading the building was some twenty years in the making, and the reader should hold both ends of that span in mind. [ed]

This chapter goes up the building from the ground, then out from it. [ed] The plan first, because it is a thing you can walk; then the spire, which is the style's argument in stone; then the wall, which is populated rather than decorated; then the theology that the population appears to follow; then the hillside around the temple, where the same workshop left a colossus unfinished and a flour-grinder's shrine standing; and finally the surfaces the building has used, for eight centuries, to write on itself. [ed]

## A plan you can walk

The temple stands inside a spacious square courtyard enclosed by a low compound wall, and along the inner face of that wall runs a line of stone seats [3]. The detail is worth pausing on, because it tells you what the courtyard was for. [ed] Seats along a boundary wall are not ornament; they are provision for people who intended to stay a while — to wait, to talk, to watch a rite they were not officiating. [ed]

From the courtyard, three porches offer a way in, facing north, south and east, each opening into a central hall; beyond the hall is the square sanctum [3]. Most of the temples of Udaypur are built of the same red sandstone, raised on plinths that lift them above the ground around them and oriented east, towards the sun [7]. The Udayeśvara's three doorways complicate that simple orientation: a visitor arriving from the town does not have to circle the building to find the way in [3].

The doorways were treated as occasions for sculpture in their own right. [ed] On the south porch, the first pedimented niche from the left holds a seated Gaṇeśa with his usual attributes — axe, lotus, sweets and broken tusk — and between the five projecting niches of that doorway are four recessed ones [8]. At the sanctum door, the lintel panel carries the *navadevī*, the nine forms of the goddess [9]. A worshipper stepping through that door passed beneath a full statement of divine feminine power before reaching what was inside. [ed]

What is inside is a large *liṅga*, and its covering has its own history: a brass face plate presented in 1775 by Khande Rao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia [3]. Seven centuries after the flagstaff went up, a Maratha officer was still investing in the fabric of this building. [ed] The hall is roofed with a low pyramid, and the exterior walls carry multi-figured reliefs of the Hindu pantheon — Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Gaṇeśa, Kārttikeya and Durgā among them — with Śaiva forms predominating [3].

The whole arrangement sits on a terrace. INTACH's surveyors record a lofty *jagatī* of 65 by 65 metres, carrying a red sandstone building that opens east, with ruined sub-shrines and a pavilion around it; they describe the sequence inside as sanctum, vestibule, closed hall, three porches and a low flat-roofed assembly hall [4]. That is the only measured dimension this chapter gives for the temple, and it comes from a survey rather than from the building's own record. [ed]

INTACH's surveyors, documenting the temple in 2022, recorded what a visitor sees from inside the hall: the ceiling, a sculpture of Śiva on the maṇḍapa pillar, the liṅga, and what they called the balance of refined ornamentation against the building's overall grandeur, its handcrafted mouldings curving upward as the śikhara reaches its pinnacle [9]. They added, in passing, that the iconography of the temple is a subject of research in itself [9]. That is an honest thing for a survey to say, and this chapter takes it as a warning: what follows is a description of a programme, not a decoding of it. [ed]

## The seven-storeyed spire

The spire is the reason the building is famous, and it rests on a principle that has to be seen before it can be described. [ed] Four *latās* — vertical bands running up the face of the śikhara — divide it, and at the base of the three on the north, south and west sits a prominent sculpted medallion set within a large *caitya* window, the *śūrasenaka* [10]. Between each pair of latās, in each sub-quarter, rise seven *bhūmis* of five *kūṭastambhas*: miniature pyramidal spires, each perched over a pillaret, five rows of them vertically and seven horizontally [10].

That is the *bhūmija* idea. [ed] Instead of one curved tower carrying a single rhythm to the top, the spire is assembled out of many small shrines, diminishing as they rise, so that the eye reads both the whole and its parts at once. [ed] The kūṭastambhas decrease in size towards the *skandha*, the shoulder of the spire, and the edges of that shoulder articulate the star-shaped plan of the temple below, the serrated edge running to five points in each sub-quarter [10]. The same serration is echoed in the corrugated rim of the heavy *āmalaka* that crowns it [10]. The plan is not a conceit added at ground level: it is restated at the top of the building. [ed]

Above the porch front, on either side of the *śukanāsa*, sit five-storeyed celestial mansions, carved as miniature architecture in their own right [11]. On the north-west face of the śikhara Pande noticed something odder: a human figure that appears to be climbing the spire [10].

Swati Mondal Adhikari, surveying the Bhūmija temples of Mālava, sets out the grammar the Udayeśvara follows [12]. Bhūmija temples occur on both *pañcaratha* and *saptaratha* plans; the pañcaratha temples carry three vertical rows of kūṭastambhas in each quadrant, the saptaratha five [12]. Horizontally, those rows rise in five- or seven-storeyed stages, and temples reaching nine are vanishingly rare — she names only two, the Siddheśvara at Nemawar and the Undeśvara at Iñjoli [12]. Read against that scheme, the Udayeśvara's five rows and seven bhūmis place it at the developed end of the type rather than at its experimental beginning [10], [12].

Comparison is what makes the point stick. [ed] Krishna Deva, writing on the temples at Un, observed that they belong to a cognate style and have essentially the same plan and śikhara design as the Udayeśvara, but are simpler: their walls are plain but for images in the three *bhadra* niches, divided into two registers by an ornate median band [13]. He also noted what ties the group to Udaypur in time rather than by style alone — an inscription of King Udayāditya found in one of them, the temple known locally as Chaubara Dera No. 1, the only *pañcāyatana* temple at the site [13]. The survey of Madhya Bharat's monuments places the Un cluster earlier still, dating Chaubara Dera No. 1 to roughly 1000–1055, the peak of Bhoja's reign, and Mahākāleśvara No. 1 to about 1055–1070, under Jayasiṃha [14]. The Udayeśvara was not a solitary masterpiece. [ed] It was the most elaborate member of a family of buildings going up across Mālava over three reigns, and the same royal name appears on more than one of them [13], [14].

Time has not treated the family equally. D. R. Patil's survey found the central hall of Mahākāleśvara I gone entirely, its śikhara still showing the clusters of miniature spires common to Paramāra temples; Ballāleśvara reduced in proportion and carrying a later dome where its śikhara had been; and a Gupteśvara so named because the shrine itself now lies underground [15].

The family extends further. [ed] The same survey lists, alongside the Udayeśvara, the Siddheśvara temple at Nemawar with its tall and perfectly preserved śikhara, the larger Sas Bahu temple at Gwalior of 1093 CE, the Hindola Toraṇa at Gyaraspur, the Gadarmal temple at Badoh, and the structural groups at Un [16].

## The wall and its people

A Nāgara wall is a sequence of mouldings before it is anything else, and Pande's account of this one reads upward like a builder's list. [ed] At the base is the *vedībandha*: its *khura* decorated with a *ratnapaṭṭī*, a band of diamond pattern; above a knife-edged *karṇikā* rests the *kumbha*, ornamented with niches shaped like miniature shrines — *khattikās* — that house gods and goddesses, with a *manibandha* across the middle and lotus petals on its shoulder [17]. Above that, between two karṇikās, runs the *kalaśa*, a torus moulding carrying a *gagārapaṭṭī*; and above the kalaśa begins the *jaṅghā*, the upper wall, set with the large ornamented niches called *khattakas*, housing more gods and goddesses [17]. On the *maṇḍovara* the buttresses take the appearance of pillars, terminating upward in capital-shaped mouldings and carrying a heavy ornamental fillet, a deep recess, and the roof-shaped *chādya* [17].

Strip the vocabulary away and the effect is this: the wall is not a surface with statues on it but a structure of small architectural frames, each one occupied. [ed] The building houses its gods the way a town houses its people. [ed]

Who lives where is not random. [ed] In the *bhadra* niches of the jaṅghā — the projections at the cardinal points — Adhikari records Cāmuṇḍā in the north, Naṭarāja in the south and Andhakāntaka in the east [18]. On the śūrasenakas of the north and east faces of the śikhara she found further images of Naṭarāja, extremely damaged [18]. Krishna Deva noted the same cardinal trio — Cāmuṇḍā, Naṭarāja and Śiva Tripurāri — recurring in the related temples of the group, and treated it as a minor diagnostic trait of the style [13]. A worshipper who had walked around one of these buildings would have known what to expect on the walls of the next. [ed]

The lesser niches are more various [19]. Adhikari lists, among the images in the miniature niches of the kumbha moulding, Śiva, Gaṇeśa, the Vāmana incarnation of Viṣṇu, Kubera, and the goddess on her buffalo mount [19]. In one niche on the south side stand two men and a woman in *dvibhaṅga*; she suggests they may represent a king with queens, or else Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā, and marks the identification with a question mark [19]. Pande's survey of the same walls finds Kaumārī enshrined in a khattaka between the west and north latās, and Kārttikeya at the bottom of the west latā; below the Cāmuṇḍā figures on the first buttress stand images of Umā-Maheśvara, one pair standing and one seated with Nandi below [20]. On the south-west side of the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* she records a standing male deity, a form she identifies as Kiraṇākṣa Śiva, and Yama [21]. Agni appears holding a book, which Pande connects to his Vedic standing as both sacrificial fire and exalted deity, and to the daily fire rituals that *caryā* required of Śaiva Siddhānta practitioners [22].

One image on the śukanāsa repays the attention Pande gives it: a Gaurī in whom the iguana that is Śriyā's mount appears in the place of an attribute in the lower right hand, and who, instead of being seated on a corpse, holds one in her upper hands and seems almost to be hurling it; Pande suggests it symbolises the enemy who is to be destroyed [11].

Some figures are still legible as performances rather than types. [ed] Pande describes one Śiva whose hips are twisted into a pose she says would not be credible even to acrobats, finds its parallel in an eleventh-century Paramāra image from Ambarnath, and suggests it may be the *kaṭibhrānta karaṇa*, a movement of the hip drawn from the dance manuals [23]. The hedge is hers, and it is the right one: identifying a carved pose with a named step in a text is an argument, not an observation. [ed]

Much of the wall can no longer be read at all. [ed] Of the Cāmuṇḍā images, Pande notes that most of the hands are mutilated, so the attributes they held cannot now be identified with certainty [24]. On the *tilaka* of the north-east gūḍhamaṇḍapa she counts six images, arranged as four Śiva figures hemmed between two Śakti figures, with a seated Cāmuṇḍā — emaciated, seated on a corpse — enshrined above a dancing Śiva [24]. The damage is not incidental to this chapter's subject: it sets a limit on how much of the programme anyone can claim to reconstruct. [ed]

## A theology you can walk around

The arrangement of those figures is where description ends and interpretation begins. [ed]

Pande reads the sculptural programme as a statement of Śaiva Siddhānta [25]. In Pande's reading, the system is dualistic, requiring the worship of both Śiva and Śakti; of the five components of the *śuddha adhvā*, Śiva and Śakti are the two principal ones; and because the female principle is *vāmā*, that which is on the left, Śakti dominates the left half of the building [25]. She points to the distribution as her evidence: Śakti images occur on the left side of the sanctum — the northern and north-eastern quarters — and on the gūḍhamaṇḍapa and its tilaka, with Cāmuṇḍā and Mahiṣāsuramardinī among them [25]. She notes too that the Paramāras of Vāgaḍa placed Cāmuṇḍā in the northern quarter of their temples, and suggests the Udayeśvara follows the same habit, with prominent Cāmuṇḍā images in the north and on the northern latā [26].

Whether a medieval worshipper experienced the building as a diagram of the *śuddha adhvā* is not something the stone can settle. [ed] What can be said is that the temple belonged to a dynasty with a serious doctrinal investment [27]. The Udayeśvara rests on a Śaiva sect deriving from Śaiva Siddhānta; its builder Udayāditya and the Paramāra rulers generally were followers of it; and Bhoja Paramāra, the most celebrated of the line, was a Śaiva philosopher in his own right, author of the *Tattvaprakāśa*, one of the authoritative texts of the school [27]. That text is concerned with the practical application of philosophy — the purification of the soul, the stages by which bondage gives way to liberation, and the possibility that a mortal might come to share the company of the lord, *śivasāyujya* [27].

Read against that, the wall's insistence on Śiva in many forms is less a catalogue than a repetition, and the repetition is in keeping with the temple's Śaiva affiliation, as Adhikari also observed of the same building [19]. Pande argues, further, that the left-and-right distribution encodes the doctrine; that remains her reading rather than a statement the building makes [25]. It is a good one. It is not a fact the building states. [ed]

## Outside the walls: a colossus and a flour-grinder

Walk east out of the village and the workshop's ambition becomes visible in a form nobody finished. [ed] On the outskirts, at Ravan Tol, lies a colossal Śiva carved from a single rock, shown in his dancing form on the dwarf Apasmāra, the figure of ignorance [28]. INTACH's surveyors, who recorded it twice in the same volume, describe the god with six hands, holding a trident, a *ḍamaru* and attributes they could not identify [29]. It is a monolith, dated to the eleventh century, and the state archaeology department holds it as a protected sculpture, graded in the survey's highest class and recorded as being in good condition [30].

The two INTACH descriptions do not agree on its size. One gives approximately nine metres in length by three in width, with a height of one and a half metres [29]; the other, in the same survey's catalogue, gives 8.2 metres by 4.2 [28]. The discrepancy is worth stating rather than smoothing: even measured field data, taken by one body in one campaign, needs checking before it goes into print. [ed]

Around the sculpture stand the beginnings of a structure that was never completed — stone pillars, going nowhere [28]. The surveyors record a local belief that Udayāditya had planned a second temple here, on the scale of the Nīlakaṇṭheśvara, and that the work was abandoned after his death [28]. That is tradition, not testimony, and it is offered here as such. [ed] But the unfinished pillars are real, and whatever story explains them, they mark the limit of a building programme that the temple in the village represents at its height. [ed]

The other building worth walking to makes the opposite point. [ed] Near the Udayeśvara stands a small, plain and unostentatious temple of later date, the Pisanhārī kā Mandir — the temple of the woman who ground flour [31]. Pande reports the local account that a grinder saved, gradually, until she could have it built [31]. Inside is a seated Umā-Maheśvara image: Umā on Śiva's lap to the left, their mounts the lion and Nandi below, Bhṛṅgī dancing between them, and Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya on the frame beneath — finely chiselled hair, proportioned bodies, now coated with the grime and soot of the lamps burning in front of it [31]. Pande draws the contrast herself: royalty patronised temples, and so, on a smaller scale, could a grinder of flour, with Brahmins performing the rites in both [31].

The workshops that cut this stone have left their names elsewhere in Mālava, if not here. [ed] On the Un temples, the structural blocks preserve the signatures of the medieval *śilpis*: on Chaubara Dera No. 1 the names Ratanu, Bala, Ilapala, Barālaba and Supata, two of them carved with such calligraphic care on the main hall beams and pilaster capitals that the survey reads them as master builders; on Mahākāleśvara No. 1, a completely different set, which the same survey takes as evidence of another generation or guild [32]. The medieval sculptor, in the wider account that survey gives, worked to rigid iconographic texts and covered temple walls with stylised animal forms and multi-armed divinities, a tradition it traces back through Gupta motifs — the vase-and-foliage, the *kīrtimukha*, the *caitya*-window tracery — and finds *in situ* at Udaypur among other sites [33].

## What the stone has survived

The Udayeśvara's most unusual feature is not carved but written [9]. INTACH's surveyors counted more than sixty inscriptions on the building, a number that outstrips any other historic monument they recorded in the area [9]. Singh describes the temple as an epigraphic archive with a continuous sequence in Sanskrit, Persian and Hindi spanning eight centuries, from the consecration in saṃvat 1137 to an archaeological restoration recorded in saṃvat 1985 [6].

His own find shows how much remains to be noticed. [ed] During fieldwork he identified an inscription that had gone unremarked in the scholarly literature: a slab of pinkish sandstone, seventy centimetres high, engraved in Nāgarī and built into the outer parapet wall where the courtyard projects to the south [34]. It had been in plain sight, in the enclosure wall of one of the most-described temples in central India, for nine centuries. [ed]

The building's survival has attracted stories, and they should be handled as stories. [ed] Ganguly records the tradition that Sultan Muhammad Tughluq, after the conquest of Udaypur, ordered the temple blown up; that gunpowder was heaped in and around it; that the priests secretly ran water over the charges and rendered them useless; and that the Sultan, taking this for a miracle, revoked the order — on which occasion one of the halls in the corner of the courtyard, used for reading the Vedas, was converted into a mosque [5]. He records a second tradition, that the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb halted here on his way back from the Deccan, was amazed at the temple's beauty, and ordered its destruction until the deity appeared to him in a dream [5]. Both are traditions about how the temple survived, and both are evidence of how the town explained its own good fortune; neither is a record of what happened. [ed] Ganguly's own summary is the soberer statement: this is one of the few temples in Mālava spared complete demolition [5]. The mosque itself is still there, and Pande's plates include its *miḥrāb* on the western side [1].

What the temple could not escape was the slower damage: the hands broken from the Cāmuṇḍā images, the Naṭarāja figures on the śikhara worn past reading [24], [18]. And yet the fabric holds. [ed] The Nīlakaṇṭheśvara — the Udayeśvara — remains the largest and most significant of the historic structures INTACH catalogued at Udaypur, and it is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India [35].

## Bridge

The building has been described here as architecture: a plan, a spire, a wall, a programme. [ed] But the last section points at something description cannot contain. [ed] A temple carrying more than sixty inscriptions is not only a made object; it is a surface that successive centuries chose to write on — in Sanskrit when it was founded, in Persian when a mosque was set in its courtyard, in Hindi by the time the archaeologists arrived [9], [6].

Those texts are not marginal to the architecture. [ed] The two dates on which this chapter opened come from inscriptions on the porches of the very building they describe [3]. The lintel over the sanctum door announces the goddess in nine forms before a worshipper reaches the liṅga [9]. A grammatical diagram in the shape of a knotted serpent sits in the parapet wall, waiting nine centuries for someone to read it [34].

The next chapter takes up those texts on their own terms: what they say, who wrote them, in which languages and scripts, and what a building that has been recording itself for eight hundred years can tell a historian that its stones alone cannot. [ed]
