# Outline: C11 — Built Heritage: Fortifications, Mosques, Shrines and Stepwells

**Scope note:** Owns other built structures' EXISTENCE and historical/architectural description (forts, mosques, shrines, stepwells) -- excludes the main temple entirely. C17 owns the SAME structures' conservation status specifically; a structure can appear in both, but this chapter should not narrate conservation history.

**Thesis:** Beyond the celebrated main temples of central India, the historical landscape is dense with fortifications, mosques and tombs, stepwells, and subsidiary shrines that together reveal both the practical statecraft of pre-modern rulers and the successive religious and political transformations of the region.

## Fortifications: Design, Construction, and Military Function
- Classical Indian treatises such as Bhoj's Yukti-kalpataru and Samarangana-sutradhara laid out detailed prescriptions for forts, distinguishing natural (akritrima) from artificial (kritrima) types and specifying moats, ramparts (vapra/prakara), and bastions (attalaka). (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-80; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-86)
- Town ramparts were built to precise specifications—height, width, brick proportions, and tapering profile—with bastions spaced at regular intervals (about 30 cubits) and gateways elaborated with gopuras shaped like a lizard's mouth. (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-86; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-87; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 29-30; Bhojadeva (attrib.), Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra: An Ancient Treatise on Architecture, vol. 1, pp. 205-217)
- At Udaypur hill fort, bastions were built in semi-circular, rectangular, and circular forms using massive iron-clamped sandstone blocks, with peepholes for defense, and a 4-metre-thick wall enclosing the hill on three sides. (Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 98-99; Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 90-98; Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 87-90)
- Bastion shape evolved over time from square to round, a change attributed to the need to deflect cannon fire after the introduction of artillery. (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-80; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 48-49)
- Madhya Bharat's forts generally consisted of massive battlemented walls with bastions, gates (including a 'Hawa Paur' or wind gate at the highest point), and internal structures for garrisons and military stores. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 66-76)
- The Modi Pattan site preserves the archaeological traces of a Paramara-era town rampart, square bastions, and grid-planned streets, later destroyed in the Muslim period. **[THIN — single source]** (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 48-49)

## Mosques, Tombs, and Islamic-Period Structures
- At Mandu, the Great Mosque (Jami Masjid) and the tombs of Hoshang Shah and Mahmud Khilji form a grand, axially planned ensemble, with the marble tomb of Hoshang noted for its chaste simplicity. (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 130-134; Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 45-51)
- At Chanderi, the Jama Masjid was built in the Mandu style, and the town preserves numerous Muslim tombs, including the Shahazadika Roza and a madrasah converted from an earlier tomb. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 107-110)
- At Gwalior, Man Singh's palace with its glazed-tile mosaics and the adjoining Gujar Mahal represent a distinctly Hindu architectural idiom that later influenced Mughal palace design at Agra and Fatehpur Sikri. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 86-91)
- Near the Udayeshvara temple at Udaypur, secular and religious structures survive including the Bijamandal (a time-keeper's house), the Bara-khambi hall, and the Shahi Masjid, begun under Jehangir and completed under Shah Jahan in 1632 (AH 1041), alongside a mahal believed to have housed the local Mughal governor. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 114-119)
- Muhammad Tughluq is recorded to have damaged the attendant shrines of the Udayeshvara temple complex between 1336-38 and erected a mosque behind it using material from the demolished shrines, per Persian inscriptions. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 87-90)
- Some secondary sources frame Islamic-period construction across the region, including forts, maqbaras, and mosques such as the Quwwatul Islam mosque in Delhi, as built using material and wealth obtained from destroyed Hindu and Jain temples. (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 90-91; Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 86; Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 87-88)

## Stepwells, Tanks, and Water Architecture
- Central India developed distinctive traditional water structures—including baolis (stepwells), kunds (stepped ponds), and tanks—that served both practical and ceremonial/religious functions from roughly the 2nd to 8th centuries CE onward. (Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 76-77; Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 77-78)
- At Udaypur, a three-sided baoli on the slopes of Pindi Hill has twenty steps and incorporates reused temple column fragments dated to the 7th-8th century, while a Baoli inside the fortification wall held a strategic position on the eastern side. (Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 111-114; Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 98-99)
- Sanskrit architectural texts classify stepwells into four types (Nanda, Bhadra, Jaya, Vijaya) by number of mouths and terraces, and prescribe standard well and tank forms and dimensions for town planning. (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 108-109; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-108)
- Madhya Bharat records 211 old tanks, ghats, and wells, with the most elaborate stepped wells—featuring built-in apartments and inscriptions—found at Mandu, Chanderi, and other sites, reflecting a later medieval/Muslim-period refinement of well architecture. (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 66-80; Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 107-110)

## Subsidiary Shrines and Temple-Complex Appendages
- Beyond the main shrine, temple complexes frequently included planned ensembles of minor shrines, most commonly the panch-ayatana (five-shrine) plan with four corner shrines, and were often bound by enclosure walls (prakara) or common podiums. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 102-109)
- Multiple-shrine arrangements, typical of the Deccan, could combine two, three, or as many as nine interlinked shrines sharing a mandapa, exemplified by the Jambulingeshvara temple at Badami and the Keshava temple at Somnathpur. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 102-106)
- The Udayeshvara temple complex originally had eight attendant shrines surrounding the main structure within a walled courtyard with four gated entrances, though only a couple of ruinous subsidiary shrines survive today. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 87-90)
- At Udaypur, secondary human interventions such as small enclosed room structures and a 'Female Mazar' were built later by reusing scattered pillar, beam, and lintel fragments from older structures. **[THIN — single source]** (Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, pp. 114-121)

## Site-Specific Ensembles as Case Studies
- Padhavli-Mitaoli and the Bhuteshwar/Bateshvara valley preserve a dense assemblage of ruined Shiva and Vishnu shrines alongside Jain shrines, illustrating a once-thriving multi-sectarian sacred landscape now mostly abandoned. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 96-99)
