# Outline: C10 — Words in Stone: Inscriptions, Languages and the Serpentine Scimitar of Letters

**Scope note:** Owns epigraphy as a discipline: script, paleography, the serpentine scimitar inscription, translation and dating cruxes. Other chapters (C06, C07) may cite an inscription's CONTENT briefly for a historical claim without re-doing this chapter's epigraphic analysis.

**Thesis:** The Udaypur serpentine scimitar inscription and its sister sarpa-bandha diagrams at Ujjain, Dhar, and Un reveal how Paramara-era temple epigraphy fused Nagari paleography, Sanskrit grammar, and visual/magical design into instruments of royal and religious power, and their study exposes the interpretive cruxes—damaged surfaces, contested readings, uncertain dates—that define epigraphy as a discipline.

## The Discovery and Physical Description of the Udaypur Inscription
- A previously unpublished inscription was found on a rectangular sandstone slab set into the parapet wall of the Udayeśvara temple at Udaypur, engraved with a serpent's knotted body formed of Sanskrit letters plus grammatical endings. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), p. 1)
- The slab is in Nagari script on pinkish sandstone 70cm high, and physical evidence—its inscribed face turned inward while its ornamental balustrade faces outward—shows it was reset out of its original orientation during modern restoration campaigns by the Gwalior State and the Archaeological Survey of India. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), pp. 1-2)
- The visual-textual composition is known in scholarly literature as nagabandha or sarpabandha, and while flaking has damaged some characters, the record remains legible enough for interpretation, with paleography comparable to other late-11th-century local records. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), pp. 1-2)

## Naming the Genre: The 'Serpentine Scimitar of Letters'
- An accompanying five-line inscription identifies the diagram as the varnanagakripanika, a 'serpentine scimitar of letters,' a compound term that draws together sound, image, and ritual efficacy. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), p. 1)
- The Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum edition of the related Dhar chart describes the same design as a scimitar or dagger formed by the combination of letters and a snake, with the snake's head as the dagger's blade, fourteen vowels in the head, and twenty-five squares of consonants (sparsas) arranged below. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 138-142)
- The identical composition at Ujjain is further described in dedicatory verses as a 'unique magical sword' (siddhasiputrika) associated with the Paramara kings Udayaditya and Naravarman. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), p. 1)

## A Network of Serpent Inscriptions: Ujjain, Dhar, and Un
- Three related sarpa-bandha inscriptions are known from the Mahakalesvara temple at Ujjain, from Dhar, and from Un, all sharing the same subject of the Nagari alphabet and grammatical terminology. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 144-146)
- The Ujjain inscription's second chart is made of two intertwined serpents, probably male and female, displaying the personal terminations of ten lakaras together with sixteen dhatu-pratyayas. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 144-146)
- The Un inscription, found on a wall of the dilapidated Chaubera Dera temple, is an alphabetical chart with some conjugational terminations engraved on the body of a single serpent, in Nagari characters closely resembling those at Ujjain and Dhar, undated but similar in style. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, p. 150)
- Comparative paleography across these related inscriptions allows the Udaypur example to be placed in the late-11th century, associated with the Paramara kings Udayaditya and Naravarman. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), p. 1)

## Script and Orthography: Reading Nagari in the Paramara Corpus
- Across dozens of Paramara-era copper-plate and stone inscriptions, editors consistently identify the script as Nagari and the language as Sanskrit, with recurring orthographic habits such as using the sign for v to denote b, doubling a consonant following r, and confusing the dental and palatal sibilants. (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 51-52; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 224-225; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 169-170; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 74-75; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 62-64)
- Individual letterforms are frequently ambiguous or confounded by scribes and engravers—for example n, v, and l appearing almost alike, or bh being mistaken for h—which paleographers must reconstruct through comparison of multiple occurrences within a single record. (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 421-422; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 452-454)
- Some inscriptions, like the Nagpur Museum stone of Naravarman, show idiosyncratic engraving errors, such as a redundant chisel stroke making ma appear as mva, requiring later editors to weigh earlier readings by Sircar against fresh impressions. (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 175-176; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, p. 175; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 175-176)

## Praśasti as Genre: Language, Meter, and Purpose
- Many Paramara inscriptions take the form of praśasti, laudatory verse compositions recounting dynastic origin myths and royal deeds, as seen in the Kiradu stone inscription of Somesvara, which opens with obeisance to Suryajna and narrates the Paramara's mythic origin from the fire-altar of Vasishtha on Mount Abu. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 480-481)
- Other records, such as the Piplianagar and Bhopal copper-plate inscriptions of Mahakumara Harischandra, follow a customary structure of opening invocatory verses to Siva, a central donative statement, and closing exhortations to future kings, showing the formulaic conventions of the donative-praśasti genre. (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 241-242; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 233-234)

## Dating and Translation Cruxes
- No source in the evidence establishes with certainty the exact original date of the Udaypur serpentine scimitar inscription itself; its late-11th-century date is inferred only by comparison with the dated Ujjain and Dhar analogues associated with Udayaditya and Naravarman, not from an internal date on the Udaypur stone. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, A., A Serpentine Scimitar of Letters (the varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā inscription at Udaypur), p. 1)
- Scholars disagree over authorship and status claims within these texts, as in the debate over whether the poet Bandhu composed the Dhar verses and whether Naravarman could properly be called a king (mahibhuj) at the time, a dispute unresolved between Sastri, Lele, and Sircar. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 138-142)
- Translation history itself becomes a source of textual crux: the Nagpur Museum inscription of Naravarman was translated into English, then German, then re-edited multiple times, with later editors like Kielhorn noting that the English translation sometimes aids understanding of difficult verses more than the German. **[THIN — single source]** (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, p. 175)
