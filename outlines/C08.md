# Outline: C08 — Udayeśvara in Stone: Plan, Structure and Style

**Scope note:** Owns the temple's structural/architectural form (plan, courtyard, gateways, Bhumija/Nagara sikhara, typology). Assumes the founding (C07) as settled fact, cited briefly if needed, not re-argued. Does not cover sculpture/iconography (C09's) or inscriptions as texts (C10's). Clean split from C11 (built heritage) -- no overlap.

**Thesis:** The Udayeśvara (Nīlakaṇṭheśvara) temple at Udayapur is the grandest surviving expression of the Bhūmija mode of Nāgara architecture, its stellate plan, sapta-ratha sanctum, and multi-tiered śikhara embodying the geometric principle of the rotating square that defines Bhūmija design and distinguishes it from Shekhari and Dravida traditions.

## Typology: Placing the Temple within Nāgara Architecture
- The temple belongs to the broader Nāgara tradition, whose curvilinear śikhara and cruciform, Bhadra-and-Ratha plan distinguish it from Dravida and Vesara modes. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 1, pp. 221-223; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 1, pp. 301-306)
- Within Nāgara, the temple is classified as Bhūmija, a form defined by Kramrisch as 'both square and circular' in plan, generated by rotating the square of the Prāsāda around its centre so its corners inscribe a circle. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 102-103)
- Hardy identifies Bhūmija as emerging in the eleventh century, later than Shekhari, and as the preferred temple type of Malwa and Seunadesha for over two centuries, recognizable by radiating cascades of kuta-stambhas between out-thrusting bhadras. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 122-123)

## The Ground Plan: Stellate Geometry and the Sapta-Ratha Sanctum
- The Udayeśvara is stellate on plan, comprising a sapta-ratha sanctum, a vestibule (antarāla), and a hall (maṇḍapa) with three porches. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 96-98)
- The temple's plan generates its acute-angled rathas between Bhadras and karṇas by rotation within a circumscribing circle, a Bhūmija planning principle also seen in comparable Malwa temples such as the Mahākāleśvara at Un. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 102-103; Some Paramāra Temples, pp. 22-25)
- The temple stands on an extensive platform-terrace, originally approached through a stepped entrance flanked by large Shaiva dvara-palas. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 96-98)

## The Elevation: Bhitta, Pīṭha, Maṇḍovara and the Vertical Divisions
- Bhūmija temples of this school are organized vertically into bhitta, pīṭha, maṇḍovara and śikhara, each articulated with a sequence of mouldings (khuraka, kumbha, kapotapālīkā and others) that mark the transition from base to curvilinear tower. (Some Paramāra Temples, pp. 30-31; Some Paramāra Temples, pp. 22-25)
- The maṇḍovara's jāṅghā is decorated with pilasters on acute-angled rathas, while the cardinal Bhadras hold large niches for divine images framed by parikarmas and crowned by udgamas -- a decorative scheme comparable in related Bhūmija shrines. **[THIN — single source]** (Some Paramāra Temples, pp. 30-31)

## The Śikhara: Bhūmija Superstructure and Its Miniature Towers
- The śikhara is decorated with seven vertical and five horizontal rows of miniature shikharas in each quadrant, an elaboration praised as the grandest specimen of Paramāra architecture, begun in 1059 and completed in 1080. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 96-98)
- Kramrisch's detailed plate description confirms the śikhara's arch rises unbroken in the four quarters from flat Latās with Gavākṣa lattices to the Āmalaka, with seven Bhūmis of five Śṛṅgas each in every sub-quarter, each course carried on faceted square shafts. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 103-105)
- Hardy notes that Bhūmija shikharas are structurally constrained to particular bhūmi counts (commonly five or seven) matching the number of projections, a rule the Udayeśvara's own seven-bhūmi, seven-projection scheme exemplifies within the wider Bhūmija corpus. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 122-124)
- Sculptured medallions set in bold chaitya-dormers at the base of the central ratha of the śikhara form a conspicuous feature of the monument on each face. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 96-98)

## Interior Spaces: Maṇḍapa, Antarāla and Garbhagṛha
- The maṇḍapa's pillars show the characteristic mediaeval transition from a square base through an octagonal section to a circular top, with carved chapels and a frieze of Gaṇas marking the shift from static to dynamic form. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 108-109)
- The doorway to the garbhagṛha bears guardian deities on the pedya and mother goddesses, gods, and personified planets on the lintel and sub-lintels, with a square ceiling set within two squares adorned by a lotus and kīrtimukhas. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 83-87)
- The garbhagṛha's śivaliṅga is now covered by a later brass mukha-sheath donated in 1775 CE, a post-medieval addition distinct from the temple's original medieval fabric. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 83-87)

## Regional Comparisons: The Udayeśvara among Malwa's Bhūmija Temples
- Nearby Bhūmija temples at Un, including Chaubara Dera No. 1 and Mahākāleśvara, share the style but are more ruined and generally lack the surviving superstructure that distinguishes the Udayeśvara. (Temples of India, pp. 96-99; Ganguly, D. C., History of the Paramāra Dynasty, pp. 273-276)
- The Siddheśwara Temple at Nemawar has a loftier śikhara with nine vertical rows of turrets yet lacks the Udayeśvara's grace and proportion, while the Amareshwara Temple at Onkara Mandhata, dated 1063, offers the closest comparable plan and design. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 96-99)
