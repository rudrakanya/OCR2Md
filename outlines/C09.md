# Outline: C09 — Gods on the Walls: Sculpture, Iconography and Meaning

**Scope note:** Owns sculptural and iconographic content: which deities, what attributes, what the imagery means. Hands off to C08 for structural context and to C12 for the deeper ritual/philosophical meaning behind a depicted deity or symbol.

**Thesis:** The sculptural program of the Indian temple is not mere decoration but a structured iconographic language, in which the placement, posture, attributes and gender of gods, goddesses and celestial beings on the walls encode cosmological hierarchy, mythic narrative and the devotee's ritual ascent toward the divine presence in the sanctum.

## Reading the Gods on the Walls: Why Iconography Matters
- Because Indian temple walls teem with multi-armed, zoomorphic and composite deities that can bewilder outsiders accustomed to purely anthropomorphic divinity, systematic iconographic identification—by attributes, posture and hand-gesture—is necessary to read the temple's imagery at all. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, p. 12)
- Iconographic manuals organize deities by tabulated attributes precisely because visitors to cave and structural temples otherwise find it difficult to identify who is depicted. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 12-15)
- Every hand pose (mudra), pedestal, weapon and object carried by a deity carries fixed meaning, and mastery of this symbolic vocabulary was a formal requirement of the sculptor trained in the Shilpa texts. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, p. 24)

## The Architecture of Divine Placement
- Images on temple exteriors are not scattered but are organized in belts and registers around the body of the Prasada, ornamenting it while also expounding its meaning, so that the wall becomes a legible ascent toward the finial above. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 12-15; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 12-16)
- On the cardinal projections the deity closest to the sanctum's main god is normally placed, following either family relationships (Parvati, Ganesha and Karttikeya around Shiva) or a hierarchy of emanation radiating outward from the central image, though this scheme is not universal and sometimes the wall images instead resemble a gathering of celestial beings rather than a strict hierarchy. (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 62-63; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 12-17)
- In some temples, such as the 7th-century Parasurameshvara temple at Bhuvaneshvara, the descending images within the gavaksha arches of the tower spine even trace an explicit emanatory sequence in the Shaiva Siddhanta manner. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 62-64)
- The stances given to the images—samabhanga, abhanga, tribhanga, atibhanga—and the doctrine that divine bodies are eternally sixteen years old, weightless and breath-filled, reflect textual prescriptions for how the sculpted body should embody divinity rather than ordinary human anatomy. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 12-20; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 12-19)
- **[→ see C08]** How this iconographic layering fits within the temple's larger sequence of mandapas, garbhagriha and superstructure is structural context belonging to the chapter on temple architecture.

## The Great Gods: Shiva, Vishnu and Their Company
- The Vedic gods Rudra and Vishnu fused with non-Vedic cults in the early centuries AD to become Shiva and Vishnu, the two great sectarian gods (alongside the Goddess) whose images occupy temple sanctums and animate the walls, while lesser Vedic gods like the eight dikpalas survived only as corner guardians and Surya alone retained many dedicated temples. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 52-53)
- Shiva appears on temple walls in his teaching form as Dakshina-murti—seated facing south as Jnana-Dakshina-murti, Vyakhyana-Dakshina-murti expounding the Sastras, or Vina-dhara-Dakshina-murti as music teacher—each identifiable by specific hand gestures and objects. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 68-69)
- The marriage of Shiva and Parvati (Kalyanasundara-murti) is a recurrent temple subject, rooted in the Mahabharata legend of Daksha's curse and Sati's self-immolation, and is sculpturally rendered with Vishnu and his wives standing in as Parvati's kin giving away the bride. (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, p. 67; Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 67-68)
- Shiva is also worshipped in the abstract form of the linga, sometimes with faces (mukha-lingas) representing the fivefold Sadashiva emerging from the unmanifest, an image type rooted in early Shaiva sects such as the Pashupatas and Kapalikas. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, p. 56)
- On Vaishnava temples in Rajasthan the cardinal niches display family deities such as Narasimha, Trivikrama, Varaha, Vishnu or Harihara, with the Regents on corner buttresses and Ganesha, Surya, Chandra, Revanta, Brahma and Parvati on auxiliary offsets. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 45-47)
- Deogarh's Gupta-era Dashavatara temple carves Vishnu as Sheshashayi and depicts gajendramoksha and the penance of Nara and Narayana, along with early narrative panels from the Ramayana and Krishna-lila. **[THIN — single source]** (Temples of India, pp. 19-26)

## The Goddess and the Feminine Divine
- Sculpture treats the celestial female form—apsaras, surasundaris, Sakti—as a body of embodied intellect rather than ordinary flesh, built of movement and breath like the gods themselves. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 51-53; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 51-55)
- Mahishamardini, absorbed from a non-Aryan deity fond of wine, flesh and the tiger-skin into the Aryan pantheon as slayer of demons like Mahisha, appears with eight or four arms bearing javelin, wheel, conch and other identifying attributes, and often occupies the central shrine of a Panchayatana group. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 78-79)
- The Sapta-matrikas (Seven Divine Mothers), carved with distinct attributes such as Varahi, Brahmi, Vaishnavi, Chamunda and Maheshvari, are recurrent lintel and doorway images flanking Ganesha and Shiva. **[THIN — single source]** (Some Paramāra Temples, p. 18)
- River goddesses Ganga (on her makara) and Yamuna (on her tortoise) were carved flanking shrine doorways from the Gupta period onward, symbolizing purity and devotion as qualifications for entering the sanctum. **[THIN — single source]** (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 80-85)
- In Jain temple sculpture the Yakshini Chamundi resembles the fierce Hindu Chamunda, shown emaciated and skeletal as a destructive Mother figure, illustrating how the same iconographic type of the terrible goddess crossed sectarian lines. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 97-99)

## Celestial Attendants, Guardians and the Symbolism of Threshold
- Flying Vidyadharas, carved singly on buttresses or in pairs with consorts in wall recesses, carry weapons and instruments and represent the topmost register of celestial beings above the main gods, as seen at the Duladeo Temple, Khajuraho. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 97-99)
- Dvarapala guardian figures flank temple doorways bearing the weapons of the god they serve—Shiva-dvarapalas, or those of Brahma, Vishnu, etc.—marking the threshold between the mundane world and the divine presence within. **[THIN — single source]** (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 26-27)
- The temple door itself is treated as a symbolic image: its proportioned jambs, sill and lintel carry guardian figures occupying a quarter of the door's height, and the lotus carved on the threshold symbolizes the universe through which the devotee passes toward the divine image. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, p. 26; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, pp. 26-27)
- At the Udayeshvara temple, minor celestial beings—yakshas with fan-shaped headdresses and six hands, four-armed flying gandharvas, and naga figures in namaskara-mudra—populate the brackets and capitals according to a canonical hierarchy of inferior celestial beings recorded in the Samarangana-sutradhara. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 79-82)
- Even minor astral and mythic imagery, such as Rahu, Ketu and Saturn depicted on doorway lintels, and the tutelary Yaksha couple Gomedha and Ambika flanking the Tirthankara Neminatha in Jain shrines, shows the iconographic program extending to planetary and minor tutelary deities alongside the great gods. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 79-82; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 2, p. 110)
- **[→ see C12]** The deeper ritual and philosophical significance underlying why particular deities and symbols were chosen for these positions—their meaning within Saiva Siddhanta or comparable theological systems—is treated separately.

## Narrative and Erotic Imagery on the Temple Wall
- Alongside static devotional icons, temple walls carry narrative friezes drawn from the Mahabharata, Ramayana and the Puranas (compiled from the Gupta period onward), such as Deogarh's scenes of Ahilya's release from her curse, Lakshmana mutilating Surpanakha, and Krishna-lila episodes of the infant Krishna with Nanda and Yashoda. (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 52-53; Temples of India, pp. 19-26)
- Erotic mithuna and copulating couples, proliferating on temple walls from around the 9th century onward, likely combined several meanings at once—magical and apotropaic protection, fertility symbolism, and courtly celebration of kama as one of life's four goals—rather than reflecting a single Tantric explanation. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 62-65)
