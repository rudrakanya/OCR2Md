# Outline: C12 — Ritual and Knowledge: Śaiva Traditions and Intellectual Worlds

**Scope note:** Owns the intellectual/ritual/philosophical thought-world (Saiva Siddhanta, tantra, worship practice) as ideas and practice. Hands off to C09 for the visual/sculptural representation of a given deity or symbol.

**Thesis:** Śaiva traditions in this period fused a sophisticated philosophical system—Śaiva Siddhānta, grounded in Sāṃkhya categories but reworked as a theistic path to union with Śiva—with tantric ritual practice and royal patronage, making temples like Udayeśvara physical embodiments of a whole cosmology of bondage and liberation.

## Śiva and the Growth of Śaiva Sects
- Śiva, originally the fearsome Vedic Rudra born of Prajāpati and Ushas (so named because he wept, 'rud'), was gradually Aryanised and rose to rival Vishnu in importance, coming by the early centuries AD to be worshipped as a supreme, transcendent and immanent God. (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 59-60; Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, p. 56)
- Numerous Śaiva sects developed—Pāśupata (traced to the sage Lakulisha), Kāpālika, Kālamukha, Vīraśaiva and others—with the Pāśupatas regarded as the oldest known school and the esoteric Kāpālika Bhairava cult representing an early proto-tantric current from which Puranic orthodoxy distanced itself. (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, p. 56; Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 59-60)
- Worship of Śiva in phallic (linga) form has ancient roots, possibly traceable to the Indus civilization and to Vedic references to a 'phallus-god' (Shishna-Devatā), and became a central, if debated, mode of representing the god as simultaneously formless and all-form. (Gupte, R. S., Iconography of the Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, pp. 60-61; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, p. 99)

## The Linga and Tantric Emanation
- The linga was understood as an abstract symbol of the unmanifest transcendent, placed in the garbha-griha as an axis of concentrated power, with mukha-lingas and multi-faced lingas developing from the 5th century to express the theological concept of the fivefold Sadashiva emerging from the unmanifest into manifestation. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, p. 56)
- Tantra was not a separate sect but a pervasive approach across Indian religions, articulated through a 7th-century-onward corpus of Tantra texts (many from Kashmir and Nepal), emphasizing emanation, guru-led initiation, and yantras as diagrams embodying a hierarchy of deities mapped onto the body, psyche and universe. **[THIN — single source]** (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 53-55)

## Philosophical Foundations: From Sāṃkhya to Śaiva Siddhānta
- Classical Sāṃkhya philosophy, attributed to Kapila, provided an atheistic, dualistic scheme of 25 tattvas emanating from purusha and prakriti through buddhi, ahamkara, manas, the senses and the elements, conceiving cosmic creation as a process of successive emergence mirrored in the human body and psyche. (Hardy, Adam, The Temple Architecture of India, pp. 51-52; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-108)
- Śaiva Siddhānta adapted this scheme theistically: the 25th principle, puruṣa, is replaced by jīva or paśu, whose nature resembles Śiva's own but is obscured by mala, karma and māyā, and liberation (śivasāyujya) is achieved only through Śiva's grace rather than through karma alone as in Sāṃkhya. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-108; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-105; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-104)
- The pāśas (bonds) binding the soul are classically fourfold or reducible to three—mala, karma and māyā—with māyā understood not as illusion but as a positive creative power of god, a point distinguishing Śaiva Siddhānta sharply from Advaita Vedānta and Kashmir Śaivism. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 112-121; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 112-119)
- Souls (paśus) are classified into three types—vijñānākala, pralayākala and sakala—according to which bonds still afflict them, with the most purified attaining deity-like status as Vidyeśvaras. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-109; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 112-120)

## Bhoja's Tattvaprakāśa and the Northern Śaiva Siddhānta School
- Bhoja Paramāra, a renowned ruler and Śaiva philosopher, authored the Tattvaprakāśa, the foundational text of the Northern school of Śaiva Siddhāntins, paralleling the role of the Śivajñānabodham of Meyakandeva for the Southern school. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-104; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-105)
- The Tattvaprakāśa, elaborated through the commentaries of Śrīkumāradeva (Tātparyadīpikā) and Aghoraśivācārya (Vṛtti), describes god's five functions—creation, maintenance, destruction, obscuration and grace—and lays out the śuddha adhva of tattvas: Śiva, Śakti, Sadāśiva, Īśvara and Vidyā, distinguished by the balance of jñānaśakti and kriyāśakti. (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 112-120; Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 112-122)
- The text claims to derive its authority from the Śaivāgamas, naming works such as Kāmika, Yogaja, Cintya, Kāraṇa and others, of which the first ten are classed as dualistic Śaivāgamas. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-104)

## Ritual Practice, Architecture and the Cosmic Body
- Temple building itself was conceived as a tantric and philosophical act: the temple was seen as a three-dimensional model of the cosmic body of god (vāstupuruṣa), embodying the traditional parallelism of piṇḍa (body) and brahmāṇḍa (cosmos), so that understanding a temple required understanding both Śilpaśāstra and Āgamaśāstra. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, p. 99)
- The architect (sthapati) was himself required to be a tāntrik, versed in the śāstras and ritually and morally qualified, since architecture (vāstuśāstra) was regarded as an applied branch of Tantra and of the Atharvaveda. (Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 1, pp. 18-22; Kramrisch, Stella, The Hindu Temple, vol. 1, pp. 18-21)
- The Udayeśvara temple, built by Udayāditya Paramāra under the patronage of a dynasty devoted to this Śaiva sect, stood as a physical reminder of the Śaiva Siddhānta path from ordinary bondage to companionship with the lord. **[THIN — single source]** (Pande, Anupa, The Udayeśvara Temple: Art, Architecture and Philosophy of the Śaiva Siddhānta, pp. 103-104)
- Sculptural evidence from sites such as Un confirms that Śaivism was the predominant religious current among the Paramāras, alongside a continuing presence of Vaiṣṇavism and Jainism. **[THIN — single source]** (Some Paramāra Temples, pp. 43-44)
