# Outline: C13 — Fields, Wells and Markets: Agriculture, Economy and Crafts

**Scope note:** Owns economic activity: agriculture, irrigation, trade, land grants, temple endowments. May reference C03's soil/geology for context (see C03's note) but is the owner of agricultural discussion itself.

**Thesis:** Across medieval and early modern India, agriculture, irrigation, land tenure, temple endowments, fairs, and craft production formed an interlocking economic system in which temples, states, and rural communities together organized land, labor, and trade.

## Land, Soil and the Rhythms of Cultivation
- Cultivation in the Malwa tract followed a fixed seasonal calendar of ploughing, sowing, weeding and reaping tied to the monsoon, with local proverbs encoding agronomic wisdom about ploughing timing. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 100-101)
- Wheat, gram and jowar dominated the cropping pattern, with wheat commanding half the cropped area by the early 1970s, while the discontinuance of poppy cultivation freed land for food crops. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 99-100)
- Oilseeds such as linseed and sesamum, along with minor fibre and tobacco crops, formed a secondary but persistent component of the agricultural economy. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 100-104)
- Soil fertility was maintained through crop rotation, fallowing, mixed cropping and the large-scale distribution of urban and rural compost and green manure. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 100-109)
- Post-independence agricultural modernization introduced improved and hybrid seed varieties for wheat, gram, maize and jowar, administered through a dedicated Agriculture Department. (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 100-108; Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 232-233)
- Reclamation of vast tracts of culturable waste land, especially land overrun by the kans weed, was pursued through mechanized clearance during the Five Year Plan era. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 93-94)

## Water for the Fields: Wells, Tanks and Canals
- Irrigation played a historically minor role in this region owing to fertile soil and abundant rivers, with irrigated area never reaching even one percent of cropped land before rapid post-1951 expansion. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 94-95)
- Wells were the largest source of irrigated area until the late 1960s, after which canals became the dominant and expanding irrigation method, while tank irrigation remained comparatively negligible. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 94-95)
- Organized irrigation development began under princely rule after the famines of 1898 and 1900, when the Gwalior State created an Irrigation Department and advanced loans for well construction. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 95-96)
- By the late 1960s the district had 67 irrigation tanks and seven canal systems, including the large Jajone tank project completed at a cost of about Rs. 20 lakhs. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 96-98)
- Block Development programmes distributed improved seed and fertilizer while also constructing and repairing hundreds of irrigation wells and tanks and reclaiming thousands of acres for cultivation. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, p. 196)
- State loan schemes such as those under the Land Improvement Loans Act and Agriculturists' Loans Act financed well construction, bunding and other agricultural inputs from princely times into the planning era. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, p. 118)

## Land Grants, Revenue and Tenancy
- Copper-plate grants from the Paramara period record the formal mechanics of royal land donation, including detailed physical descriptions of the inscribed plates themselves as historical artifacts. (Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 21-22; Trivedi, Harihar Vithal (ed.), Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, Vol. VII, Part 2: Inscriptions of the Paramāras, pp. 29-30)
- A normative Paramara-era text prescribes graduated land assignments—hereditary brahmadeya grants to priests and scholars, revocable one-generation grants to tax-paying cultivators, and administrative land to court officials—alongside state encouragement of cultivation through provision of grain, cattle and gold. **[THIN — single source]** (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 79-85)
- Mughal-era revenue assessment under Akbar's Todarmal system classified land by soil quality and fixed the king's share at one-third of commuted produce value, collected by officials such as the Amil and Kanungo. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, p. 204)
- Under later Gwalior administration, the exploitative ijaredari farming system gave way to more regularized settlements, though early surveys lacked soil classification until reforms began in 1893 and 1908. (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 204-205; Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 204-206)
- Post-independence land reform abolished the jagirdari and zamindari systems, replacing them with an elective Patel-based revenue collection system and codified tenant protections under the Madhya Pradesh Land Revenue Code. (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, p. 209; Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 211-215; Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 211-212)
- Rural labor arrangements included partnership cultivation (batai), attached workers, and casual daily wage labor, studied by a state committee on minimum agricultural wages in 1959. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 211-217)
- By 1961 the large majority of Scheduled Caste and Tribe workers were engaged in agriculture as cultivators or laborers, with most cultivating households holding land from government or private sources. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 303-305)

## Temples as Landowners and Agrarian Managers
- Temples became major landholders through royal grants such as Sarvamanya (whole villages) and Devadana (tax rights), with rulers like Rajaraja the Great endowing lands across many villages to support temple functions. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 42-43)
- Temples managed distant agricultural holdings both through direct cultivation supervised by appointed officers such as Kankanippar and through tenant leasing arrangements that specified owner-cultivator produce shares, which could be reduced during droughts. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 42-44)
- Temple charitable endowments, financed by their agricultural income, supported education, hospitals with staffed pharmacies, and religious recitation, showing land revenue funding a broader institutional economy. (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 16-17; Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 16)
- **[→ see C09]** In the modern era, temple-controlled agricultural land in Tamil Nadu under the HR&CE department has generated far less realized revenue than its scale would suggest, a controversy over temple asset management rather than a matter of the medieval agrarian system itself.

## Fairs and Seasonal Markets
- The Hardwar spring festival provided a fixed annual rhythm for a major trade route, drawing merchants dealing in shawls, dry fruits, brass goods, and functioning as India's largest horse fair, attracting East India Company buyers of cavalry remounts. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 69)
- Regional melas such as Pushkar and Sonepur combined livestock trading in cattle, camels and horses with retail stalls serving whole families, redistributing goods between city and countryside. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 117)
- Pilgrimage fairs like the Pandharpur Wari generated substantial seasonal retail economies, including trade in children's garments, blankets, and moneylending services catering to traveling pilgrims. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 63-67)

## Artisans, Guilds and Trade Networks
- Temple-linked craft traditions such as Gujarat's applique work and Sankheda furniture, and Bengal's Baluchari silk and Dhokra Damar metal sculpture, drew both artistic inspiration and market demand from religious imagery and temple patronage. (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 55; Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 57)
- Madhya Pradesh's craft towns—Bhedaghat marble carving, Khajuraho metalwork, Tikamgarh bell-metal casting and Budni Ghat woodcraft—illustrate how riverine and temple geography shaped localized artisan economies producing both religious and utilitarian goods. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, pp. 55-56)
- Gwalior's ceramic tradition traces to the fifteenth-century tilework of the Man Mandir fort and has continued into modern commercial production of archaeological reproductions. **[THIN — single source]** (Patil, D. R., The Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat, pp. 61-63)
- Sironj, on the Deccan-to-Agra trade route, was described by Abul Fazl as a prominent business city (bandar) in 1593 and by Tavernier in the seventeenth century as crowded with merchants and artisans famed for fine muslins and chintzes. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 154-161)
- The wider Shilpa Shastra tradition classified sixty-four arts and crafts as a formal body of knowledge governing artisanship, from sculpture to weaving, linking handicraft production to temple-derived cultural frameworks. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 54)
- Handicraft production remained economically significant for tribal and rural households well into the twentieth century, contributing over thirty percent of household income in surveyed communities despite being treated as secondary to farming or wage labor. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 57)
- District-level retail trade in the twentieth century remained dominated by small proprietors and urban shopkeepers, with limited female participation, alongside state trading mechanisms for procurement of wheat, rice and sugar. (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 181-183; Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, p. 164)
