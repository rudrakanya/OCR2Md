# Outline: C15 — Stories of Udaypur: Festivals, Folklore and Oral Histories

**Scope note:** Owns oral/folk tradition specifically -- festivals, legends, myths -- as content, distinct from C01's written/scriptural tradition and C18's present-day register.

**Thesis:** Beyond its stones and inscriptions, Udaypur lives in oral memory: the annual fairs that still mark its ritual calendar, the legends attached to its temple and ruins, and the folk traditions of the wider Paramara-era Malwa region that surface in stories, proverbs and songs about kings like Bhoja -- a layer of tradition distinct from, but as revealing as, the written record.

## The Ritual Calendar: Fairs and Festivals of Udaypur
- A seven-day Shivaratri fair is held at Udaipur in February/March, alongside other regional religious fairs such as the Sankrant fair at Rajghat Barat, the Ramlila fairs at Basoda Mandi and Gyaraspur, and the Navratri fair at Ghatwar. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 87-90)
- At the Nilkantheshwar temple in nearby Sironj an annual fair is held in the Ganpuri Bagh, and local memory holds that the temple's lingam was positioned so that the sun's first ray strikes it at both solstices. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 87-90)
- Village festivities woven around these fairs include Ramlila performances before Dussehra and folk dances such as raslengi, chachad and ral, accompanied by dholak, manjeera, dhap, jhanjhar and ghungru. **[THIN — single source]** (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh District Gazetteers: Vidisha, pp. 87-90)

## Fairs as Living Institutions: Why Melas Mattered
- Across India, melas historically fused four purposes at once -- dharmic, commercial, pleasurable and spiritual -- allowing rural and urban goods to circulate and letting whole families, including children, encounter folk artists and travelling theatre. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 117)
- Large fairs such as those held on pilgrimage occasions doubled as major livestock and trade markets, showing that a town's religious calendar was inseparable from its economic life -- a pattern into which Udaypur's own fairs fit as a smaller-scale regional example. **[THIN — single source]** (Singh, Sandeep, Temple Economics (Arthavyavasthā of Mandirs), Volume I, p. 69)
- **[GAP — UNSUPPORTED]** No source in the evidence gives a documented economic account of Udaypur's own Shivaratri fair specifically, so its scale and trade relative to larger melas like Kumbh or Hardwar remains unestablished here.

## Legends of the Temple and the Town's Name
- A local legend recounts that a king named Balahara of Karnata, cured of a snake in his stomach through his queen's cunning, built ninety-nine temples in gratitude at a place called Un -- and the same story, in near-identical form, circulates at both Lalitpur and Udaypur in Madhya Pradesh, making it impossible to treat as reliable history for either place. **[THIN — single source]** (Some Paramāra Temples, p. 5)
- The persistence of this migratory legend across multiple sites suggests Udaypur's own foundation stories belong to a shared regional stock of temple-origin tales rather than to a uniquely local memory, a caution the chapter must state plainly. **[THIN — single source]** (Some Paramāra Temples, p. 5)

## King Bhoja and the Paramara Kings in Folk Memory
- King Bhoja, whose Paramara dynasty also produced Udayaditya (associated with Udaypur's own temple), became in his own lifetime a magnet for folk attraction, remembered not only through inscriptions and eulogies but through proverbs, plays and story-cycles such as the Bhoja-prabandhas. (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 154-156; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 154-163)
- A famous Malwi proverb -- "Kathe Raja Bhoj ne kathe Gangu Teli" -- preserves in distorted folk form a memory of Bhoja's real rival Gangeyadeva Kalachuri, showing how historical events could survive centuries of oral transmission in altered shape. **[THIN — single source]** (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 154-157)
- Folk anecdotes about Bhoja -- such as the story of the clay cooking-stoves revealing the poverty of his subjects, or the queen's private jibe that led him to interrogate his courtiers -- present him as a ruler whose wisdom and humility were tested by ordinary people and animals alike, down to a legend of a bear who recognized him as the rightful "giver" king of Dhara. (Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 154-172; Raja Bhoj aur Parmarkālīn Malwa (Raja Bhoja and Paramāra-period Malwa), pp. 154-170)

## Collecting the Oral Record: Interviews and Local Testimony
- Recent heritage documentation of Udaypur, such as the Geo-Heritage survey, explicitly built its account on personal interviews with local villagers to recover the legends attached to the site's hills, water bodies and the giant sculpture known locally as Ravan Tol, alongside earlier gazetteers and archaeological reports. (Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, p. 46; Geo-Heritage of Udaypur, Vidisha District, Madhya Pradesh: The Third Eye, p. 45)
- A grassroots band of local enthusiasts and outside visitors organized monthly visits to gather and publicize Udaipur's oral and architectural heritage, treating the town's own residents -- families like the self-described Ghadiyali family, who recount how bells and gongs once signaled temple worship times from the Bija Mandal building -- as primary carriers of local memory. (Udaypur: Jagta Hua Kasba (Udaypur, A Town Awake); Udaypur: Jagta Hua Kasba (Udaypur, A Town Awake))
