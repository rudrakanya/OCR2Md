# **Part I. Site overview**

Confidence: **Documented**

Udaipur (also Udaypur) is a historic town in Basoda Tehsil, Vidisha district, Madhya Pradesh. It sat on an important north–south trade route. The settlement dates to at least the 9th century. It took its present name and rose to prominence under the Paramara king Udayaditya in the 11th century. The main monuments are protected by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI).

## **Key monuments**

**Udayeshvara (Nīlakaṇṭheśvara) Temple**

A Shiva temple and the only surviving royal temple of the Paramaras. Its inscriptions place construction between 1059 and 1080 CE. It is regarded as a leading example of the Bhumija style, with a red-toned curvilinear tower (shikhara) covered in miniature spires. Visitors widely report that its interior stays cool in summer.

**Monolithic Nataraja sculpture**

Reported as roughly 9 m long and 4 m wide, carved from a single rock. Social-media posts describe it as the world's largest Nataraja sculpture.**\[V23\]**

**Tughluq Shāhī Mosque**

A small 14th-century mosque, now partly ruined, west of the temple.

**Shahi Mahal and Masjid**

A 17th-century Mughal governor's residence and congregational mosque, east of the temple.

**Qanungo Baoli**

A stepwell on the eastern outskirts with a trilingual inscription dated 1645 CE.

# **Part II. Historical timeline**

Confidence: **Documented**

**By 9th c. CE**Settlement exists on the regional trade route, near the older centre of Vidisha (Besnagar).

**c. 1055**Death of Raja Bhoja. Chaulukya and Kalachuri invasions follow.

**1059–1080**Udayeshvara Temple built under Udayaditya, per its inscriptions.

**c. 1070–1093**Reign of Udayaditya. Some passages give c. 1060\.**\[V2\]** Town takes the name Udaypura.

**1229 and 1232**Sanskrit pillar inscriptions of King Devapala record land grants. His treasurer Dhamadeyava is named.

**1323–24**Votive record on an elephant sculpture names Brahmins Madhava and Keshava.

**1336–39**Two Arabic–Persian inscriptions at the Tughluq mosque, reign of Muhammad bin Tughluq.

**8 Jan 1338**Sanskrit record (VS 1394\) of a temple procession (jātrā) led by Harirāja.

**1377, 1446**Pilgrim records on the eastern porch doorjambs.

**1616–1632**Shahi Mahal and Masjid built, from Jahangir's reign to Shah Jahan's (1026–1041 AH).

**13 Jan 1645**Qanungo Baoli completed, with its Arabic–Persian–Sanskrit inscription.

The town remained an important urban centre from the 14th to the 16th centuries. Under the Mughals it was a qasba in sarkar Chanderi of the Malwa subah.

# **Part III. Before the Paramaras**

## **Setting and eras**

Confidence: **Reported**

The settlement lay on the eastern edge of Avanti (Malwa), where the land transitions toward Chedi (Bundelkhand). It sat on feeder lines of the north–south route linking Besnagar and Mathura to the Deccan. The session describes it as a caravan staging post.

| Era | Role described in the session |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Maurya and Śuṅga (3rd–1st c. BCE)** | Agrarian satellite of Besnagar, influenced by Bhāgavata and Buddhist centres near the Heliodorus Pillar. |
| **Gupta (4th–6th c. CE)** | Exposure to advanced stone-cutting through the Udayagiri Caves. Possible grain supply to monastic centres. |
| **Aulikara / Huna transition** | Frontier realignment (no detail given). |
| **Gurjara-Pratihara (8th–10th c.)** | Revenue pocket under local feudatories. Bhillasvamin (Sun) worship at nearby Bhilsa. |

The economy relied on monsoon millets and oilseeds along seasonal streams of the Betwa basin. Generations of local stone artisans and blacksmiths are said to have carried skills forward into the Paramara period.

The session states the Udaipur Prashasti describes Udayaditya expanding, fortifying and renaming an existing settlement. The Wikipedia-backed point is that Udaypur's history reaches back to at least the 9th century. The specific wording attributed to the Prashasti needs checking.**\[V25\]**

*Sources: itihaas.ai (Vidisha); Wikipedia (Udaypur; Paramara dynasty).*

## **Links with the Udayagiri Caves**

Confidence: **Verify before use**   See V26

**Stone-working lineage**

Both sites use horizontally bedded Upper Vindhyan sandstone. The session proposes that Gupta-era carving skills at Udayagiri (e.g., the Varaha panel, Cave 5\) passed to the guilds who later quarried for Udaipur.

**Solar ideology**

Udayagiri means "sunrise mountain", and its shrines have documented solar alignments. The session suggests the east-facing, elevated Udayeshvara Temple echoes this idea.

**Architectural lineage**

Udayagiri Caves 1 and 6 are early prototypes of a sanctum with a pillared porch. The sanctum–vestibule–hall sequence later appears in full structural form at Udaipur.

**Serpent imagery**

Udayagiri has early Nāga iconography (including Vishnu on Ananta, Cave 13). The session links this to Udaipur's later serpent grammar charts.

*Sources: Vajiram & Ravi; Wikipedia; ASI Facebook; Heritage Unfolded (YouTube); Academia.edu.*

## **Indigenous communities**

Confidence: **Verify before use**

The forests of the Vindhyas and the Malwa plateau were home to ancestral Bhil, Gond and Sahariya communities. Maurya- and Gupta-era texts refer to forest polities (Āṭavika Rājya) in central India.

* Communities practised hunting, gathering, shifting cultivation and honey collection.

* The session describes earth-and-clay check dams on seasonal streams and local knowledge of shallow water tables.

* It proposes that Paramara expansion involved treaties with or subjugation of forest chieftains, and later employment as forest guards and haulers. The quoted "subdue the wild" phrases are unverified.**\[V25\]**

*Sources: District Vidisha website; IGNCA ("The Gond of Madhya Pradesh"); History Atlas.*

# **Part IV. Udaipur under the Paramaras**

## **Overview and rulers**

Confidence: **Reported**

After Bhoja's death (c. 1055), Malwa faced joint invasions from the Chaulukyas of Gujarat and the Kalachuris of Chedi. Udayaditya, a relative of Bhoja,**\[V1\]** restored the kingdom and made Udaipur an eastern stronghold and showpiece. The session calls it a sub-capital rivalling Dhara.

| Ruler | Reign (as given) | Association with Udaipur |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Udayaditya** | c. 1070–1093 | Built the Udayeshvara Temple and gave the town its name. Died around 1093, apparently of natural causes; no record gives the cause.**\[V3\]** |
| **Naravarman** | c. 1094–1133 | Credited with additional Sanskrit inscriptions, hymns and grammar charts. |
| **Devapala** | c. 1218–1239 | Issued land grants recorded in 1229 and 1232\. |

### **Urban form (interpretive)**

The session describes a fortified administrative zone with treasury and palace, an outer civic zone, the temple compound at the centre, and a network of tanks and stepwells. These specifics lack archaeological citation.**\[V16\]**

### **Strategic economy**

| Aspect | Description |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Location** | Eastern frontier facing the Kalachuris of Tripuri and the Chaulukyas, on the North India–Deccan route. |
| **Agriculture** | Fertile black cotton soil supplying grain revenue to the treasury (kosa). |
| **Trade** | Merchant guilds (shreni); the treasurer Dhamadeyava linked to temple funding. |

*Sources: Wikipedia and Wikiwand (Udayaditya); Unacademy; rajput.digital; Puratattva.*

## **Administration and daily life**

Confidence: **Verify before use**

The session presents the temple as a civic registry and describes these roles. The office titles are standard in early medieval Indian records. Their specific functions at Udaipur are not tied to named inscriptions.

| Role | Function described |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Pattakila** | Village headman. Collected revenue, settled property disputes, linked town and king. |
| **Mahajanas** | Council of elders. Managed utilities and stepwells and enforced decrees. |
| **Rajapurushas** | Royal officers from Dhara, e.g., the treasurer (bhandagarika) Dhamadeyava. |
| **Śreṣṭhis and vaṇiks** | Bankers and merchants who financed public works and managed grain stores. |
| **Shilpins and sutradharas** | Sculptors and master builders with high social standing. |
| **Malakaras** | Gardeners supplying daily garlands in return for tax-free temple land (devadaya). |
| **Devadasis** | Temple performers with institutional roles and property. |

Daily life is described as centred on seasons, agriculture and temple activity, with festival processions (yatras) and joint donations benefiting Shaiva, Vaishnava and Jain ascetics.

## **Architecture**

### **The Bhumija style**

Confidence: **Reported**

Bhumija is a sub-school of Nagara architecture developed in Malwa under the Paramaras. Its tower carries rows of miniature spires between central vertical bands. The session calls Udaipur the style's crowning achievement.

### **Building roles**

| Role | Responsibility |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Sthapati** | Chief architect. Plan (vastu-purusha-mandala), proportions, orientation. |
| **Sutradhara** | "Holder of the cord". Transferred the design to stone and supervised cutting. |
| **Shilpin** | Master sculptor of icons, friezes and ceiling panels. |
| **Takshaka** | Stone-cutter and mason. Quarried and dressed blocks. |

### **Structural components**

Confidence: **Verify before use**   See V28

The session gives each element an engineering role. The terms are real; the load-bearing explanations are interpretive.

| Component | Description | Engineering role claimed |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Jagati and adhisthana** | Platform and moulded base with projections and recesses. | Spreads the tower's load over a wider footprint. |
| **Shikhara with kutas / sringas** | Tower clad in miniature spires. | Corbelled layers lock ceiling stones without mortar. |
| **Lata** | Continuous vertical band on each face. | Acts as the spine of each face. |
| **Sukanasa** | Projecting frontal arch over the vestibule. | Counters thrust where tower meets hall roof. |

### **Plan geometry**

Confidence: **Verify before use**   See V22

* A base grid of 64 (Manduka) or 81 (Paramasayika) squares.

* A circumscribing circle, with the square rotated at 45° or 22.5° steps to derive 8- or 16-point stellate outlines.

* Outer points (karna) and central faces (bhadra) set by fixed ratios. The formulas were missing from the original text.

* The session attributes this method to the Samarangana Sutradhara, ascribed to Bhoja.

### **Quarrying sequence**

Confidence: **Reported**

Described from general studies of historic Indian quarrying, applied to the Basoda ridges.

1. Read the bedding planes and natural fractures.

2. Cut trenches on three sides to isolate the block.

3. Chisel a line of lenticular or rectangular wedge holes, about 4–6 inches apart.

4. Split with wetted wooden wedges, or by fire-setting and quenching.

5. Drive iron wedges in unison, lever the block free and move it on rollers.

Blocks were rough-dressed at the quarry, leaving a thin protective layer for transport.

*Sources: NIAS Repository ("Stories from Silent Stones", 2018); Britannica; PrimeKnowAll; ASI supporters' Facebook.*

### **Acoustics, light and heat**

Confidence: **Verify before use**   See V17

* **Coupled volumes.** Hall, vestibule and sanctum act as linked acoustic chambers. A corbelled sanctum ceiling lengthens low-frequency reverberation.

* **Light.** East orientation lets morning light enter along the axis. Reflection off the vestibule floor softly lights the linga.

* **Heat.** Thick walls and self-shading projections give thermal mass.

*Sources: Taylor & Francis; ResearchGate; Rethinking The Future; ICCAUA (Markanda Temple study); Scribd; Slideshare.*

## **Inscriptions and genealogy**

### **The Udaipur Prashasti**

Confidence: **Documented**

A Sanskrit panegyric in Nagari script found at the temple. It sets out the Paramara lineage up to Udayaditya. The session describes it as 24 lines, later housed in the Gujari Mahal Museum, Gwalior; both details need confirmation.

### **Agnikula origin**

Sage Vasiṣṭha performs a sacrifice on Mount Abu. A warrior rises from the fire pit (agnikuṇḍa), recovers the sage's cow taken by Viśvāmitra, and receives the name Paramāra, "slayer of the enemy".

### **Serpent grammar charts (Varṇanāgakṛpāṇikā)**

Confidence: **Reported**

A newly discovered inscription on a sandstone slab at Udaipur, published by Ghent University in 2023, is titled "A serpentine scimitar of letters". Such charts arrange the Sanskrit alphabet and grammatical endings inside the body of a serpent. Related examples are known from Dhar, Ujjain and Un.

* The loops hold letters in phonetic groups.

* The session says the grid also maps noun endings (7 cases × 3 numbers) and verb endings across the 10 lakāras.

* The serpent tapers like a sword. The text calls the chart a magical blade (siddhāsiputrikā).

The session compares Udaipur with the serpent charts on the Bhojshala pillars at Dhar: pillars there versus a wall slab here, and a basic alphabet there versus combined noun and verb endings here. These comparisons and the verses said to praise Udayaditya below the tail need checking against the published paper.**\[V6\]**

*Sources: Ghent University Academic Bibliography (2023); Academia.edu; Wikipedia (Udayaditya); Puratattva; Siddham; Annual Report on Indian Epigraphy 1961–62 (via Puratattva).*

## **Education**

Confidence: **Verify before use**

The session presents the temple complex as a centre of learning modelled on the Bhojshala at Dhar. The grammar charts are real. The wider institutional picture is interpretive.

* **Teaching spaces.** Mandapa and courtyards used for debate (shastrartha), recitation and lectures.

* **Grammar (vyakarana).** Serpent and tortoise charts as permanent visual aids.

* **Metrics (chhandas).** Engraved metrical tables for composing kavya.

* **Music.** Fragments of notation studied alongside the temple's acoustics.

* **Architecture.** Bhumija proportional templates on temple walls.

* **Funding.** Devadaya and brahmadeya grants supporting scholars in mathas.

* **Scribes.** Kayasthas drafting grants and genealogies.

* **Plurality.** Shaiva, Vaishnava and Jain scholars side by side.

# **Part V. After the Paramaras**

## **Tughluq inscriptions and architecture**

Confidence: **Documented**

The Tughluq Shāhī Mosque stands west of the temple. Two Arabic–Persian inscriptions date to 737 AH (1336–37) and 739 AH (1338–39), in the reign of Muhammad bin Tughluq.

* The session says one inscription quotes the hadith on building a mosque and receiving a house in Paradise.

* The mosque uses a trabeate (post-and-lintel) system and reused carved elements (spolia) from subsidiary shrines.**\[V27\]**

* The Sanskrit record of 8 January 1338 (VS 1394\) on the southern forehall balustrade records a procession led by Harirāja in the same period.

*Sources: Wikipedia (Tughluq Shāhī Mosque); Wikimedia Commons; Siddham (record of a temple procession, VS 1394); ResearchGate ("Monuments and Monumentality in a Changing Socio-Political…", 2021).*

## **Adaptation of water systems**

Confidence: **Verify before use**   See V9, V10, V11

* **Tughluq period.** Mosque positioned to use existing channels for ablution; farmland sluices left to local control to protect revenue.

* **Malwa Sultanate period.** Wide ramps for animals at stepwells. The examples given (Moti Darwaza, Sher Khan Mosque stepwell) belong to Mandu.

* **Mughal period.** Channels to the Shahi Mahal; domed covers over well shafts; the Qanungo Baoli serving travellers outside the town.

*Sources: Down To Earth (1992); IGNCA; Wikipedia; Instagram (Suveer Singh Sikarwar); Siddham.*

## **Mughal Shahi Mahal and Masjid**

Confidence: **Documented**

Built east of the Nīlakaṇṭheśvara temple between 1026 AH (1616–17) and 1041 AH (1631–32), from Jahangir's reign into Shah Jahan's.

**Shahi Mahal**

Residence of the local governor. Rectangular plan with two storeys around two courtyards, four corner towers, and cusped-arch niches.

**Shahi Masjid (Qāḍiyon-kī-Masjid)**

Congregational mosque associated with the town's qadi. The session describes a central dome, pillared flat-roofed sections, jali screens, and a raised platform used as a burial ground.

**Inscriptions**

Two Persian inscriptions dated June 1632\.

*Sources: Wikipedia (Shahi Mahal and Mosque; Udaypur); Instagram (Suveer Singh Sikarwar, 2024).*

## **The 1645 trilingual stepwell inscription**

Confidence: **Documented**

A 13-line relief-carved slab set into the right side wall of the Qanungo Baoli, on the eastern outskirts of Udaipur.

| Part | Content |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Lines 1–3: Arabic** | Bismillah and Qur'anic verses, including "Help from Allah and a near victory" (61:13) and "Allah is the best guardian" (12:64), per the session. |
| **Lines 1–3: Persian** | Records construction in the reign of Shah Jahan. |
| **Lines 4–13: Sanskrit** | Opens "Siddhi\! Śrī Gaṇeśāya namaḥ". Names the builders and their family and gives the date. |

**Builders**

Brothers Gokuladāsa and Dāmodaradāsa, sons of Haridāsa, of a Māthur Kāyastha family holding the office of qanungo (revenue record-keeper).

**Date in three eras**

Vikrama Saṃvat 1701, Śaka 1566 and Hijri 1054, equal to Friday, 13 January 1645\.

Author credited on Siddham: S. Singh.

*Sources: Siddham ("Udaypur tri-lingual stepwell inscription dated 1645 CE", 2022); X (@historiakayasth, 2025).*
