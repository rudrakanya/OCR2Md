## INTRODUCTION

The legend current at Un was published by Alexander Cunningham in *Tours in Bundelkhand and Malwa in 1876-77* and a brief introduction of the temples was included in it.

In 1908, C.E. Luard wrote a description with some photographs in *Indore State Gazetteer*, Vol. 2. Gazetteer though not directly related to the study of art and architecture but sometimes deals with the local monuments and history. Mention may be made about the *Holkar State Gazetteer* (Vol. II, 1931) which possesses old photographs of temples of Un. The oldest photograph, which shows the temple of Chaubara Dera no. 1 with its *Sikhara still in situ* can be seen in this book. In the Progress Report of ASI, Western Circle, 1918-19 R.D. Banerjee wrote an account on the temples of Un.

In 'The Hindu Temple' (1946), a regional style was first identified by Stella Kramrisch which is classified as 'Bhumi-ja', i.e. country born. To narrate the architectural pattern, she preferred the temple of Nilkanthesvara of Udayapur as a model and marked the essential feature of *bhūmija prasāda*. But temples of Un are ignored there.

D.R. Patil enlisted the temples of Un in his *The descriptive and classified list of Ancient monuments in Madhya Bharat* and gave a brief summary of them in *Cultural Heritage of Madhya Bharat* (1952). According to him the nameless small shrine at northwest corner of Chaubara Dera no. 1 is attendant of the former.

A discussion about the '*bhūmija*' temple architecture by Krishnadeva in *Studies in Indian Temple Architecture* (1975) incorporates a brief paragraph mentioning the temples of Un and brings them under the category of 'bhūmija architecture'. He describes that the Un temples are contemporary with the Nilkanthesvara temple of Udayapur, but the chronological sequence regarding the building of temples at Un was still wanting. In the Presidential address of the seminar of Prachya Niketan, Bhopal, temples of Un are mentioned occasionally. Here he states that there is only one orthogonal *bhūmija* temple in Malwa and that the temple of Jāmalesvara at Jamili and thus, did not mention the orthogonal *bhūmija* temple of Un viz. the Ballālesvara temple. In *Temples of North India* (1992). The temples of Un are mentioned again, but without any detailed description or any new information or chronological sequence.

# LOCATION AND TOPOGRAPHY

Un (Latitude 21°50' North, longitude 75°27' East) is a village in the
Kharpone Tehaul of the West Nimar District of Madhya Pradesh. It lies 14
kilometers to the west of Kharpone, the district headquarter of West Nimar.
The Agra-Bombay national highway runs through the heart of Kharpone and
thus it is well connected with important centers like Indore, Khandwa, Dhar
and Jalgaon by road. The West Nimar District is bounded by the districts of
Dhar, Indore and Dewas in the north, Maharashtra in the south, the East Nimar
District in the east and the Badwani District in the west.

Un covers an area of 860 hectares of land and according to the census of
1981, its population is 11,947. It contains schools, a state post office, a Jain
Dharmasala, a police station and a panchayat office.

From the geographical point of view, it is situated on the northern
fringe of the Satpura ranges in the plain land of Nimar to the south of the
Narmada. A local stream, named Hathai (a tributary of the Borad), is flowing
through the east of the village. On its north a rocky highland of sandstone,
which was probably used as a quarry for the building of its temples, is located.

According to the Buddhist tradition, there were two routes to Vijayini.
One passing through the western side of Nimar and crossed the Narmada
opposite to modern Maheswar and the other, passing through still further
west, crossed that river at modern Chikkhalda and went northwards through
Bagh, near Gwalior. Un lies near this important tract, which was continuously
used in ancient period. The location of the village suggests that the place may
have enjoyed in the earlier period some importance as a center of commercial
activity. The presence of a number of temples of the Paramara period at the
village may be due to its prosperity as a trading center.

## UN IN LOCAL LEGENDS

According to a local legend, Balabāra, king of Karnāta, was once suffering from great pain due to a snake that surreptitiously entered his stomach. As no remedial measure could relive him from his pain, the king decided to end his life at Kāśī. On his way to the holy city, the king stopped at a place under a tree for overnight stay. During the night when the king was asleep and the queen was sitting by his side, a snake wriggling out of a hole of the tree, started conversation with the snake in the king's stomach. From the conversation the queen learnt that, of the two snakes, the former was guarding a huge treasure inside the tree hole and that the creature could be killed if somebody poured hot oil into the hole. She also came to know that the latter could die if the king drink water mixed with lime. The following morning the queen told her husband everything she had heard in the night and gave him a drink of limewater. Drinking it, the king was relieved immediately of his pain. The queen also poured hot oil into the tree hole, killed the snake in it and recovered the hidden treasure. At a mark of his gratefulness, the king built ninety-nine temples at the place. Since the number of temples built was one hundred less one (un), the place was named as Un. The currency of identical legends at Lalitpur and Udaypur of Madhya Pradesh makes it difficult to give any credence to such stories. As a matter of fact no historically reliable information is available regarding the concentration of large number of temples at Un.

The architectural features of the temples affiliate them with the Mālava style developed during the Paramāra period (c.A.D.800-1305). Later with Kumārapāla's conquest of Mālava in mid 12th century, a few Jain temples bearing Caulakya influence were built at Un. These temples, datable towards the 13th century A.D., suggest that Un continued to retain its importance as a holy seat for a long period.

As the village elders tell, Un flourished in Paramāra period and after the decline of Paramāras, Un lost its palmy days and the place was gradually turned into a forest inhabited by wild animals. Priests used to come to the temples to perform daily worship of deities installed in them and returned hurriedly once their duty was observed. In this densely forested land, the

2

Bhilis, Gonds and Korkus became the principal inhabitants. A Gaoli chief named Bija built the fort of Bijagarh in a hilly area in the neighbourhood of Un. Bhilis occupied this area and from here they used to control the whole tract. They had their own religious belief. During the survey of the author, many square and rectangle pieces of stones have been found in the site museum at Khargone. They are carved with religious symbols of local tribal people and religious images carved in the stylistic manner of local folk art. Perhaps, people collected the stone blocks from the sites of the temples and used them for this purpose. In consequence, the temples of Un gradually lost their glory and dense jungle covered them. Such a situation continued during the regime of the Holkers of Indore and even in the early years of the British Raj. As a result of neglect most of the temples at Un collapsed and those standing are on the verge of collapse.

When Un turned into a desolate place, fortune seekers made regular forays there in the hope of discovering hidden treasures in the temples. The myth of Un's hidden treasures is said to have lured even Malkar Rao Holkar of Indore to the place. The vandalism of these treasure hunters caused irreparable damage to the temples. Today Un is a sparsely populated village with ruins of lost temples and these remnants stand as testimony of the glory that Un once was.

The name of some Ballāla is connected with Un. A temple is named after him as Ballāleśvara and a badly damaged six-lined inscription on the outer wall of the temple bear the name Ballāla. Another decipherable word is rājupatra. But this may or may not be the epithet of Ballāla. Unfortunately, it is so much whether worn that it can not be deciphered fully. Attempts have been made to identify this legendary Ballāla with Hoysala king Ballāla, who invaded Mālava in the period of Paramāras. But the temple bearing the name Ballāla may not be related to him. Because, firstly, there is no evidence that he proceeded to the locality of Un. Secondly, the temple is a typical Paramāra temple, bearing the trace of characteristic features of Paramāra architecture and sculpture. From no point of view, it can be treated as a Hoysala creation. According to some scholars, Ballāla may be identified with the ruler Ballāla, who usurped the Paramāra throne at the capital city named Dhura taking the opportunity of the political unrest and instability occurred in the Paramāra kingdom in about the first half of the 12th century and was killed in about 1151A.D. # UN AND ITS ARCHAEOLOGICAL REMAINS

Tradition tells that ninetynine temples were built here, which may be an exaggeration. At present ten temples are standing here in varying stages of preservation. Existence of nearly seven temples is traceable in the ruins of their bases. There is a mound (approximately 1 meter high) in between the Chaubara Dera No. 1 and Mahākāleśvara No. 1. Most probably it buries a ruined temple. From the religious point of view, the standing temples at Un can be divided into two broad groups - Brahmanical and Jain. Of the two groups, the number of Brahmanical temples are more than that of Jain temples. But from the point of view of architecture, there is little difference between the two groups of temples.

The Brahmanical temples include the Chaubara Dera No. 1, Mahākāleśvara No. 1 and 2, Ballāleśvara, Nilakantheśvara, Hatakēśvara, Omkāreśvara and a small nameless shrine of Śiva. Two temples, viz. the Chaubara Dera No. 2 and Gośelśvara belong to the Jain group. Among these temples, the Nilakantheśvara, Hatakēśvara and Omkāreśvara are located within the village. The Nilakantheśvara and Hatakēśvara are close neighbours, the latter standing to the south of the former. The Chaubara Dera No. 1 and Mahākāleśvara No. 1 stand on the east, Ballāleśvara on the west and Gośelśvara on the south of the village. The Mahākāleśvara No. 1 stands very near to the north of the Chaubara Dera No. 1. Mahākāleśvara No. 2 stands by the side of the main road leading from Khargone to Un. Within the compound of Chaubara Dera No. 1 is placed the small nameless temple of Śiva. Except the Gośelśvara, which rises on a small rock, all the temples of Un stands on the plain ground. All these temples are located either by the side or far from the Khargone-Un road that winds outside and through the village.

In addition to those still extant, ruined remains of temples can be traced at Un. Thus ruins of three temples can be noticed respectively on the south, southwest and west of Ballāleśvara. On a small rocky area near the Gośelśvara on the south of the village, foundations of five small temples are identifiable even now. In the compound of the Mahākāleśvara No. 2, on the side of the

The archaeological wealth, represented by extant temples and their ruins, testifies to the importance of Un in the days gone by. Though the local tradition tells that ancient Un possessed ninetynine temples, ninetynine basilis (reservoirs), ninetynine talass (ponds) and ninetynine kundas is an obvious exaggeration, a large number of them seem to have actually existed at the place. # RISE AND FALL OF THE
PARAMARA PATRONS

The central Indian tabirland, known as Malwa (ancient Malwa), comprises territories which was once within old Central Indian Agency in period of British rule. This Agency was a collection of native states, viz. Gwalior, Indore, Dhar, Bhopal, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand, Bhil and a group of states like Jaora, Ratlam, Sillana and Sitamna. Taken together, they are commonly referred to as western Malwa. The contiguous district of Sangor is also within the geographical periphery of Malwa. In this region, the family representing the main monarchical branch of the Paramara, began its career as a vice-regal house of the Rastrakutas of Manyakhetra in the late eighth and early ninth century, under the leadership of Upendra. The political fate of his early successors was, intimately connected with the Prathara-Rastrakuta struggle, for Malwa being situated between the territories of these two imperial powers had to bear the first onslaught when these two states clashed. The decline of the Pratharas, the dominant power in Malwa, after their discomfiture at the hands of the Rastrakuta king Kyaa III (c.A.D. 940-51) gave the Paramaras an opportunity to gain undisputed political control over this region. When Siyaka II ascended the throne sometimes during the second half of the 18$^{th}$ century A.D., the Paramaras were so powerful as to endeavour for removing the yoke of the Imperial Rastrakutas. Siyaka not only achieved military victory against Khotjiga in A.D. 971, brother and successor of Kyaa III, but also plundered the city of Manyakhetra.$^{1}$ He assumed the title *Mahārajādharajapati*, signifying a sovereign status, in complete disregard of his Rastrakuta overlords. In this way Siyaka became the real founder of the Paramara kingdom of Malwa. When he abdicated the throne in favour of his son Vakpatirāja, he left behind a kingdom extending to the Banswara State on the north, to the Godavari on the south, Vidisha on the east and Mahi on the west.

Vakpati-Mutija, (c.A.D. 973 - c.A.D. 995) like his father, was a powerful king. Literary and epigraphic evidences show his military victories against a

With the accession of Bhoja (c.A.D.1000-c.A.D.1055), the son and successor of Sindhuraja, a brilliant chapter was opened in the history of the art and architecture of Mālavā. Epigraphical evidence eloquently testifies his participation in a vigorous temple building activity. For example v.20 of the Udaypur *Prakasti* of Udayāditya eulogies Bhoja for beautifying the world with temples dedicated to Śiva under such names as Kedāreśvara, Rāmeśvara, Somanābha, Sundra, Kāla, Anāja and Rudra. While Ganguly believes that all these temples were built in the Mālavā region, Trivedi asserts that the first three correspond to the famous temples of similar names respectively in the north, south and west. Trivedi further assumes that the fourth temple, Sundra, must have been built somewhere in the eastern quarter though no trace of it can be found today. Of the other temples, mentioned in the inscription, Kāla has been identified with the Mahākāla temple at Ujayini. The Bhojasālā located at the Paramāra capital Dhāra (modern Dhar), as its name suggests, was obviously built by Bhoja. Presumably other great work of Bhoja was the Bhojpura (modern Bhojpur) which seems to have been founded and named after him. As in the field of literature so also in the domain of architecture Udayāditya's reign constitutes the brightest chapter in the history of Paramāras. He renovated the Mahākāla temple at Ujayini which was probably built by Bhoja. If that be the case, the religious activities at Un used to receive liberal patronage of the Paramāra king. Representing the quintessence of the *Bhāmya* temple style that was nurtured and developed by the Paramāras, the Nilakṣṭheśvara provided the model to be followed by the later architects of Mālavā. # THE BHŪMĪJA BACKGROUND

In the *Aparāṭinaprechā* and the *Kārāmava*, two north Indian śilpa texts, the word *Bhūmija* occurs as one of the temple styles of India. Kramrisch identifies the style with a group of temples in Mālavā and adjoining region. They show certain common characteristics, the most prominent among those being the *śikkara* on which the central effect of each face of the body of sanctum cube is continued and carried up to its summit as an unbroken vertical band (*latā*). A rich tracery of miniature *caitya* arches covers the *latā* all through. The quadrants between the *latās* on four sides are filled in up to the peak of the *śikkara* by horizontal rows of miniature *śikkaras* (*śṛṇḍas*) of diminishing height as they go up. These miniature *śikkaras* are known as *kāyastambha* or *stambhakāya* meaning *kāyas* (*śikkaras*) on *stambhas* or *pillars*. Another characteristic feature is a *tukamāṇa* (a large *caitya*-window design) enclosing a sculptured medallion at the base of the *latā* on the front face and a similar but smaller version of it in the corresponding place on three other sides of the *śikkara*.

In plan also, the *Bhūmija* temples show certain distinctive features. Thus the exterior walls of the sanctum are variegated either by the usual right-angled *ratha* projections or on the principle of rotating a square on the same axis. In the latter case, a foliated star-shaped plan is obtained but the central buttress, the *bhadra*, on each face, runs parallel with the square plan of the sanctum chamber. The *bhadra* is carried upward and continued as an unbroken vertical band on the *śikkara* where it is known as *latā*. The prominent band of the *latā* with an uninterrupted and emphatic vertical sweep follows as a spine the main contours of the *śikkara* on its four faces and keep the latter in shape.

The novel scheme of the *śikkara* characterizes the *Bhūmija* class as a version distinct from other regional expressions of the *Nāgara* order. Kramrisch takes the *śikkara* as composite, as it combines two opposing principles, viz. a *śikkara* unencumbered with miniature *śikkaras* (latina) and

# Some observations

Was Chauhara Dera No. 1 a *Palicypatana* shrine?

A small *Bhūmija* temple stands on the northwest corner of Chauhara Dera No. 1. According to D.R. Patil it is an attendant shrine of Chauhara Dera No. 1. Scholars like M.D. Khare and Krishna Devy describe the Chauhara Dera No. 1 as a *palicypatana* temple complex. According to them, the aforesaid small temple is the extant one of the four such subsidiary shrines of the Chauhara Dera No. 1.

But the ground level where this adjacent small temple is standing is not the same with that of the Chauhara Dera No. 1. The floor level of this small temple is about 100 cm. higher than that of the Chauhara Dera No. 1. If it was a *palicypatana* temple complex then the subsidiary temples would not have been erected with a higher floor level than that of the central one. The *Shūmija* temple complex, in date and style, is the *sapthiyatana*. *Nālakanthekara* temple of Udaypur (Vidisha District). In this temple complex, all the temples standing commonly on a large plinth, rise from same ground level. The *bhiṁ* and *pitha* mouldings of the main temple or the central one are comparably more in number than the subsidiary temples. Therefore, the floor level of the *garbhagṛka* of the Nālakanthekara temple is obviously high in comparison to those of the attendant temples.

It seems that the small temple in the Chauhara complex was built later than the Chauhara Dera No. 1. Besides, there is no evidence of the presence of other three small temples during any period of existence of Chauhara Dera No. 1. Therefore, it appears that the Chauhara Dera No. 1 was constructed as a single shrined temple and remained so through the ages.

# The installed deity:

Most probably, it was dedicated to Lord Śiva. This inference is drawn from the frequent depiction of Śiva and Mahārvaṅ on mouldings, pillars and lintels.

# Temple style:

From the stellate layout of the plan, which is a dominant feature of a *Bhūmija* class of temple and following the similarity between other *Bhūmija*

30

temples and Chauhara Dera No. 1 at the same village, it is obvious that the latter had a *Bhūmija* likhara consisting of *kōrammukha*, *lāni*, *tārammukha*, and *lakamāṇa*. An old photograph of the Report of ASI attested it.

# A centre of education:

The *sarpakandha* inscription indicates that this temple was a centre of education. If that is so, once the mouldings were filled with the sound of recitation of the young students who used to gather them for study. As suggested by the inscription of the temple, learning of grammar seems to have been considered important.

# An uncommon feature:

Generally, Sapta Māṭrkās are accompanied by Virabhadra, a form of Śiva with vīna in his hands. But here Nāṭrkā is placed in the centre of the Sapta Māṭrkā panel on the door-lintel of the *garbhagṛka*.

# Stylistic character of the apsarās:

The apsarās adorning the temple are full of youth and beauty but they are conventional in their postures and murders. They also lack facial expression, which prevent the non-verbal communication between the sculpture and the viewer. Specially the thin legs, appear at a glance as metallic rods lack liveliness.

# Materials:

Two types of stone blocks were used here as the building material. For instance, fine grained ochre coloured sandstone was used here in the lintels and in the capitals of the pillars. The remaining part of the temple was built by local sandstone.

## MAHĀKĀLESVARA TEMPLE No. 2
(Plates XIV - XXII)

The Mahākālesvara temple No. 2 stands on the bank of the river Hathau that flows by the eastern side of Us. The facade of the dilapidated temple has collapsed. Other complementary structures attached to the *mulapṛśāda* in the axial length have also disappeared. At places, the foundation can only be recognised.

### TALACCHANDA

Like other temples of Us, the Mahākālesvara Temple No. 2 possesses a square *garbhagṛha* and a rectangular *antarula*. Once a large pillared *maṇḍapa* stood before it. At present, it is represented by traces of the floor and bases of pillars. The remnants of the pillars indicate that a group of four pillars in the centre of the *maṇḍapa* hall supported its roof. The *garbhagṛha* approached through the *antarula* from the *maṇḍapa*, is a square. But externally the plan of the *palcurūtha* *mūlapṛśāda* is stellate. The *bhadra* in each cardinal direction is parallel to the inner walls of the *garbhagṛha*. But the other *rathas*, moving round the gnomon within circle according to the rule of *parmurtanā*, are acute angled. As a result, the plan of the *mūlapṛśāda* becomes circular-stellate.

### URDHYACCHANDA

Along the vertical axis, the temple is divided into *pitha*, *maṇḍovara* and *śākhara*. As the *pitha* is buried under the ground, it is not possible to discern the features of its mouldings. The *vedībandha* consists of *kharaka*, *kumbha*, *antarapajta*, *cippika*, *kalala*, *cippika*, *antarapajta* and *kapotapalikā*. The *jarighā* above *maṇḍikā* moulding has in the *bhadras* large niches filled with images of deities. Other *rathas* are decorated with pilasters. Even the *satiāntaras* are decorated with *kaṭastambhas* in relief. The *baraṇḍikā* is composed of mouldings, viz. *chajjikā* and *kaṭacchādya* recessed by *antarapajta*.

# (Plates XXIII - XXXVI; Fig - 3)

# TALACCHANDA

## ANONYMOUS TEMPLE
(Plates XXXVII - XLII; Fig. 4)

A small temple is standing in a very damaged state at the northwest corner of the Chaubara Dera No. 1. Though small in size, the elaborately carved śākanāsā of the temple, shows that once it was a beautiful shrine.

### TALACCHANDA

This east facing *paṭicaraṭha* temple is built on a stellate plan. This *malapṛśāda* encloses within it a square *garbhagṛha*. Its *talacchanda* consists of *garbhagṛha*, an *antarāla* and a *dvāramandapa*. Ruins of the floor indicate that the *garbhagṛha* had a square *vedi* abutting on its rear wall. Rectangular *antarāla* has two niches in both sides. The square *dvāramandapa* is an open *balcony-cum* entrance provided with *kakiśana* and *āsanapaṭa*. As it is a small temple the *maṇḍapa* and *mukhamaṇḍapa* are combined here in a small *dvāramandapa*.

### URDIHACCHANDA

The *malapṛśāda* has the usual three vertical parts viz. *pāṇa*, *maṇḍavara* and *śākara*. Above two plain *bhatā* courses, the sloping *pāṇa*, *maṇḍavara* and *śākara*. In the pedestal of the left side, a short votive inscription records that it was dedicated by a Jain *śikhara* named *Vālakirti*.

### Some observations

In some respect, the temple stands apart from others at Un. For instance, the embellishment of the temple lacks figure sculptures and the *bhadras* are not provided with niches. The summarised treatment together with the presence of simple *grāsapattikā* in place of *gajambara* and *narathara* suggests for the temple a late date comparing to the *Bhāmiṇa* temples at Un.

The temple can be assigned to the beginning of the 13$^{th}$ century. It has been suggested by an inscription, dated V.S. 1263 (A.D.1206), on the pedestal of the Tirthankara images installed in the *garbhagṛha*. It shows that by the time when it was built, tradition of building typical *bhāmiṇa* architecture probably ceased here.

The *talacchanda* and the basal mouldings of the temple is more or less similar to the previous Jain temple at Un. But in outer wall, absence of the niches on *bhadras* and other *rathas* are noteworthy. It was not the conventional scheme of Paramāra period to build a temple without niches in *bhadra* filled with some image of deity. It seems, the current outer wall is not the original construction and is a result of renovation.

# SCULPTURES

**CLASSIFICATION OF TEMPLE SCULPTURES :** Categorically, sculptures embellishing the temples at tin can be divided into two broad groups, religious and secular. Among the religious sculptures, the cult images of tin consist of *Sivalingas* and *Jain Tirthankaras* only. Most of the *Sivalingas* are lost leaving the *arghyaa* behind. At present, simple ordinary pieces of stones are placed on the *arghyaa*. In the *Jain Chaubara* Dera No. 2 the *garbhagcha* is empty. Three Digambara Tirthankaras are installed in *Godevara*. They are standing in *kayotsargal* posture on a common *tirutha* and *Godevara*. They are accompanied by pedestal. Their chests bear the *irivatyya-lancana*. They are accompanied by *cāmaradhārinis* and canopad by umbrella.

Images of various deities acting as *alambāra devatās* and *parivāra devatās* are represented on *kumhba* mouldings, *bhudra*, *sāraśenakas*, *rathikās* and pillars. They are either standing in *samapāda* or *dvibhaṅga* or they are sitting in *padnāsana* or *lalitāsana*. Sometimes, they are presented in such dancing postures as *ekapada*, *urdhvāṭiṇa*, *dyuta*, *catura* or *svatika*. All of them are distinguished by their attributes, bedecked with jewellery and are distinguishable by their attributes, *mudḍa-mālā* or *vanamālā* and characterised by sometimes have long garlands, *mūṁḍa-mālā* or *vanamālā* and characterised by *jajā*, *kīrija* or *kāraṇḍa-mukūja*. In some cases, the mounts are present. The image of *Andhakāstaka*, depicting the destruction of *Andhakāsura* by Lord Śiva, is narrative in character.

Among the semidivine secular sculptures the attendants of higher divinities includes *paricārikās*, *cāmaradhārinis* and *dvārapālas*. *Dvārapālas* have four hands which hold different attributes. All of them are standing either in *samapāda* or in *dvibhaṅga* and richly ornamented. The *paricārikās* and *cāmaradhārinis*, holding *cāmaras*, pitchers or lotus are presented in *dvibhaṅga* or *samapāda*.

Another class of the secular sculptures are the *nāyikās* and *apsarās*. They are beautiful and youthful nymphs, adorned with *hāra*, *kucabandhu*, *katibandhu*, *mekhālā*, *kundalā*, *kankuna*, *aṅgāda* and *manjīra*. They are dressed by fine clothes without obscuring the lovely contours of female

# THE QUARRY – CHIRAKHAN
(Plates XCVIII – XCIX)

In the neighbouring areas of Us there is a small hilly area which local people use to call as Chirakhan. Significantly, 'Chira' means cutting and 'Khup' means 'Khadar' or quarry. Obviously this area was used as a quarry in the long for supplying masonry blocks for building temples at Us. Marks of quarrying stone from this area are still present. The coarse grained sandstone of the quarry is not of a very high quality. It explains why the temples at Us, which are built of stone quarried from this hillock, are either in decay or in complete ruins. It may be assumed that the continuation of temple building activity at Us for a long period demanded the stonecutters to stay at or near the quarry site for years together. A settlement of the stonecutters, therefore, seems to have developed in the neighbourhood of the hillock. The population of the settlement, if there was any, must have comprised veterans and young men who were attracted to the place hoping to learn as well as find employment.

Irrespective of the external stellate or orthogonal plan, all the temples internally are composed of some square and rectangular segments.

Temples at Un open themselves in *dolrumundapa*. Through the entrance parch, one enters in the spacious and peritiyled mandapa. There are accompanied by projected half open *pārīvaṃandapa* in sides, provided with *śaktiṇanas*. From the richly adorned mandapa, a passage through the *sattanthi* culminates in the austerity of the *parbhāgra*.

Among the vertical axis, temples reveal a restricted and balanced contrast work of bold vertical lines and horizontal layers. In elevation, they present a cognate program of *pithu, maṇḍabara*, and *śākara*. A three series of mouldings, recessed by *antarapottas*, ornate the *pithu* and *vedhuvudhu* only with little difference regarding the number of *bhūṣa* courses (in *pithu*) and *paṇikā* (in *pithu* and *vedhuvudhu*). Prominent *bhudras* in cardinal points dominates the *janṅkā*, rest portion is built by alternate *rathas* and recesses. The *janṅkā* is surmounted by *barandhā* where the most prominent mouldings are *chājikā* and *kutaḥbhāya*. The *śākara*, still extant, are curvilinear decorated by Jain, *kutaḥambha* and *śākāṇasa* - all marked by regional characteristics. As regards plan and elevation, the brahmanical temples at Un share certain common features.

These temples present a common catalogue of arrangement of divine images. In this respect importance is given to the *bhudra* niches, *śākāṇasa* and *jārasundhā*, and lintels. Placement of different divine images, either in panel

The Jain temples, though have common resemblance with brahmanical temples form another group. They are unique and different from brahmanical temples in following respects:
1. These temples are north faced, but brahmanical temples are faced either east or west.
2. Presence of gajathara and narathara in the pitha of Chandra Dera No. 2 is noticeable. In place of above mentioned mouldings, two gajapajthika are added in Gajathara temple. All these features change the fixed program of basal mouldings so far followed in the temples of brahmanical faith. These new features were brought from Caulukya temples after the Caulukya invasion took place in Mahwa in 11st Century A.D.
3. Mandapara of Jain temples are eight-pillared and differ in overall look from four pillared mandapas of brahmanical temples. There are noticeable differences in the decor of the pillars in two different groups of temples. Mandapas of brahmanical temples are provided with proches in sides and have only one entrance. But the mandapas of Jain temples differ from that of first group of temples.
4. In brahmanical temples, madhyaratha has the privilege to be more ornate than the other rathas. A large niche, filled with some excellently carved image of deity is placed in bhadra. As a result, bhadra enjoyed the position as the main focus of attraction in wall than the simply carved other rathas. But in Chandra Dera No. 2 the prominence of the bhadra decreased due to the tendency of decorating all the rathas with large figure sculptures. It completely changed the appearance of large figure sculptures. It completely changed the appearance of jangha. The difference in between the two groups of temples, become more clear due to the disappearance of bhadra niche in Gajathara temple.

5. The Sithara in Gajathara temple, though renovated later, gives us an impression that Sithara of Jain temples were different in composition than those of the brahmanical temples.
Thus the Jain temples at Un represent a hybrid character and features were borrowed from both the Paramara and Caulukya temple architecture.
Continuity of common cultural wave in a vast area around Un may be recognized while the same stylistic trend of temple buildings are noticed in places like Raybaryara, Aghahan, Asangam, Puthom etc. but none of these temples of these places surpass the temples of Un either in size or in aesthetic view. It seems, wealthy and cultured people with religious best of mind inhabited the area around Un. They were inspired by the royal temples at Un, and tried to imitate the best specimens of their period. This shows how Un worked as the hub of the culture and this cultural wave inspired the adjacent area.
The experimental phase regarding the building of stellate temple in Mahwa is noticed in Omkar-Mandhata (the undated temple of Amarewara, supposed to be built in later half of tenth century). It shows a rudimentary phase of stellate layout. But in Mahwa no bhumija temple, in its fully developed form, is now in existence, which can be attributed to a prior date of Chandra Dera No. 1.
No other bhumija structure is found among the numerous temples of Omkar-Mandhata. It seems, the bhumija style could not hold a steady ground here. The endeavor and exegetation of the artisans took perfect shape at Un and a series of bhumija temples were built here. The rapt attention of the artisans regarding the building of the bhumija temples at Un was hindered by political uncertainty due to Caulukya invasion and some Jain temples of hybrid character were built here.
