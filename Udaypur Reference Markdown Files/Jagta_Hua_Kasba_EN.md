# The Story of Udaipur

This true story is of a town where history has just broken its silence. A ruined little town of eight thousand people, existing on the margins, which for several past decades had wanted to say something. Perhaps only a few centuries had passed in its waiting. It wanted to say not a little, but a great deal. It had tales of a thousand, twelve hundred years to tell. It had been waiting for someone to come and sit at ease, to feel its silence, and to grant it the hearing of its saga. This town, which appears so forlorn, has waited very long indeed; but it seems it never lost hope. Otherwise, why would all this — that has happened — have suddenly happened?

What's in a name? One Udaipur is in Rajasthan — behold its fortune. Another Udaipur is in Madhya Pradesh — come and behold its misfortune too. Each has its own stars: somewhere glittering, somewhere in eclipse. About this Udaipur no one knows anything. Many people, at the very mention of Udaipur, go straight to the address of the glorious Udaipur of the Sisodia dynasty in Rajasthan — whereas more ancient than the Udaipur of Rajasthan is the desolate Udaipura of Madhya Pradesh. But what does even that matter? This story is of this little-known — rather, unknown — Udaipur.

This Udaipur is at a distance of a hundred and fifty kilometres from Bhopal, the capital of Madhya Pradesh. After Independence, when, in the formation of new states, Madhya Pradesh was created, the days of a Bhopal stinking like a dungheap of nawabi rule turned for the better. Bhopal — which had remained for two hundred years in the grip of an Afghan family (which our spoiled habit of addressing very respectfully as a "nawabi princely state" should now be corrected) — was granted the honour of being made the capital of a new province emerging on the map.

In 1707 the most notorious Mughal, Aurangzeb, died far away in the Deccan, and in the years following the chaos that broke out in Delhi because of this, an Afghan named Dost Mohammad Khan set out from Delhi and appeared in this region for the first time. He got employment in some small Rajput fiefdom near Berasia, 40 kilometres from Bhopal, and, as was the common practice throughout the India of that era, through treachery, intrigue, discord and needless attacks, he soon set himself up as the new master of an old Gond estate. It was Queen Kamalapati of Bhopal who took *jal-samadhi* (drowned herself), and her son Nawalshah was killed in these very intrigues. A writer named Niranjan Verma wrote a novel on this sequence of events that befell the breast of Bhopal — *Banganga se Halali*. A railway station near Bhopal named Habibganj was, a few months ago, named after this very Queen Kamalapati. Just as, in Akbar's time, [befell] Maharani Durgavati of Chauragarh, a pitiful tale of the destruction of a lineage was repeated here too. These are chapters of around 1715–20, recorded in hazy memories.

In 1947 the heirs of that same Afghan — in the capacity of the Nawab of Bhopal — remained an obstacle in the path of freedom, striking bargains under the influence of Mohammad Ali Jinnah, the greatest villain of the story of India's partition. The old counterfeit coins of politics had become obsolete, but they were not easily willing to give up their hold on Bhopal. They were simply not ready to see that the times had changed and that India was now not prepared to carry the old rotten system. The loyalties of the nawabs and nizams were with the patrons of Pakistan. But they did not know that history had turned a corner and their days were numbered. For two years they kept freedom from entering the borders of Bhopal. The country had paid the price of partition by being broken into two pieces, and now it had been freed from ten centuries of long slavery; but to obtain Delhi's freedom in Bhopal, agitations went on separately for two years, and bullets were rained on ordinary people. The stubborn Nawab was in some world of dreams. His dream soon shattered and scattered.

With the broken freedom that came along with the blood-soaked partition of the country, and with the formation of Madhya Pradesh in 1956, the path to Bhopal's becoming the capital was cleared. Centuries before these events — filled with the fierce political turmoil of the 20th century — Udaipur, far from Bhopal, was an old settlement on dusty, unpaved and sickly roads, where a mighty echo of history had come to rest. It was like one of those celestial announcements

that had maintained a silence. But from the hopes born in the country after Independence, Udaipur remained untouched for decades. If freedom was to bring some new dawn for the country and for the people of the country — leave the rest of the country aside — that new dawn never came up to the walls of Udaipur's collapsed fort. It remained a desolation encircled by gloom. No one felt the need to peer into its ancient magnificence. Actually, this was not the lament of Udaipur alone. Countless historical settlements have simply remained lying in the status of ruined wrecks of a country declared free on the map. Sad though it may be, this is nothing new.

This story of Udaipur — filled with the energy of a fresh stirring — begins with a conference held at the prestigious Dr. Harisingh Gour Central University of Sagar, which was not on history or archaeology. I had gone there for a day with the aim of hearing some speakers. I met the famous historian Dr. Suresh Mishra, who was to address a session there. He had been a professor of history in colleges for 37 years; by now he had written 40 books. I noticed that at lunchtime, before the next session, he was absorbed in a copy in his room. They were printed A4-size pages, which he was reading very fast and correcting the errors. When I sought to know about his busyness, it turned out that his next book was going to press; he was reading its proof here in the remaining time. It was John Malcolm's Malwa diary, which came out in print just a few months later.

Dr. Mishra was past 80, and I found that he was fresher, full of more enthusiasm and energy than any young man. My childhood interest in history came to a boil at finding before me such a historian, with whom I had had only superficial conversation. He used to read me in the newspaper, and his reactions kept reaching me through a few mutual bridge-friends. We had hardly ever met before. I had once received an invitation to dine at his home, but had not been able to go. In that meeting he mentioned some of my news reports. But I wanted to talk to him about history — about the history of my own surroundings, and about the other important sequences of events of my country, which had been suffered during the long slavery.

To put it plainly, I was eager to know the details of the swift and merciless changes that occurred after the Islamic occupation of Delhi, because I had been engaged for several years in delving into their documents — despite the fact that history was not my subject. I had studied mathematics; I had even taught in a college for a year and, leaving it all, had come into journalism; but from childhood my interest in history had been to such a degree that history had never left my trail. Finding a famous historian before me, it felt as though all the degrees had been received in a single day. In the very first face-to-face [meeting] he seemed to me very friendly and cheerful.

We decided right there that the two of us would set out on a heritage walk to some such historical spots of Madhya Pradesh where people generally do not go — such barren and desolate regions, far from the eyes and interests of ordinary people, toward which either idol-thieves have kept going, or, in the dark of night, the daring seekers of buried treasure; and, if stone-quarries are nearby, the plunderers — enjoying the political patronage of modern India — who make money from the illegal mining of stone. It was a bilateral agreement that we would go together. My greed was that, in the long journeys of a week, I would also get to hear much from him — the essence of his long experience; who knows what useful thing I might come upon that had not so far been found in the documents.

I can say that in that meeting at Sagar, quite a few of the "qualities" in our horoscopes matched. For instance, Dr. Mishra too never leaves today's work for tomorrow; he does not put off the morning's work till the evening. To him, today is cash, tomorrow credit — and he holds that tomorrow never comes. Whatever good is to be done, do it this very day; put off the bad till tomorrow. Whatever is to be said, say it without hesitation. Record every necessary piece of information in a diary; do not go by memory alone. Talk on facts. Nurse no prejudice. Keep up the search for truth, and take it as settled that in this world there is no final truth. The foundation of today's universally-accepted truth can be shaken by some new discovery of tomorrow. See one truth from many dimensions, and only then arrive at a conclusion. These are the basic things for understanding history properly.

Between us it was decided, as a common minimum programme, that the heritage walk would begin from Bundelkhand. As soon as this was decided, Dr. Mishra made a list. He arranged the order of several important historical places — where we would begin, where we would stay how long, and that we would spend the time after evening in travel, so that we could spend most of the day at one place or another. In his list were these names — Rahatgarh, Garhakota, Ajaygarh, Panna, Dhubela, Chhatarpur, Garh Kundar. I mentioned Vrindavanlal Verma's novel on Garh Kundar, which was in my collection. It was broadly decided that we would take a few days' leave and set out together by car, and, going to these places, meet the local people; delve into their history in the documents; view history with a new eye; and, if something worth writing was found, write it too. And to write first, there is, of course, the Facebook page — the product of the fertile brain of Mark Zuckerberg, the technological great-sage of the virtual world.


As always happens, after returning to one's own world such fancies vanish into thin air. All such decisions are like the dispassion felt at a cremation-ground [that fades on returning home]. But in our case this never happened — despite the fact that within three months, in March 2020, corona attacked India, and the whole year of 2020 was going to be consumed by it. For the sake of saving lives, everything in life came to a halt. All plans were left where they were; but our plan for the heritage walk stayed fresh, lying in wait for its chance. Even after the fury of the first wave of corona subsided, the fear of infection remained; but by the time December came, we had run out of patience with everything. We decided that now, come what may, we would set out. During this time Dr. Mishra had not gone out anywhere as a precaution; doing all his own work himself, he had stayed at home. We spoke often, and he never forgot to remind me of our resolve to set out together once conditions improved. "Vijay-ji, we have to complete the heritage walk. Don't forget it." Each time, at the end of our conversation, his words seemed to linger in my ears.

The auspicious moment for setting out for Udaipur, on the third — the first Sunday of the month of January 2021 — did not come about suddenly. In December, a plan had twice been made to set out together with Rakesh Diwan, an activist of the Narmada Bachao Andolan, who had been a close acquaintance of Dr. Mishra long before me. But we soon realized that Rakesh-ji is surely one of the busiest luminaries of the country. Despite his promise, one task or another kept detaining him in the capital. I know that his own entanglements are not few. Since he could not go along — thinking "we'll go together some other time" — on the second of January 2021, the duet of one knower of history and one seeker made its first halt at Udaipur.

This was a quiet beginning to the series of our heritage walk. By then it had been decided that, before the weather warmed up, the proposed journey to Bundelkhand too should be undertaken. Not putting off today's work for tomorrow, Dr. Mishra had prepared its dates and a date-wise route as well.

In the fog-filled first week of January, the green sheet of wheat, gram and lentil in the fields was spread out like a carpet in our welcome. The icy winds coming from the north had made a season cold enough for shivering. On the third of January, the 84-year-old Dr. Suresh Mishra and I got off at that corner which — hemmed in by lumps and shanties — was Udaipur's wretched little bus stand. A dust-eating, disorderly and ruined three-way junction, in front of which the tar road runs 16 kilometres away to join Ganj Basoda. Ganj Basoda is the nearest railway station and a small commercial town. Udaipur falls in this same tehsil of Vidisha district. The other road of the junction takes a 90-degree turn and goes off 22 kilometres away toward Mandi Bamora, which is another railway station and a small commercial town. On this same road, seven kilometres from Mandi Bamora, is Kurwai, in whose assembly-constituency Udaipur falls.

In front of the corner, Udaipur — standing waiting who-knows-for-whom along the roadsides that go from here to there — seemed to us very sad. It had been surrounded by hopelessness since who knows when. Like some forgotten call buried under the dust of time, which even today is impatient to become an echo, so that it may merge into the winds and the whole world may know what Udaipur is — what its meanings are — and what there is that is beyond meaning!

Like Bhopal, Kurwai too had been, for two hundred years, a small princely state in the grip of yet another Afghan family, whose relations were with the clan that had seized Bhopal. Abida Begum, the daughter of the Afghan chieftain who had seized Bhopal, had been married to the holder of Kurwai; and she, when Pakistan was formed, did not consider either Kurwai or Bhopal fit to live and stay in. Taking one of her sons, Shahryar Mohammad Khan, she went and settled in the city of Karachi in Pakistan, formed in the name of Islam. Shahryar there rose to high positions. He also wrote a book — *The Begums of Bhopal*. In this book are recorded the merciless [blood-soaked] details of Dost Mohammad Khan's seizure of Queen Kamalapati's Gond estate. The tales of making Jagdishpur into Islamnagar, and, before this, of the massacre of Hindu jagirdars at a Holi celebration, Shahryar too has written down as though they were deeds of bravery. This sequence of historical events, recorded among Bhopal's saddest memories, Niranjan Verma made the central subject of his poignant novel.

About Udaipur no such details are found. No one wrote its details. Even if written, they were printed somewhere in the form of an essay, or appeared like a post on social media. If someone had done some research too, then, recording a doctorate in his account, it would be kept somewhere in some files. If in the form of some book, it would be adorning some libraries somewhere. Udaipur was a darkness resting in the light of its own lamp. But in Udaipur's own dark womb much is contained, which is impatient to give an account of itself. Its story is on countless inscriptions.

The third corner of that desolate three-way junction of Udaipur takes you into an extremely narrow lane, barely six or seven feet wide. This single crooked and extremely narrow lane, entering the settlement from the main road, goes upward along a slope. This is a painful path to reach the greatest identity of a Udaipur that, in its own time, was magnificent and more than a thousand years old. On both sides of this roughly three-hundred-metre-long lane are decades-old shops made of stone — of cloth, groceries, sweets. These shops are made of the stones found in Udaipur itself, in which the use of cement and bricks was next to nil. In many old and closed houses, doors of the old era, of stone itself, are clearly visible. Here everything was of stone alone. It was stones alone that once wrote, in detail, the story of Udaipur. With the passing centuries, Udaipur must have kept spreading and sprawling here and there over its old plan. Broad and orderly roads turned into disorderly narrow lanes. The picture kept changing. Centuries had passed.

At the end of the lane a very old, huge stone doorway appears, on the other side of which is the Neelkantheshwar Mahadev temple, one of the finest monuments of this region from the era of the Paramara kings. That narrow, suffocating lane is the only way that leads to the doorway in the rear part of the temple. The temple's main east-facing doorway has been shut for who knows how long. It must once have been shut for some reason, and it is shut to this day.

I had come here many times before this too, and each time this wretched lane taunted me. Seeing, through the lane, the incomparable architecture of the temple, it felt as though a glittering diamond had been placed on a heap of garbage, and no one had any care for it. From ordinary people to the traders of the lane, from the representatives of the three-tier panchayat to the other leaders and government functionaries — I used to return only after giving them a piece of my mind. This lost lustre pricked no one's eyes. Everyone's work was going on carefree. The world's business was going on as usual. The tears that had flowed from Udaipur's eyes too had dried up long ago. In the silence it had turned to stone, accepting its very neglect as its destiny — whereas these were such tasks as needed no session in Parliament or the Assembly. To a large extent these were local matters, whose picture could be changed by the joint initiative of the panchayat and the administration alone.

The condition of Udaipur seemed to us worse than that of lakhs of the country's other wretched towns. It is a gram panchayat. This condition of this region — enriched by stone-quarries — should not be at all. Here, even if the governments did nothing, the people who had come into old, wealthy status from the quarries could, joining together, have done a great deal by themselves. But it was not only this town that was asleep. Every pillar of democracy, and every parasitic creeper (*amar bel*) climbing each pillar, and all the sections and castes of society dependent even on those creepers, were expending themselves in the clamour of their own rights. Who had the leisure to peer into history?

This was such a place that hardly any Collector of Vidisha or Commissioner of Bhopal could have come more than once. Stray tourists from Bhopal and from distant lands too used to come here often. The interest of the Indian visitors was only in the temple — not even the temple, but in the Neelkantheshwar Mahadev seated in the sanctum of the temple. People would go straight in, perform worship and ablution (*abhishek*), and, hanging one more category of the poison of their endless desires around the neck of Bholenath [Shiva], come out — a son's job, a daughter's marriage, court matters, land disputes, the wish to become wealthy.

Ever since cameras became easy and ever since the mobile came into hand, people would stop outside for a while to take photos and the like. Very few people would pay attention to the astonishing architecture of the temple. Next to none would peer into its real history. All would come out of the temple and, without wasting time, get out of Udaipur. Seeing them come thus and go thus, oblivious, Udaipur would become despondent. No one stops. Hardly anyone ever peers into the surrounding lanes. No one came to ask after the state of the crumbling buildings. No one saw those inscriptions on which our own ancestors had written who-knows-what. No one went up to those stepwells and tanks where, in one age, the liveliness of people from far and wide must have been. Now, in the desolations, the breath of all of them is failing. Even patience has its limit.

But history was terribly restless to turn a corner in Udaipur. No one who came to the temple went into the narrow lane beside the temple — whereas these lanes lead into a settlement where countless stories of a thousand years are scattered at every step. On both sides of the temple's finest precinct are astonishing constructions built in the time of the Paramara kings. Old houses built from the arrangement of massive carved stones. The narrow lanes too must once have been quite broad, straight, clean roads, which, after the Paramaras, shrank and grew narrow on account of illegal encroachments. Not only did the lanes grow narrow; the minds too remained narrow — whereas Udaipur was writhing to take a long stretch. In the pages ahead of this book are recorded the very heartbeats of this Udaipur.


Wrapping up the long introduction right here, let us proceed. Dr. Suresh Mishra is with me. Having completed the climb of that narrow lane going upward, we did not enter the temple in front of us. Dr. Mishra was, of all the many people who went with me, the only one who preferred to go first into this historic settlement. Gazing at the temple on our left, we turned toward the lanes. On the basis of my broken-and-scattered knowledge, I kept telling something about the Paramara kings — because twenty years earlier, during newspaper coverage, while writing the story of the Bhojshala of Dhar, I had got a chance to work on the Paramara kings too. From within Maharashtra, Gujarat and Rajasthan, the northern boundary of the Paramaras' kingdom extended up to here, Udaipur. From here the territory of the Kalachuris began. Because of being on the border, Udaipur must have been very important for the Paramaras from a strategic point of view — which is evident too from its thousand-year-old development and expansion.

Instead of going into the temple, the two of us first went to a building which is there called Bija Mandal. Seen from outside, it is a residential compound like the ordinary houses of a village; but only on going inside does one understand that the original building must once have been very magnificent indeed. The door of one of its cellar-like rooms is just like that of some old temple — almost as old as the old temple. On one side of this door, in a splendid square stone frame, an eight-line Sanskrit inscription survives. On the other side, an inscription of the same size is blank. Either nothing was written on it, or what was written was scraped off. To look at, it seems nothing could have been written; it was clean, exactly like a blank slate.

We saw that the domestic cattle of the family living there were tied up. If it was a temple, then within, the deity was absent from the sanctum, and this empty space was filled with fodder. On both sides of the sanctum too were two rooms, resting the weight of their roof on splendid carved pillars just as old. On the roof, some liveable rooms must have been built much later. The old and new construction is clearly visible. Even the new is not new; it must be four to six hundred years old.

In Bija Mandal, Sachin Sharma lives with his family. My meeting with him was in some later journeys. Within a radius of fifty metres on the left of the temple, this is the south direction. His family is famous by the name of the Ghadiyali family. They relate that in some age, at the fixed times of the temple's worship, ablution and rites, bells and gongs (*ghante-ghadiyal*) were sounded from this very building. This was an announcement to the whole town that the time to come to the temple had arrived. It is possible that special signals were given at the times of the royal family's activity in the temple too. By this reckoning, it was an important institution connected with the temple's activities, which was possibly also used as the residence of some important person connected with the temple management or the worship-rites. In the Paramaras' rule, temples were not limited only to worship-rites; they were also used, at the local level, as a knowledge centre or for schools. Children were given, right there, education in all the disciplines of their time. The Saraswati-kanthabharana of Dhar, Ujjain and Mandu have been proof of this — as also the historical assembly-hall (*sabha mandal*) of old Bhopal in the Chowk Bazaar, which was built by this very Udaipur's creator, Maharaj Udayaditya — who came after King Bhoj in the Paramara dynasty.

We went into those lanes where the huge doorways, built in the Paramara kings' special architectural style, still stand. The houses on both sides have gradually come very close to the ancient doorways; that is why those old broad and orderly roads have turned into narrow lanes. Passing through those very lanes, we went to an old temple named Pisanhari. In lane after lane, pieces of old carved stones have been brought into use anywhere at all. When the Paramaras' glory ended, Udaipur too became an orphan. In the centuries after the 11th-century occupation of Delhi by the Turks, followers of Islam, Udaipur too came into the grip of the far-reaching changes. New marks began to appear in the old constructions. The high-handedness of the centuries is clearly visible everywhere.

Groping at the ruined grandeur on both sides of the dusty, stony roads, the two of us were guessing that, from the desolate doorways of these ruins, it appears there must have been some commercial complexes here, or the residence of some very important person. It is possible there were some offices connected with the affairs of the kingdom, where in some era the liveliness of people from far and wide must have prevailed. At some time there must have been a coming and going of chariots, bullock-carts and horsemen with great splendour. But today these old buildings were collapsing somewhere; somewhere, having collapsed, they had become entirely heaps of stone. The dust and rain of time had filled these stones with scrub and thicket.

The condition of Udaipur had become like that of a matted-locked ascetic (*jata-jut-dhari sadhak*) whose true face had been lost somewhere beyond time, and who had not become an ascetic by choice. Udaipur was in the condition of some deranged man roaming the roads aimlessly and helplessly, who had simply been left to his fate. The hope of the age had brought him to this condition. Like the long, matted and tangled beard that kept growing on the face over time, it needed only a vigorous clearing of the disorderly bushes, and it was always ready to come into some better, cleaner form. There was no end to the stories churning in its memory.

In Bija Mandal, once again the gong had sounded. For the first time, its sound was going to travel far.

The two of us, talking together, reached another corner of Udaipur, and, going along a stony footpath, arrived at the ruins of an old palace in front. It had been left utterly abandoned, on the brink of collapse — let it survive at its own peril, let it fall at its own peril. Even after centuries, in the inner part of the rubble-filled courtyard, its rooms and verandas were in fairly good condition. Standing on the uneven stones, Dr. Mishra cast a sweeping glance all around the palace. Leaping and jumping, I went some way inside and reached a room which must certainly, in the Paramaras' time, have been the residence of some very important person. On the walls were splendid shelves of carved stone, in which the articles of daily use must have been kept. At some time those stone walls, doors and shelves must have been painted inside with bright colours. But now the floors were badly torn up, where vagrants were free to spread filth. There was no one to stop or check anyone. If anyone wished, he could uproot something and carry it away — break it, dirty it. The heritage of the Paramaras was in a very bad state.

For any sensitive person, this was a very painful obliviousness toward history; seeing which, the two of us, with sad hearts, came down and, taking a round of this palace — which had at some time stood among four elders, encircled by a high protective wall — began to return toward the temple. Here neither had the central government felt the need for protection, nor is it included even among the state government's protected monuments — this we came to know later in this campaign.

This was the very place where we found something that was, for Udaipur, quite ordinary — something on account of which matters were going to travel very far. As soon as we turned left from one bastion (*burj*), we saw a second bastion in a straight line. In front of this bastion, Udaipur's present crowded settlement begins. But between these two bastions we saw something that was quite distinct and attention-grabbing. The old stone protective wall of the palace, about twenty feet high, between the two bastions, was broken in the middle. A long, high cement frame had been made. On it was fitted a big iron gate. A signboard on the wall was declaring it to be private property, on which, along with the khasra and ward numbers, some phone numbers too were written. We did not even pay attention to whose names were written on them. There was no one there of whom we could enquire about this — whose private property is this? Since when? Who lives [here]? But it seemed that some influential family of the village must have settled here. Otherwise, how could this part of an old palace be someone's private property?

Walking along, we took three or four photos with the good-quality camera of our smartphone, and, talking on this very subject, moved on — why has everyone left our archaeological heritage abandoned, to be ruined? Even the panchayats do nothing — whereas, by preserving these, what a fine and profit-earning means this could be for acquainting the new generation with their forebears. In these are the stories of our past. India has suffered so much that the identification of every ruin and every mound will bring us face to face with some truths of the past. But who has the interest to look toward these? This is such a matter in which no one sees any benefit. In any case, for all of us history is a boring sort of subject.

For so many years after Independence, not a single elected representative of any panchayat felt that this was something that ought to be better looked after. In the government machinery there was no one who would even make a proposal on paper about this place and push it forward. To no schoolteacher either did anything appear that could be taken further somewhere. In none of the citizens' minds did the thought arise that he should take at least some small initiative for Udaipur's perishing wealth. It is possible that some correspondence too had taken place; but it had no effect here. Most of the local people think of nothing besides their own livelihood and farming — even those people whose everyday needs have been fulfilled more than others'.

Roaming through the narrow lanes of unplastered stone houses and ancient doorways on both sides, we came back to the temple. On the way, we also sketched an outline of the actual map of Raja Udayaditya's time — how these roads must then have been made, cutting one another at 90 degrees. On these roads there must have been the bustle of chariots, bullock-carts, horses and elephants. Those huge doors, which the security guards must have shut after evening, and, holding torches on the fort walls, they must have kept watch. Separate detachments must have been posted for the palace's security. Every morning there must have been the bustle of worship in the temple. As soon as we came out of the lane, the temple seemed to be smiling before us. The members of the royal family who once set out from the palaces for the rites must have seen it every day exactly like this, with their own eyes — but then it must have been fully decked out. Udayeshvar Mahadev, fragrant in a decoration of fresh flowers brought from the region of Udaipur!

Seeing the temple's architecture from afar, the eyes of Dr. Suresh Mishra — who had come to Udaipur for the first time — were dazzled. On the way back that day, Udaipur alone dominated the subject of our discussion, and we kept talking about the Paramara kings. The period from the ninth to the fourteenth century, during which the Paramaras existed in this region, was itself such a time that the royal dynasties present all over India were carrying out the finest construction work in their kingdoms. From the Cholas far in the south to the Chandelas of Bundelkhand, from the Chauhans in Rajasthan to the Pala kings in Bengal-Bihar, the priceless heritage built by the contribution of generations was shining at its peak. Just as, in a match, runs are scored rapidly in the last over, so, on a war-footing, this construction work was done. After the eleventh century, this "over" was really about to end — as though destiny were constantly inspiring India's countless dynasties in every corner to gather all their energy and all the talent of their craftsmen, and to depart only after fashioning some unmatched specimens of craft for the coming generations. Wherever stone was available, no corner was left [untouched] for India's great creation.

The making of Udaipur too was completed in exactly that same period. It was not even the capital of any kingdom. It was a small place situated five hundred kilometres from Dhar, the capital of the Paramaras, on their border — which certainly had strategic importance in the sense that it touched the territory of the Kalachuri dynasty. Udaipur became an important centre situated on the frontier of the two dynasties because there were sandstone quarries here. Being under the Paramaras, Udaipur had seen itself developing like some mini-capital. Its importance lay in this — that a future emperor of the Paramara dynasty had, in his youth, lived in Udaipur. That crown-prince was Udayaditya, whose coronation the city of Dhara once witnessed — that same city of Dhara which was King Bhoj's beloved city; today's Dhar, the magnificent capital of the Paramaras.

After returning to Bhopal that day, the story of Udaipur took a different turn. We had no idea of this at all. That very evening I looked carefully at all the photographs of Udaipur. I had visited the temple many times before this too, and each time had gone with some guest or other; but, having no one within the settlement of Udaipur, I had gone [into it] rarely. This was the first occasion when someone had thought it right to go first into the lanes and ruins of the settlement rather than the temple. That is why my attention rested on these very photographs of the interior of the settlement — especially on the photographs taken at the ruins of Udaipur's palace; and among these too, on the photographs after that left turn, from where, breaking the wall between the bastions, a gate had been fitted and someone had hung a signboard of his private property. These were only three photographs, taken while walking — one photograph of the ancient wall with a bastion; a second, of the heavy iron closed door in the cement frame; and a third, of that signboard on which private property had been declared.

At first glance it had seemed almost impossible: how, after all, could the neglected ruins of an ancient palace be someone's private property? But in India anything is possible.

Can any part of a thousand-year-old palace — encircled by a high boundary wall and bastions built of strong stones — be someone's private property, one wall of which has been broken to fit a door of cement and iron, and, so that no one may cast this or that glance in this direction, a signboard has been hung on the old wall: "Private Property, Udaipur Palace, Khasra No. 822, Ward No. 141"?

About my fresh experiences of this heritage walk taken with a knower of history like Dr. Suresh Mishra, I wrote that in this settlement of eight thousand people, buildings of a thousand, twelve hundred years still survive to a large extent. The whole town exists on its old design in an uneven condition, where there are walls, doorways, temples, palaces, tanks, stepwells, rooms, walls and lanes built according to the architecture of the Paramara dynasty. Passing through them, it feels as though you have returned to the past and are touring the lost splendour of centuries ago.

This place's fame is from the Neelkantheshwar temple, which was built by Maharaj Udayaditya, who came in the generation after King Bhoj. The name of this town, Udaipur, too is connected with him. In that era, the other important members of the royal family were entrusted with the command of important towns and military posts far from the capital. In a way, this was part of a family training in running the governance of the kingdom, and the heirs must have been made skilled in the affairs of state in just this manner — just as, in the centre [heartland], before becoming emperor, Ashoka's father Bindusara had sent him to Ujjain, making him the governor of Avanti.

It is very possible that Crown-Prince Udayaditya was sent, in his father's time, to Udaipur situated on the border of the Paramaras' kingdom. It may be that Udayaditya himself made the first major plan for the development of this frontier town and, according to the tradition of his lineage, began the finest constructions, which must have continued even after he became king. Otherwise, such temples are there village by village, but the layout of a palace encircled by a vast boundary wall and bastions, within a town protected by a square fort, is only there in this region. It must certainly have been a settlement inhabited before Udayaditya, where the Paramaras' military detachment must have been posted for the protection of the borders. Udayaditya's appointment had awakened the fortune of this settlement, where now something was going to happen that would remain alive in memory even after centuries.

Dr. Suresh Mishra said, with regret, that not only the temple — here the whole town needs to be looked after anew. If these are saved [well and good]; [otherwise] before our very eyes this is turning to dust. Now no foreign invader is doing this ruin; we are responsible for it.

The stone quarries provided the basic material necessary for construction in abundance. As large as the population's extent is, the ancient town's is larger. Every day people come to Udaipur from far and wide. Most come only to have darshan of the 11th-century temple — and that too only for the worship of Mahadev. Coming out of the sanctum, they stare up dumbfounded, craning their necks, at the temple's architecture. If one peers into Udaipur or the surrounding villages encircled by fields, forests and quarries, it is hard to understand: if people have no interest in history, then in what subject do they? They are not running even their farming or businesses in any big way either. A kind of carelessness and gloom hangs equally over all. To them nothing in this seems odd. It is a world apart — cut off from the past, carefree of the present. Even hazy imaginings of the future there are none. For every small and large task, they are dependent on the governments.

Merely writing "Paramar" does not make everyone a Raja Bhoj or Udayaditya!

Right under the nose of the capital, Bhopal, only a hundred and fifty kilometres away, a whole old town, surviving in its thousand-year-old splendour, seemed to be breathing its last. This is an episode 258 years after Raja Udayaditya. This inscription gives us a clear indication of an event when we see the presence of Tughlaq's plunderers in Udaipur. Of course, they must have come not to worship in the temple or to view the splendid sculpture, but only to seize the town and to plunder it. By this time the Paramaras' splendour was ending. Perhaps Udaipur must have been that last city where the people of the Paramara dynasty itself must have kept up the fight to the end, because at Dhar and Mandu they had been surrounded and killed.

After the death of the last Paramara king Mahlakdeva at the hands of a chieftain named Ainul Mulk Multani — who came at the behest of Alauddin Khilji — at Mandu, the bands of Turk-Muslim plunderers who had seized Delhi must have begun to hover up to the other prosperous towns and cities of his kingdom. The real guardians of Udaipur too must one day either have been killed in some battle, or, under some settlement, have left the town and gone away, or have settled somewhere right here. Udaipur, like an orphan, must have been left behind only to await the further bad days coming to its lot. It is certain that the surviving people of the Paramaras' clan must then have been the feudatories there, and they must have had their own strength. The direct combat of Tughlaq's plunderers must have been with them. This is the first named evidence, seen in Udaipur, of damage to a temple by foreign Muslim invaders — although this was not the first Islamic terrorist attack in this region.

First, in 1235, Iltutmish, and after him, in 1291, the plundering armies of Alauddin Khilji had already made bloody pounces in this region. Those who need to know the truth of these attacks must certainly read what Minhaj Siraj and Ziauddin Barani wrote, who were writing the reality of these attacks at that very time. Although we had not gone to Udaipur either with the intention of doing any such thing. Who are we to "do"? In democracy, the sense of doer-ship belongs to the government; it belongs to the leaders and officers. We mere creatures have, for ages, been mere instruments.

The sharp IPS officer Dr. Priyanka Mishra, who had come to Bhopal from Delhi, had also studied history. She had come to Bhopal in the first week of January itself in connection with her office work, and was watching and hearing everything from the very first step of Udaipur's story. She dubbed our pair the pair of Jai and Veeru, who, like *Sholay*, was creating a sensation in Udaipur — although it was nothing of the sort. It was certainly a passion to peer into history and see something new, but there was nothing like fanfare. We had, indeed, gone roaming quietly. We gave her the breaking news, on the phone, of the departure, in the next installment of our heritage walk, to Vidisha, and at the break of dawn we set off. This was my second heritage walk under the guidance of Suresh Mishra.

Now we had reached the district headquarters of Udaipur, which historically has been a prosperous chief city from long before even Udaipur. Vidisha's past takes [one] directly up to Emperor Ashoka, who was married to a girl from here — 2300 years ago. But there is no need to go that far now. We stay around the very clear construction works of the Paramaras.

In the old settlement of today's Vidisha, the most special of all is that very Vijay Mandir, which was built by the Paramara king Narvarman. In the genealogy of the Paramaras, king Narvarman's order comes after king Udayaditya; he was his successor. It is possible that at the time of the construction of Udaipur's Neelkantheshwar Mahadev temple, the young Narvarman lived in Udaipur itself, and that, after taking up the affairs of state in the capital Dhar, his inclination remained toward this region. The vastness of the Vijay Mandir is, in comparison with Udaipur's temple, quite different and magnificent. It must have been several times more magnificent and larger than Udaipur's. Maharaj Narvarman had, indeed, to draw his own bigger line; he had, indeed, to have a construction made vaster and more magnificent than Maharaj Udayaditya's.

Vidisha was a larger and more ancient city than Udaipur, whose known history comfortably takes [one] up to the Mauryas. In the Maurya empire, before becoming emperor, Ashoka had lived in Ujjain. The stupas of Sanchi, near Vidisha, had begun to be built just then. Kalidasa's Meghadutas (cloud-messengers) are seen making the aerial journey of Vidisha. In between, the Gupta period passed, the proofs of which are scattered here and there in this region. When the Paramaras' turn came, they too played their part well. Every century went by composing something or other of the very finest.


The two of us spent quite a lot of time in the Vijay Mandir precinct itself. Govind Saxena stayed with us the whole time and kept hearing and telling of the Udaipur experiences. The news of the heritage walk of us two had been printed in the local newspapers, so several media people kept around us the entire time. They asked about our purpose. Why are we roaming; what do we want to know; what is going to happen in Udaipur; what did we see in Vidisha? — they had many such questions. For the first time, we got such a chance to tell everyone about our intention of the heritage walk of the obscure forts and ruins of Madhya Pradesh.

In the old settlement of Vidisha, the ruins of the Vijay Mandir, surrounded on all sides by dense narrow lanes, are such as to make any sensitive Indian weep. It is such a wound of the India of the Islamic era as is oozing to this day. That temple was first razed to the ground in the attack of Shamsuddin Iltutmish. The contemporary historian Minhaj Siraj wrote the height of this temple of Bhilsa as 105 *gaz* (yards). If you estimate the height of the temple that stood upon the vast foundation of the Vijay Mandir, the height must have been exactly this much.

In my opinion, a signboard of warning ought to be put up right outside here, in the public interest — that persons of weak heart should go to see this monument only at their own risk. The scenes here can disturb you!


After the Vijay Mandir, we roamed in the lanes of old Vidisha, which is called Qila Andar (Inside the Fort). Although now there is not the least trace of any fort there. But amid the pressure of population, peer from anywhere, and the vanished wall of the fort, and the surviving traces of the old buildings inside the fort, will appear writhing, taking their last breaths. The stones of the Raisen Gate are clearly the remains of some broken temple. This was a memorable journey of such a city where, two thousand years ago, a Greek ambassador had come. He, coming to Vidisha, had decided — under the influence of the society and customs here — to accept the Vaishnava religion. That is an immortal episode, worthy of pride for the Vidisha recorded in history, the memory of which is recorded in a stone pillar on the bank of the Betwa river. That pillar is famous by the name of Khamba Baba, which must once have been erected, in the form of a flag-pillar (*garud-stambha*), in front of a temple. Today no trace of the temple remains. But that inscription of Heliodorus can still be read on that pillar today.

Returning from Vidisha, I wrote this memorable heritage walk of a whole tiring day in an altogether different way. On my page, the title of this post of 20 January 2021 was — "The Return of Heliodorus in Vidisha."

2100 years later, suddenly coming to Vidisha, Heliodorus is dumbfounded...

His joy is [there], but his sorrow is more than his joy. The joy is that in the memories of Vidisha he is preserved to this day — otherwise the Taxila, in the capacity of whose ambassador he had come here, there no one is left to take its name. That Taxila, lost somewhere in the ruins near Rawalpindi, is now not even a part of that India. He loves Vidisha, because in Vidisha's memory he is Khamba Baba. When he had come here for the first time, the tales of the Magadha emperor Ashoka and of Pushyamitra Shunga were very fresh. The construction of the stupas at Sanchi was going on. Mahendra and Sanghamitra had come to meet their mother before departing for Sri Lanka. The fragrance of the smoke of the Shungas' sacrificial altars kept the winds fresh. Who would come here and not be enchanted? He too lost his heart to Vidisha — so much that he changed his very religion. He had the formal declaration of it inscribed upon a large pillar, so that history might know it well. Seeing that pillar on the banks of the Vetravati (Betwa), that whole era revolved in Heliodorus's eyes — when his glittering chariots had reached here via Mathura.


Today Heliodorus has returned once again to his beloved city...

But where is that Vidisha? How deep are the marks of the buffets of centuries on the face of this old city! Tired-looking, bewildered and troubled, running and fleeing — a kind of directionlessness, depression, dim aspirations: such a city as has, in its long life, passed through experiences of every sort, and now spends its days in apathy. Like such a neglected old man as, after being used, has been made to sit in the desolate corner of the house. He remembers, again and again, the splendid and honoured periods of his life. Today's generation has neither anything to know nor anything to learn from his bright past. What is there anyway in anyone's stories?

Heliodorus remembered that grand ceremony of Besnagar...

when he had taken initiation into the Vaishnava religion. That splendour-filled peace of Vidisha has been shattered. Yes, in the crowd-filled markets of Besnagar there is the glitter of modernity, but it is faded — like the struggle of light against fog. The fog is of centuries: deep and old. It is not only in the eyes; it has descended deep into the thinking too. In the eyes of the people come from the villages in the hope of good days, there is some hope that yet remains. "Ours

has passed; may the children's tomorrow be set right." Life entangled in the everyday needs. Whether it is Vidisha or Mathura — what difference does it make? Heliodorus is amazed.

While going inside, Heliodorus stands, halted, at the Raisen Gate...

He is looking at the badly-scraped images. On one side someone has smeared green colour and tied cloths. This is the introduction of Islam — a new introduction for Heliodorus. Before coming to Vidisha he had heard this name in Taxila, which is now within the border of a new country. That country is Islamic — such a religion as came into existence far away in Arabia, long after the Vaishnava religion, and very rapidly reached the Raisen Gate, leaving its mark. In the buffets of history, the face of Vidisha has changed. Very fierce storms, striking against the Raisen Gate, have filled the lanes. In the frightful whirlwinds of dust, Vidisha is lost for a moment.

Heliodorus, groping the walls, is in the shadow of a ruin...

Countless broken images, as though rained down from the sky. When the noise of the blasts subsides, before his eyes a vast temple lies scattered in the form of a corpse. Heliodorus has trembled. Someone's sobs are heard. Gathering courage, he moves forward. A heap of rubble is around him. Who is it? He sees the bright eyes of a vast image flowing [with tears]. She is some goddess whose hands and feet have been cut off. Her face has been disfigured. She is hiding her face. Until yesterday she was the centre of the devotion of thousands; today her everything has been ruined. Closing his eyes, Heliodorus sat down on the steps of the Vijay Mandir. A "secular" began telling him the glory of Alamgir [Aurangzeb], who had built a mosque from the rubble.

What happened, who did it, who is it? The images heard the faint voice of Heliodorus...

A person came out from somewhere. He gave his name as Minhaj Siraj. He said — "Sir, nothing will come of sitting here and weeping. The game is out of hand. This was a 105-*gaz*-high temple. It was visible from very far. Delhi's sultan Shamsuddin Iltutmish has annihilated it. They have looted the city and set off toward Ujjain. There they will do the same to the Mahakal temple. For the first time, the Islamic armies


have knocked here for the first time." Saying this, Minhaj vanished. From the other side another bearded person came and gave his name as Ziauddin Barani. Without even being asked, he said — "Sultan Alauddin Khilji has begun looting the city. If you wish for safety, get out of here." Then some third one came and began talking of the loot and plunder in the glory of some sultan who had come to Vidisha from Gujarat. The years of the calendar have hidden in the splashes of blood — 1234, 1291...

Heliodorus quietly set off toward the banks of the Vetravati...

Nothing around is known or recognized. For a moment he feels that the other name of a directionless and distraught crowd is India itself. This is not that India. This is not that Vidisha either. He crossed the river and went far off, and stands before a rock. Something recognized has appeared. Recognizing Vishnu on that vast rock, he felt some relief. Vishnu stands, in his majestic posture, hand on hip, splendidly, in the Varaha (boar) incarnation. The Earth, in the form of a woman, is on his tusk. Vishnu has rescued the Earth drowned in the deluge. He is gazing, unblinking, at this hope-giving scene of his adored Vishnu. In Udayagiri, the 1500-year-old sacred sign of the Gupta emperors, inscribed on stone. The episode of Chandragupta and Dhruvaswamini has come alive.

Heliodorus is taking a bird's-eye view, like Kalidasa's clouds...

This is not that Vidisha. He is not even able to recognize Vidisha. Everything is dream-like. There are countless shadows, running toward who-knows-what destination. There is a harsh political noise, in which there are deceitful slogans. There are some vile faces, alert in some unknown ambush — faces wrapped in speeches somewhere, stuck in files somewhere. Upon whose heads crowns are decked, on their foreheads a thick layer of history's dust has settled. Alas, India! They themselves are today's Mauryas, they themselves the Shungas, they themselves the Guptas, and they themselves the mighty Paramaras. Some Iltutmish, emerging from some lane, and some Khilji, is screaming in some distant lane. The lanes have filled with screams and cries. This is a bad time for Vidisha to weep over its own fate.

Heliodorus sits alone in the spectators' gallery, quietly...

Before one's very eyes, Vidisha has turned into a stage, where the characters come and go one by one. He is, with relish, tasting the savour of the namkeen of love. Kamal's strong boiled tea has refreshed him. Running from the Kanch Mandir, jostled about in the crowded markets, striking against the Raisen Gate, he has reached a heap of stones named Kala Pahad, where the remains of a demolished temple are impatient to come out. Here one need not dig into the ground. Just the turning over of the stones lying on the ground is all that is wanted, and a page of Vidisha's wounded past sits eager to open.

Finally, Heliodorus has come into the garden of sadness. Coming to Vidisha, today he too is sad...


16 February 2021...

This was the day of Vasant Panchami — the holy day of the remembrance and praise of Saraswati, the goddess of knowledge, who was the adored deity of Raja Bhoj. We once again headed for Udaipur, because we were continually getting phone calls. Updates were being received every day. That narrow, six-to-seven-foot, suffocating lane leading up to the temple had been widened by ten feet each on both sides. The front portions of the houses and shops of the people on both sides had been removed. When the *farman* of the Mughals can go into the garbage, then what status do these occupations of six or eight decades have?

Naib Tehsildar Daujiram Ahirwar was in full current. Above him, SDM Rajesh Mehta became the high-tension line. Collector Ankit Jain had become an IAS while studying to be a neurosurgeon. His operation theatre was in Vidisha, from where he was watching Udaipur's online surgery live on the screen. The power-house of the operation was in Bhopal. On Twitter, every necessary update was being sent to the Prime Minister and the Union Tourism Minister. The turning-over, after centuries, of a historic town had, indeed, to be visible far and high up.

The wish to see the changed form of the narrow lane once again drew, on the day of Vasant Panchami, the twin-pair of a knower of history and a seeker to Udaipur. On the way itself, I searched out, on the mobile, an old article of mine. It was centred on Raja Bhoj. In the Saraswati-kanthabharana of Bhoj's capital Dhar, the liveliness of this day's occasion must have been decked out for who knows how long. On my Facebook page I posted that old article. We were on the way to Udaipur. Its state — later. First, on the day of Vasant Panchami, remember Raja Bhoj and his adored Saraswati. This is a twenty-year-old flashback. After all, Udaipur too has a deep relation with Raja Bhoj. Udayaditya became Maharaja only after him. Bhoj must have been well aware of every outline of Udaipur's development. Udayaditya must have prepared himself, under his very protection, along with his rich tastes, for skilled statecraft.


The title of that old post of 16 February 2021 is — "Vasant Panchami and Raja Bhoj."

It is a matter of the year 2000. It was a day in March. In those days I was in Indore. It was the time of the movement that took place for Dhar's Bhojshala. The Hindu Jagran Manch had started a movement over Bhojshala. On the pretext of its coverage for *Naidunia*, I got a chance to peer into the personality of Raja Bhoj. I felt that, instead of table-coverage of the movement sitting in Indore, I should first go and have darshan of the Bhojshala.

That day I reached Dhar. Bhojshala was then under strict police guard. A police jawan stopped me from going in at the splendid door of that building surrounded by an ancient stone boundary wall. When I asked the reason, I got an amazing answer. He said — permission is not there. I introduced my press [credentials] and, expressing my interest in history, requested that there should be no harm in going in for ten minutes and coming back. He said — I cannot do that, because the *mazhab*-people (the religious folk) take objection.

"*Mazhab*-people" means Muslims. Right in front, around the graves, local Muslim families lived in some shanties. Although none of them had stopped me from going up to the door of Bhojshala. In Bhojshala then, Hindus were permitted to go only one day in a year — on Vasant Panchami. Whereas Muslims could come every Friday — for *namaz*. If you are only a seeker of history — neither a Hindu nor a Muslim — then our secular system did not permit you to enter this thousand-year-old building. At the Centre was Atal-ji's government.

In the state it was the second innings of Digvijay Singh's government. The Hindu Jagran Manch had heated up the Bhojshala issue. The argument was one that went down [the throat] — that 52 days a year for them, [but] only one day for us, why?

That day the police jawan did his duty by stopping me. From the main door itself I peered inside with a deep gaze. In front appeared a series of tall pillars. I looked carefully at the stones of the door. Even a blind person could tell, by groping these stones, whether it is a temple or a mosque! Whatever it be, now it was a political tangle. While in newspaper and TV, I covered it on two occasions, when Friday too fell on the day of Vasant Panchami. The crisis that arose before the government was — when should the *namaz* be [held], when the worship? The Hindu Jagran Manch, engaged in splendid preparations for the worship, could not tolerate that, for even one day, anyone should come into the ancient temple of Saraswati in their presence.

On the other side, ordinary Muslims had no concern; but for the government it was a challenge to show the world that the *namaz* too was held. So some Muslim employees working in government departments were brought at noon under strict security and made to stand in the posture of *namaz* on the roof above, and photographs were propagated that the *namaz* had been held without any obstruction. During that, the worship was stopped. A few lathis were wielded on the angry Hindus. Tear-gas shells were fired. As evening fell, everyone breathed a sigh of relief that everything had been dealt with. This happened in Congress's time too, and in the BJP's rule too.


I had then used all my weekly holidays of a year in the study of the Paramaras and Raja Bhoj. I devoured the books available in Indore's archaeology museum and in *Naidunia*'s library. I held several meetings with Dr. Shashikant Bhatt, who did deep research on the Paramaras. The world knows one Bhojshala, and that too because of controversies. During this research, I went to the other two Bhojshalas as well.

The first was in Ujjain, the second in Dhar, and the third in Mandu. Ujjain was the first capital of the Paramaras, which Raja Bhoj took [and shifted] to Dhar — not Dhar, [but] Raja Bhoj's beloved city of Dhara. These splendid buildings, made in Raja Bhoj's time, were Sanskrit schools, where the images of Saraswati too were installed.

Bhoj was not only a king. He was also a deep knower and scholar of many subjects. There was a big, famous council (*parishad*) of scholars of all subjects — grammar, language, literature, drama, dance, music, water management, architecture, town planning, temple construction, vastu — to join which there was competition among scholars from as far as Kashmir. In Bhoj's time, the names of these buildings were not Bhojshala. He was not small-hearted like the rulers of today — that he would build a school in his own name, install a statue at some crossroads, or run schemes of governance. In Bhoj's time their names were — Saraswati-kanthabharana. He had written two works of this name too, and it was his title too.

These Bhoj-era magnificent places of all three places — Ujjain, Dhar and Mandu — Dilawar Khan Ghori turned, together, into similar [structures] in 1401. From the rubble of the Saraswati-kanthabharana itself were made similar precincts surrounded by boundary walls, which are now called Bhojshala today. In Ujjain, that is the foundationless mosque in the Anantpeth locality today. In the royal precinct of Mandu is the tomb of Dilawar Khan Ghori, and about Dhar everyone, of course, knows. Dhar is Raja Bhoj's beloved city of Dhara. There are wonderful details of its splendour in Bhoj's time. What happened in the later centuries is also recorded in Malwa's wounded memory.

The last king of the Paramaras is Mahlakdeva, in whose time Delhi's invader-plunderer Shamsuddin Iltutmish had attacked Malwa. Then all the temples of this region were razed to the ground. In Ujjain, the temple of Mahakal too was looted and broken for the first time just then. Iltutmish took the images of Bhagwan Mahakal and Vikramaditya from Ujjain to Delhi along with the plunder, and had them flung outside a mosque so as to disgrace them every day. Minhaj Siraj testified to this. Ibn Battuta saw, with his own eyes, those images of Ujjain lying outside a mosque in Delhi.

In Vidisha, the Vijay Mandir turned into rubble. The broken temples and shattered images that come out, village after village, giving an introduction to their wounds to this day, tell the stories of those very seven centuries, when such storms were blowing on all sides. In Dhar there are mentions of the coming of Khilji, Mohammad bin Tughlaq, Malik Kafur, Akbar, up to Jahangir. In the very time of the Mughals, the Muslim rulers occupying Gujarat too wreaked plentiful loot and plunder in the Malwa region of Madhya Pradesh.


On the route of the plundering invader campaigns of Delhi's emperors and sultans to the south, unfortunately, Bhoj's city of Dhara too lay. Today it is one more city like all the other wretched cities of the country, which has no relation left with its own rich past. Dilawar Khan Ghori became the first independent sultan of Malwa. This is an event of a hundred-and-a-quarter years before Babur came to India. Hoshang Shah Ghori was his successor — after whose name is Hoshangabad, and who had Bhoj's dam, built near Bhojpur in Bhopal, demolished. This dam created a sea-like spectacle in front of that temple of Bhojpur. It was the Bhimkund, spread over an area of six hundred square kilometres.

We would not remember even the names of the Presidents and Prime Ministers of 70 years in order — but on this side of a thousand years, Raja Bhoj is still shining in our memories. Why? If he had been only a king, to whom would he be remembered? Thousands of kings, sultans, emperors, nizams and nawabs are in the dustbin of history. Bhoj, like an ideal ruler, developed every kind of art in his kingdom. He gave respectful place, around himself, to virtuous people. He had splendid constructions made. He wrote books. He established knowledge-centres like the Saraswati-kanthabharana to shape the new generation.


He lived a small life, and in it too a very small opportunity of rule, in such a way that all the splendour of his time came to an end, but he is even now remembered with respect. If our rulers of today learn even any one thing from him, they can create much that is memorable.

The Bhojshala of Dhar — once an active centre of the Indian knowledge-tradition — stands before us, in the later centuries, having become a smouldering part of the story of the ash and smoke of all India...

Every post was read, on average, by more than ten thousand people each. Before reaching Udaipur, we reached the circuit house of Ganj Basoda. In Udaipur's first heritage walk, the two of us had just come and gone. The past one-and-a-half months had been full of much action. This time we came having announced the full programme beforehand. That door of the enthusiastic youth of Ganj Basoda was awaiting us. SDM Rajesh Mehta gave the update of the action going on in Udaipur — although, administratively, he was not answerable to us; nor did we ask him anything. He knew the subject well. We had come for Udaipur. We did not have to do any worship-and-ablution of Neelkantheshwar Mahadev. Our method of worship is a little different. In this ritual, our chief priest was Dr. Suresh Mishra.

This time, when we set out for Udaipur, the heritage team of Ganj Basoda too came along, which had not yet taken any formal form. For the very first time we met these enthusiastic youths of Ganj Basoda origin. The throbbing history around them had touched their minds. A new introduction to Udaipur had, in the meantime, come to them too. We got many new pieces of information from them too. First on social media and then in the media, Udaipur had knocked on the doors of everyone's heart-and-mind.


We all got off together, for the first time, at that same corner of Udaipur. Far in front, the western door of the temple had begun to be clearly visible. In the narrow lane, ten feet each had been cleared on both sides. Those whose houses' and shops' front portions had been removed were getting fresh construction started. But on the way, on the left, that house was still jutting out into the middle of the road — the one on which we had seen the Jama Masjid signboard hung. Its ten-foot portion stood alone, which had not been touched. Today the local MLA was to begin the construction work of this three-foot-wide road, but he did not come. That very morning, in Satna in Madhya Pradesh, a bus had fallen into some dam, in which 40 people had died. It was a big accident. All the government events had been cancelled. The MLA sahib did not come. Rubble, certainly, was scattered on the road. A government [warning] jeep, sounding its siren, was swaying here and there.

We all went up to the temple on foot. For some time, in the temple itself, with the members of this brand-new heritage team, we kept talking about history around the images. This was our first meeting with these people. They had read about us only in the media. They knew only this much — that in Udaipur, only after the heritage walk of us two did the sudden improvement begin. They were all very happy with this stir, and, for this reason, very enthusiastic that two outsiders had drawn attention to the unheard and unknown facts of local history. They were most of all impressed by this — that what, after all, had compelled an 84-year-old historian to come wandering on these ruins, and to come back to see them again? Twenty years after retirement, how many professors keep their subject alive within them with such energy as everyone saw in Dr. Suresh Mishra? But history was not, for him, a subject limited to a job. If he had studied and taught history, then, after his release from college, he lived that subject too. He writes, on average, one book every year. These are not time-passing poems, story-collections or novels. They are priceless documents of history, emerging from his months of hard work, or collectible translations of old documents, which have been much praised. His readers await his next book.

Coming out of the temple, we were first introduced to Raja Miyan. After coming out of the temple, on the left was his very shop. He did not wait for any government order to remove the old construction at this place, and gave eight feet more space. Dr. Suresh Mishra embraced Raja Miyan right there. The phone-cameras flashed. Everyone captured that memorable moment in photographs. Raja Miyan was the bright example of new Udaipur. He kept telling us about his family for a long time. He felt that now more people than before would come to Udaipur; outsiders would now not be troubled by the narrow lane; they would enter Udaipur with ease. Happy with these changes, Udaipur too had spread out its hem. The lanes were ready for the welcome.


Praveen Sharma took us to the house of Mallu Chand Jain sahib nearby. It was in that same lane, adjoining the temple, which takes us into the throbbing arteries of Udaipur. The broken remains of the temple were visible everywhere, outside and inside his house. In a narrow old room at the front of the house was his small grocery shop. Praveen-ji took us inside. The sight of the inner rooms was ten centuries old. It was exactly like the sanctum of some temple, on which carved stones of splendid images were embedded. Inside there was no image, but there were two more rooms around. This was very old construction work. On tapping the floor-stones of the dark room on the left, they made such sounds as though there were another room below. We inspected the walls and the ceiling in the light of the mobile-torch and came out. Mallu Chand-ji told us that only three Jain families are left in Udaipur.

This house was once buried in a mound. They used to live in the front part. When, for more living space, the clearing of the mound began, these rooms — built of splendidly carved stones — were found made [there]. A great deal of junk was filled in them, which had been sold to some junk-dealer. Praveen Sharma brought, from inside, a piece of an old musical instrument. It was the upper wooden part of some sitar or sarangi, which had survived in the junk of the room buried inside the mound. As a keepsake, with the permission of Jain sahib, he handed it to me right there as a gift. Vasant Panchami is the day of Devi Saraswati. Getting this remnant of a sitar on this day made me extremely happy — as though Devi Saraswati herself had sent some blessing. Sometimes I think that our faith too is strange. What a strange relief it is. I took that instrument in my hand. The small carved wooden pieces that used to bind the strings, whose notes must once have been struck, too were firm in their place.

The goods found inside the house long ago, which Jain sahib had handed over, taking them for junk — who knows what all there must have been in that iron-and-wood? Now this was the only clue. The finding of one instrument was, at the least, giving the news that there must have been the teaching of music, or the presence of knowers of music, here. Certainly, for the praises of Shiva sung in the temple, some group of musicians must have lived here. One reason for the prestige of the art-loving Paramara kings was this too — that they were great lovers of learning. Knowers of all disciplines — language, grammar, poetry, drama, vastu, architecture, astrology, music — used to be around them. Raja Bhoj himself held the status of a scholar among these talented art-experts. In such an atmosphere as that in which Udayaditya must have been raised, around him too there must have been exactly such an atmosphere. It is clearly visible on the huge stones in every nook and cranny of Udaipur. This piece of a sitar, as it were, set Udaipur's memories to music. My joy knew no bounds. It was a very priceless piece. He used the word "survey," which was a low-budget task. He knew that if he talked of excavation and the like, the British government would not put its hand in — the finance people would stall the file. In a slave country, to do digging for the study of history was no work of any benefit for the English government. Yes, of whatever is scattered, a survey could be got done. Cunningham argued that we rule over this country here; this is our duty; we must certainly get this work done.

Cunningham, through his contacts in London, got it pushed so that this work would begin soon in India. Only then, in 1861, was the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) formed. In the very name of this new department, "Survey" came joined, which has remained joined ever since — from after independence to now. Cunningham himself was told — since you have shown the way, now go on ahead. He was made the first Director General of the ASI. What he did to search for India's lost splendour in the forests, mounds and ruins is worth underlining for this reason — that he was not even an Indian. He was English. He was a soldier. In Udaipur there is not Mahadev alone. Before them and after them too, the construction of temples had not stopped. The king Udayaditya, after whose name this city was named, is the 12th king of the Paramara dynasty. He is a kinsman (*bandhu*) of Raja Bhoj. That is, he must have learned and understood the ways-and-manners of statecraft with Raja Bhoj himself; and when Raja Bhoj oversaw his empire from the capital Dhar, Udayaditya must have been sent here, made the feudatory. On the hill adjoining Udaipur — encircled on all sides by a fort — the remains of a fortification are visible even from Google Map. Besides the main temple, the presence of the vast precinct of the palace tells that the kin of the Paramara kings themselves must have lived here for the security of this border. It is possible that at some time, during some war-campaign, Raja Bhoj himself came up to Udaipur. What a time it must have been!

The abundant availability of stone gave the Paramara dynasty the opportunity to erect splendid specimens of architecture. In this work the Paramara kings, as it were, competed with the Chola kings of Tamil Nadu. Everywhere in their kingdom they built matchless temples, palaces, tanks, stepwells, and Sanskrit education-centres. Raja Bhoj's work *Samarangana Sutradhara* is testimony to what high-grade creativity they were scholars who gave patronage. Among the Paramara kings, Bhoj's status is like that of the sun shining among the stars. He had Saraswati-kanthabharanas built in Ujjain, Mandu and Dhar, which were education-centres — of the temple of Saraswati and of all the knowledge-disciplines of their time, including the Sanskrit language. One of the two mosques, the people of Udaipur have been calling "kachahari" (the court) since childhood; their fathers and grandfathers too told just this — that it is a kachahari. People remember that once a signboard of the name "kachahari" too used to be put up. When a kachahari got transformed into a mosque, no one knows. Not merely a mosque, but a Jama Masjid. Going inside, it will seem that on the other side of the splendid double huge pillars and the splendid ventilators carved on a single stone, there is the bustle of King Udayaditya's arrival. He has set out from the palace side. Crossing the decorated square in the middle, he is coming this way.


This is a structure like a Darbar-e-Aam or Darbar-e-Khas, where the king or emperor must have sat with his ministers, guests, or the influential people of the city. People must have come to them with their problems and their information. They must have understood every nicety of the temple's construction, sitting with their engineers and architects, and discussed financial matters. On some festival occasion, the news of the inauguration (of a performance) of a royal dancer (*rajanartaki*) too must have been heard, sitting somewhere here. Matters connected with the common people too must have been presented somewhere here. Messages coming from the capital Dhar too must have been heard here, and from here their replies must have been sent.

The special thing is that this kachahari — or the Jama Masjid of the Muslim brothers — has clearly been a part of the Paramaras' palace, which was once on the right side of the palace, in the four-bigha long-and-wide courtyard encircled by four bastions and twenty-foot-high walls. Before departing from Udaipur, we went toward that Uday Samudra, where a week earlier INTACH's team had already worked and had measured its length as 980 metres. The steps of the splendid ghats of carved stones now descend not into Uday Samudra but into those fields where, in the passing, warming month of March, the wheat crop stood ready for harvest. They are now fertile black-soil fields spread far and wide, where once the vast tank built by Udayaditya struck the banks like a sea. Amit Shukla and Gagan Dubey made detailed videos of this occasion. Here, everywhere, Udaipur was telling some new story.


The carved stones on the banks of Uday Samudra are like the vast reservoirs made on the way to Bhojpur in Bhopal — the stepped stone dams, which had presented the view of the waving sea of the Bhimkund in front of Bhojpur's temple. Mandidip was then an island peeping up in the midst of that reservoir. That island is joined [to this fact] by the name Mandi to this day. That dam of Bhojpur, Hoshang Shah Ghori, the occupier of Mandu, had demolished. It is said that the water accumulated in the five-hundred-year-old dam took several months to empty completely, and that land took several years to dry and become ready for farming. Hoshang Shah was among the invader rulers who occupied [Malwa] after the defeat and death of the Paramaras' last king Mahlakdeva at Mandu. The story of destruction was kept ceaselessly going on.

Dam- and reservoir-construction was the Paramaras' expertise. Wherever vast temples were built, there even vaster reservoirs were built. In the eight months of the dry season, for the load-carriers, businessmen, sadhus, pilgrims and soldier-groups travelling in caravans, and for the thousands of cattle of their chariots and bullock-carts, these very reservoirs came in use at the time of the halt. The city's water-supply was there, of course. In Udaipur, besides Uday Samudra, countless stepwells had been built, where the craftsmen of the Paramaras have made a strong display of their skill.

A reclining image on Uday Samudra is a piece of who-knows-what story. This image is considered that of a royal dancer, who is also called Bedni Mata. About this vermilion-smeared image, a popular story among the local people is that she had to dance on a thin thread tied across the reservoir. Once someone broke that thread, and that dancer could not complete the dance. She died from falling. It is her samadhi. We do not know since when this story is current. Who was that royal dancer, in whose kingdom she was, whether she is some character of Udayaditya's golden period or of some later feudatory's time — we know nothing about this. But Dr. Narayan Vyas, after looking at that image carefully, on the way back told [us] that it is the image of a yaksha. If it is a yaksha's image, then with which episode of the royal dancer was it connected and installed here? Every story had centuries-old layers, which were so tangled and wrapped up with one another that to separate them is difficult.

If any character of a royal dancer too has been preserved in the memories of Udaipur, then certainly this is an important link of Udaipur's splendour. In the city from which the Paramara dynasty got a great ruler, a royal dancer must have been an interesting character of Udaipur's bright story — some beloved character of Maharaj Udayaditya; otherwise, besides those unnamed *satis*, how many such women are there who have survived in Udaipur's memories? It is very possible that when Udayaditya, becoming king, departed for the capital Dhar, then this beloved character of his did not move from her beloved city Udaipur, and accepted staying right here. Staying right here, she must have always expressed good wishes for her king; and when the construction of the temple was completed, she must have awaited the king's chariots at the time of the flag-hoisting ceremony of 30 March 1080. That liveliness of Udaipur's palaces too was not the last. Until two-and-a-half centuries later, Udaipur kept shining just like this.


During the fierce attacks of Delhi's Turk occupier Shamsuddin Iltutmish, and Khilji's loot, on Vidisha, very bad news must certainly have come to frighten Udaipur; but Udaipur remained untouched. In those attacks, certainly, Udaipur's warriors, along with the Paramaras' army, must have clashed with the bands of these two plunderers, and must have succeeded in saving their city.

Incidentally, I would like to mention here the Turks' Marmaray tunnel project. Work on it started in 2004. A large part of a 76-kilometre-long railway line had to pass through a deepest tunnel, which began to be built 50 metres under water in Istanbul's Marmara bay. Obviously, this work was that of the top-grade civil engineers of the Turks' railways, and it was they who were doing it with full zeal. But some strange structures on a sea-shore gave this project another reason altogether to become a subject of discussion throughout the world.

Had such a development project been taking shape in India, take it as settled that everything would have been disposed of right there — as happened a few months ago in the Mahakal temple precinct in the Ujjain city of Madhya Pradesh itself. There, the structures of an old temple came out in the digging, but no one took them seriously. Often, in the big-budget — and correspondingly fat-commission — works, any such historical discovery becomes an obstacle. In a country like India, the decisive officers can sacrifice, a thousand times over, every kind of discovery that makes new revelations of history — [but] the fat budget, not even once. No one knows which path of history those doors in the Mahakal temple's precinct were indicating inward, and now we will never come to know what was there.


In Turkey, neither was this facility [of hiding] before the officers, nor were the Turkish officers desirous of any such facility. In that mega railway project, as soon as something old and priceless came out, the country's best exploration teams were deployed. From the director of the Istanbul Museum to Canada's geologists, marine-archaeology and flora-fauna experts, and expert historians of the remains of the third-and-fourth-century empires, kept joining one by one. New and newer pages of ancient Turkey's splendid past kept opening. These were the remains of a prosperous city from much before the coming of the storms of Islam in Turkey. After minutely-done digging and cleaning, 35 thousand objects, and countless pieces of animal bones, were sorted and set apart. Among these, a hundred bones were of humans. But this was nothing before that splendid discovery which came out on this coastal land equal to 10 football fields. These were the remains of 37 vast, centuries-old ships. For the first time, parts of the oldest ship too were found.

When the aerial view of the whole area was seen minutely from a drone camera, the special pattern of woods emerging above the ground drew everyone's attention. When the ground around those woods was dug, it was learned that they were the surviving parts of the very upper structures of ships, and that whole ships had stood firm within the ground, bearing the blows of time along with earth and water. Turkey's government called this a rescue operation. They were digging the ground to save a part of their history. This was no ordinary kind of excavation. Everything had to be extracted from here and saved forever, because the tunnel being built for public transport was later going to end all possibilities of any kind of rescue.

It was said that, for the first time in a thousand years, modern Istanbul was opening its past. In Istanbul — the choice of tourists worldwide — this project of the necessary fast-paced movement for ten lakh people every day had become, in other senses, more valuable, and there was no scope to move forward by ignoring it or by burying the archaeological evidence. No one could even think, "Why should time be wasted in digging up buried corpses to complete the tunnel work?" For them, those were not buried corpses. For them, whatever was buried in the ground, bringing it forward was the most important work. History is the most valuable [thing].

The Turks used modern technology from all over the world and put a whole team of experts down on this site. They left no scope for knowing something new from any corner. To get such remains from any corner of any city with an ancient history is not such a thing as one should be astonished at. So this team working in Istanbul was not surprised. But one question certainly stood before everyone. After all, what had happened here, that whatever was where, stayed right there?


During the digging, on descending two or three metres below the ground, it was seen that the layers of earth are wavy. The battered skeletons of many kinds of animals, including horses and camels, too were saying that they had not died their own [natural] death. Certainly something had happened here that had wrecked everything in the blink of an eye. Now, after centuries, from these suppressed layers, the truth was coming forth. The geologists said that these were the very proofs of a tsunami — born due to a big earthquake in the early seventh century — which had stayed alive within the ground. For the people of Istanbul, this was an interesting discovery.

After everything became clear, finally, through digital technology, a 3D model of the old, real era was made. For this, on that vast landmass of the seacoast, joining the pieces of old walls and bastions visible far and wide until now, the old-era harbour was brought alive on the screen. It was a splendid historical harbour — an ancient port-place, where the anchors of ships bringing goods loaded from far and wide were dropped, and the ships departing from here reached distant countries. The shapes in which those woods peeped above the ground and were found joined below with their original ships — those shapes were prepared again into ships of their probable original size. This was a vessel of the Byzantine empire, and in that era Istanbul's splendour was at its peak. It was the Ottomans (Usmanis) who had occupied it, and then a country's original identity began to disappear. It stood, as a Muslim country, in the twentieth century. This discovery connected with the real past took six years. But what of time — its very destiny is that it will pass. More important than time was this work of a big dream. Priceless things are not found easily anyway. What is more valuable than history? — provided you know its value.

Turkey knew its value; so, for the sake of a splendid unheard, untold, unknown story connected with its past, it spent six years and crores of rupees over and above. Malcolm reminded [readers] of the status of many such Indian kings of his time, whose descendants have been riding the wave of today's political powers. But about the Paramaras he mentioned everywhere with respect. He says that in the history of Central India, the Panwar Rajputs are considered more honourable than the other dynasties. But they were defeated by the Muslims, and long ago their kingdom ended. Later, a chieftain of this line got established in his ancestors' capital, Dhar; but he came as an attendant of a Maratha king. When his king was in the Deccan, then his family ate food with Maratha Shudras and established marriage relations with them. The Panwars of Dhar consider themselves southern, and others too consider them thus. But many Rajputs of that line, and many other less-notable lines, believe that their ancestors were of Hindustan.

In the era of the Rajputs' decline, John Malcolm added lustre to the glory of the Paramaras, at a time when the state of the other Rajput castes was extremely bad. The conditions worthy of the dynasties living with honour had been gradually finished off. This is a truth of centuries before the coming of the English. We know better that the merciless followers of Islam, the Khiljis, had made it their primary policy to make the Hindus impoverished to such an extent that they would not even get the leisure to scratch their heads; that their women would wander door to door at the Muslims'; that they would be seen begging on the roads. As far as their power could reach, Khilji's advisers, staying in Delhi, had this actually carried out. The scholars (*ulema*) of Islam declared the oppressive policies against the Hindus to be in accordance with religion, and made Khilji Prophet-like for the deadly and humiliating treatment of the *kafirs*.


John Malcolm described the state of Central India's Rajputs five hundred years later in these words — "The greater part of the military Rajputs of Central India is sunk in a life of sensual indulgence, and, according to their status, sunk in as vile habits as possible. They especially remain sunk in the intoxication of strong liquor and opium. They make arbitrary use of liquid and dry opium. Because of its excessive use, they commit unimaginable atrocities. Their women too have the habit of eating opium; they give opium even to newborn infants. But beyond this, Malcolm comes to the psychological cause of these habits of the Rajputs. He writes that it seems as though, in the society of which they were once, for a long time, the chiefs, now, feeling themselves fallen and unimportant in that society, they seek relief — if not from the decline, then from the emptiness of mind that is in their real condition — through the dreams given by this intoxicating thing.

The translation of this diary of John Malcolm, Dr. Suresh Mishra did two hundred years after its original writing. In 1832 his diary had come out in print in English. Who knows — the last memories of the state-rule may bring [us] right up to Udaipur, or in their memories Udaipur too may be found in some form or other. In joining together the broken, interesting links of history, this initiative can surely be of some use.

Malcolm also shows us the exceptions to this character of the Rajputs. Around him there are several such examples. Seeing them, he says that many have made brave attempts to resist oppression and have proved themselves worthy of the lineage of Ramchandra. Even now there are among them many people of great ability. ### The Meanings of Udaipur

Is it merely a temple of carved stones, of the kind India is full of, which has by luck survived, and for which we can now do nothing — whatever is to be done being only for the governments to do? Are these such monuments that we common people have no concern with? I think that Udaipur is a meaningful purpose for us. For me, every such heritage around me is a big question — and also an answer.

Udaipur is a symbol of great India's cultural identity, and if it has survived — even if only by luck — then the work of keeping this priceless heritage safe in its original form, and of tending to it, is no one else's. Nor is it merely that of the residents of Udaipur's panchayat. It is that of every citizen of this region.

Udaipur is such a creation of our creative great ancestors that even today it reminds [us] of our relationship with them. Those countless inscriptions are the documents engraved upon the very stones of that relationship. Pandit Shringavas and Pandit Mahipal are saying something to us the very moment we enter the temple.

Udaipur is a poem written on stones, which the Paramara kings sang in praise of their adored Lord Shiva. In composing this praise, three years' time was taken, and thousands of artisans, having come from who-knows-where, had displayed the very best of their art.


Udaipur is a monument of India's architecture. It is not merely a temple, whose only responsibility upon us is the worship of the Shiva enshrined in its sanctum, and nothing more. It is a proof of the imaginativeness of India's devotees of art. It is also an introduction to India's art-loving society.

Udaipur is also a proof of India's prosperous economic heritage, for such vast creations simply cannot be the mark of a destitute country. If King Udayaditya had the Udayeshvar temple built, then the common populace of Udaipur too contributed many creations here — and only an economically robust society could do such a thing.

The meaning of Udaipur is also to listen to those tales-of-suffering of India that have passed over us in the form of a long, dark epoch. This settlement, which appears desolate today, saw the finest lights that came into India's lap — yet it did not remain untouched by the footfall of countless darknesses either.

Udaipur is a throbbing proof of India's indomitable willpower — a direct testimony to our industrious and valorous ancestors. It must truly have been a land of heroes, one that for centuries confronted the enemies hovering over its head, and each time they must have emerged victorious; otherwise Udaipur could not have survived those terrible blows of the medieval age. What had happened to the Vijay Mandir in Vidisha?

Udaipur could today have become the emblem of our freedom, but unfortunately there has been an unforgivable delay in this. The country did become free, but it could not add the elements of a cultural renaissance to our consciousness of freedom, and we wasted several decades in vain. In the hope of its good days, Udaipur kept standing, sad.

The redemption of Udaipur is not merely the superficial polishing of an ancient temple or settlement and, by this show, the attracting of others. The spread of tourism is a material initiative, which ought to happen without delay. But if Udaipur's call could not touch our soul, then these efforts too would remain superficial — and such calls are, even now, writhing to come out from the ground in India's villages, towns and far-flung areas.

Udaipur is the inspiration of ever-new creation, and a message of the right and collective good-use of every moment of life that falls to our share. What we should create with our means, and where we should invest our wealth — about this, Udaipur stands present like a guidance. Something that can become eternal in this world of towns.

Udaipur is shaking the historians and archaeologists [by the shoulders], [urging] that they leave books and files, come once, and — halting — look attentively at every corner. Many new stories lie buried in its womb. Countless pages are fluttering. There, the cleaning of the mounds is necessary. The excavation of the ruins is necessary. From every place, history is peeping and peeping [at us].

When Udaipur saw that, around it, nothing remained but to grow hopeless, then — since who knows when — it has been screaming into the ears of the sleeping power-holders. Earlier it had heard the sounds of the warriors' arms *[the source reads "अक्षों" — unclear in source]*, and that very uproar had then turned into electoral noise. But in these victory-processions there remained no possibility of Udaipur's rising (*uday*). First, centuries passed. Then decades too went by.

Udaipur is in search of a noble human being like an Alexander Cunningham — be he an engineer or an army officer, a doctor or a teacher, a leader or a secretary, a businessman or a farmer, an official or a broker *["अलावा" is unclear in source]*. But one who is full of curiosity and who, going beyond all his own claims, both hears the voice of the past himself and makes others throughout the world hear it too.

Udaipur is but a waiting — a long, endless sort of wait. Udaipur's lanes await the footfalls of those school-and-college students who show the courage to peer into the tunnels of India's past. There is much to tell here that is more factual than the stories of grandmothers. In these stories are the tales of many royal dynasties of kings.

Udaipur is such a fertile subject-land for imaginative litterateurs, writers and poets that there is no reckoning of it. For the composition of novels, plays, stories and songs, there are many threads here. Udaipur is eager that someone come, take hold of these threads, and — reaching somewhere — tell how far back its imaginativeness carried him. And there is not just one such Udaipur. Around us there are countless such Udaipurs, with which we do not have even a distant salutation.

In the end, Udaipur is an invitation. A respectful invitation of the past...


## The Paramaras' Inscriptions upon the Stones


30 March 2021...

**941-year-old breaking news!**

The oldest breaking news coming from Udaipur's side is right here. This news is, as of today, 941 years old. It was written in six lines on a stone. It was inscribed on the right-hand side of the temple's east-facing *[the source reads "पूरे," apparently for "पूर्व" — east]* doorway. In it is recorded Vaishakh Sudi 7, Vikram Samvat 1137. According to the English calendar, this date works out to 30 March 1080. A news-item of six lines, once upon a time, is now a historical document — about which most people know nothing.


Not known, because for us history is a boring subject. Our priorities lie in wealth (*arth*). History may teach the meanings (*arth*) of life, but the glittering wealth of the mint's hard-struck coin does not come out of it. In a society oblivious of history, we do the talk of the pride, prestige and glory of our great culture while seated in drawing rooms, or in the verbosity of speeches on stages — and roam about with necks stiffly raised at the very outset of being "nationalist." Inside that stiffened neck, our consciousness keeps its vulture-gaze fixed the whole time upon high-package jobs, trades-and-professions, and earnings. Whereas the past, at every moment, makes silent gestures to us. These gestures are extremely precious — like this breaking news recorded upon the stones of Udaipur!

In this news is the most important information about the temple's flag-hoisting ceremony (*dhvajarohan*) on this very day — that is, 30 March. Among the dozen inscriptions found in Udaipur, this one is the most important because it is of 1080 — the oldest, of Udayaditya's time. In keeping with the inscription-tradition — like the print-line or byline printed in today's newspapers — the name of the gentleman who "broke" this news is also recorded in it. He is Pandit Mahipal, the illustrious son of Pandit Shringavas, who is telling of the flag-hoisting ceremony in the temple. Certainly, on the main entrance-gate of so grand a temple, his name would have been inscribed only with the permission of Maharaja Udayaditya.

We can only imagine the temple's grandeur and the scene of Udaipur at that time — what would have been happening? One can only guess at the splendour of this city that gave the Paramara dynasty a worthy ruler. What days must that Udaipur — now lost among narrow lanes, splendour-less and wretched — have seen then! The collapsing ruins of the royal palace are the most magnificent testimony of that glorious time.

What a festival it must have been, for the flag-hoisting ceremony! Around the temple, the scene then would not at all have been like today's. On the open, wide roads all around, there would have been the bustle of chariots. The palace too would have been specially adorned. From the presence of King Udayaditya, it is certain that from all over the kingdom many distinguished guests — creators, singers, musicians, nobles (*shreshtha*), feudatories (*samant*) and rulers — would surely have come. This temple, which took more than twenty years to be built and made ready, must, even while under construction, have been a subject of discussion far and wide; and at the time of the flag-hoisting, art-loving guests from far and near must have seen with their own eyes what the might and creativity of the Paramaras is called. Pandit Mahipal too must have been a personage of high standing, whose name, on this occasion, was recorded along with his father's in the six inscribed lines.


**What is our relationship with it?**

**Why should we pay attention to these?**

**Are they merely inscriptions recorded upon lifeless stones?**

No, no, no. They are not such inscriptions recorded on lifeless stones that we ought to ignore. In these lines is the call of our ancestors. These great works of construction were done by our creative and prosperous forebears. They were knowers of Vastu, knowers of mathematics, artisans of the highest order. Over the developmental course of centuries they had fashioned this splendour. Upon the temple's walls they had, before departing, composed the very society of their own time. Whether we know anything else or not, we ought to have known about these in great detail.

It is the fault of the governments of independent India that we do not know all this, not even superficially. India's Department of History, Culture and Archaeology ought to have been activated in top gear on 15 August 1947, and, with the help of experts from all over the world, the tending of every ruined heritage ought to have begun. Every inscription was to be read.

Children ought to have been given the information of every attack by foreign invaders upon these ancient points-of-pride. India's true history ought to have been taught to every child in school itself — even if he later became a doctor, engineer, leader, farmer or businessman. Because this was not done, the heritage in every nook and corner of India kept going toward the brink of ruin *[the source reads "बरबादी के कारण पर," apparently for "कगार पर" — brink; unclear in source]*. We taught children only the tricks of earning money. The misdirected generation of a money-worshipping *["पारंपंडी" — unclear in source]* society, which cheers on account of earning money, has become cut off from its past — and it is not cut off just idly. I often repeat: what kind of India did the author of *The Discovery of India* discover?

In Udaipur, that six-line inscription is gently awakening us. In our society — which glorifies the birthdays and death-days of two-penny leaders by printing full-page advertisements — the day of 30 March went on passing in anonymity. This day is important because, in the past one thousand years, countless such pieces of our evidence have been destroyed. If Nalanda, Vikramshila and Vijayanagara were burnt to ash, then we do not know how many writings, how many inscriptions, and how many manuscripts were reduced to ashes there. What was written in them? Much was written in them. In Udaipur alone, INTACH *[the source reads "इटैक"]* has traced more than a hundred inscriptions. But neither do the people of Udaipur know, nor those of Vidisha.


### What is in the oldest inscription?

According to the young historian Santosh Kumar Verma, this inscription is of Vikram Samvat 1137, Vaishakh Sudi 7. In it the day of the week is not mentioned. In it, along with the sun's transit (*sankraman*), the *karana* is mentioned, but its name is not given. According to the mathematical calculation done on the basis of Vikram Samvat, this date comes to Monday, 30 March 1080 CE. As of 30 March 2021, 941 years have been completed. He tells us that this date-testing has been carried out by experts in the past as well. In keeping with the inscription-writing tradition of that time, the name of the writer of these lines is also recorded. This is the great composition of Pandit Mahipal, son of Pandit Shringavas — in today's language, a single-column news-item, which has stood inscribed in the temple for nine centuries.

Santosh Kumar Verma is an assistant professor of history at the Girls' College of Ganj Basoda itself, whose Facebook page I got the chance to visit when the fresh tremors of Udaipur, on the Richter scale of history, reached him. After I returned from Udaipur on the day of Mahashivratri, Deepak Sharma of Pragya Pravah sent me the link to his fresh post. These posts give splendid information on the inscriptions of Udaipur. He has done a fine scrutiny of the inscriptions read so far. Not just translation, but — with very good analysis, comparing them with other inscriptions of the Paramaras' own time — he has made a relevant effort to understand, and to explain, that thousand-year-old era.


We had met Dr. P.K. Mishra, the Regional Director of the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), because most of the inscriptions are in the temple itself, and the temple is under the aegis of the ASI. You enter the temple only by [passing] and touching the countless inscriptions on both sides of the temple's eastern entrance-gate. Among these, in the very upper corner on the left side, in one inscription you can clearly see the name "Shriman Udayadityanripati" (श्रीमान उदयादित्यनृपति).

Amid this very stir of Udaipur, Santosh Kumar Verma began a vigorous series about these inscriptions on his Facebook page. He wrote — "In the records of the Paramaras, usually only Vikram Samvat is written. Even if Shak Samvat has been used somewhere, it will appear only after the marking of the Vikram Samvat. The importance of this inscription is the greatest, because the date recorded in it is the first recorded date of the reign of Maharaja Udayaditya. In the other inscriptions, this is the one deficiency."

Now let us see what has been written and left for us in this most important, mere six-line inscription of Udaipur.


The script of this Sanskrit-language writing is the Nagari script of the eleventh century. A prose-dominant style has been used in it. In it, one shloka is in the Anushtubh metre, and the rest is in prose. This writing gives us the account, too, of two characters who are neither Paramaras nor of the royal dynasty. Nor are they priests. One name is of the composer of this inscription; the second, of his revered father. The composer of this writing is Pandit Mahipala, the beloved son (*chiranjeev*) of Pandit Shringavas. The sixth and last line of this very brief writing—

—is an important "live" piece of information. It is written that, on the said date, the auspicious act of flag-hoisting upon the Shri Udayeshvardev temple was accomplished by Maharaja Udayaditya. The present temple's name has been written as "the temple of Udayeshvar Dev." When, in Dr. Suresh Mishra's company, I had begun writing on the stir of Udaipur, then, with his consent, it had been decided that if this came forth in the form of some document, we would dedicate it to our ancestor Pandit Mahipal, son of Pandit Shringavas.

Let us see what the original text of the inscription, and its translation, tell us—


> **स्वस्ति । एकच्छत्रां करोतु क्षमामुदयदित्य भूपति ॥ (1)**
>
> *Translation —* Svasti (Hail). May King Udayadity make the earth [united] under a single umbrella (i.e., one sovereignty).

> **इत्याहां सिद्धिं वेदं शंसामः सर्वतो नृप ॥ (2)**
>
> *Translation —* O King, thus do we extol, on all sides, the one who bestows such accomplishment (*siddhi*)—

> **क्षमासि(शि) रसि भूत्या स क्षमाभूधरस्तु ॥ रवि संक्रांति (3)**
>
> *Translation —* (Now) may he acquire glory from the earth. The Sun's transit (*Ravi Sankranti*)—

> **करणं(णम) ॥ श्लोकोयं पंडित श्री श्रृंगवाससूनोः ॥ (4)**
>
> *Translation —* —of the *karana*. This shloka is of the son of Pandit Shri Shringavas—

> **पंडित श्री महिपालस्य ॥ संवत् 1137 वैसा(शा)ख सुदि ७ (5)**
>
> *Translation —* —[the son being] Pandit Shri Mahipal. Samvat 1137, Vaishakh Sudi 7.

> **श्रीमुदयेश्व(श्र)र देवस्य ध्वजारोहः संपूर्णः ॥ मंगलं महाश्रीः ॥ (6)**
>
> *Translation —* The flag-hoisting of Shrimat Udayeshvar Dev was completed. May Mahalakshmi (*Maha-Shri*) be the bestower of auspiciousness.


It is not as though no one knew about this inscription before. It has been in the ASI's records for quite a long time. I could never understand this: if the inscriptions written on stones in such far-flung areas had [already] been read, then for whom were they read? Why should they remain locked up in the ASI's government documents? Are they not the sort of thing common people ought to know? Should not, first of all, the people of Udaipur know what has been written and left there by the hands of their forebears?

In the temple's vast precinct, King Udayadity's planners have perhaps left, in advance, quite a lot of space for this very purpose — so that there, [everything] about these inscriptions could be set out before all. There, the translations of all those inscriptions, in Hindi and English, ought to have been mounted in letters so large and clear that even a sightless person could all but make them out from afar — [showing] what our ancestors have written and left for us in them. Better still, they ought to have been carved, in Hindi and English, on that same stone dug from Udaipur's quarries out of which these temples were built. These are such trifling matters that, for them, the governments do not even need to take separate policy decisions. Everything is ready; only, the thought does not enter the mind. *If but the wick would catch, the thing would be done!* *["बची जले तो बात बने" — apparently for "बत्ती/बाती जले तो..." (if the lamp were to light up); rendered idiomatically]* In any case, among the two or four requests we made to Dr. P.K. Mishra, the request to display the inscriptions' translations right there was the very first. Even so, our insistence was that he should first go and see, and only then take any decisions. It is possible that some new idea of doing something new and big might come to his mind right there.


Before moving on, let us see since when this inscription has been kept locked in the ASI's vault, lest anyone see it or anyone come to know of it. First of all, Major General Alexander Cunningham mentioned it in the *Archaeological Survey of India Report*, Volume 10, page 83. This was first printed as long ago as 1880. The famous historian Devadatta Ramakrishna Bhandarkar gave a description of this inscription on page 22 of the *Progress Report of the Archaeological Survey, Western Circle, 1913–14*. Apart from this, M.B. Garde mentions it in the *Annual Report of the Archaeological Department in Gwalior State, Samvat 1974*. This means that for a hundred and twenty-five years it has been within our knowledge what is written here — but neither do the village people know anything, nor the local officers, nor do the tourists who come every day know anything about them. So for whom, after all, are these governmental exercises conducted? Merely to fill the belly of files? For paper formality? Why does history not throb within us? Have we become utterly dead?


### What was the relationship between Maharaja Bhoj and Udayaditya?

In yet another inscription of the Udaipur temple itself, the date Vikram Samvat 1116 and Shak Samvat 981 is inscribed. According to the English calendar, this year is 1059 CE. This is a rather large writing. This inscription — of twenty-two lines in all, and teeming with a plethora of errors — has not only impure language and grammar, but has deficiencies from the standpoint of craft too. These are the sort of reasons that make a meaningful translation difficult. This one too, like the Paramaras' other inscriptions, begins with the worship of Shiva, and then goes on to begin telling about Udayaditya. Every inscription gives some new information; makes some different gesture. This one casts new light upon the relationships that appear vague in the mist of the time between Bhoj and Udayaditya.


In its second shloka is — "The illustrious Pawar (Paramar) dynasty's king, a brave warrior, became a learned king who established the Malava kingdom and who punished the sinners. From him [came] a famous son, endowed with all virtues — a god-devotee named Gandal (Gondal), a giver (*dana*), an enjoyer (*bhokta*), discerning, and conqueror of all enemies, who became renowned by his fame."

In the third shloka is — "His son Pata (Jnata), by churning the enemy armies (*arivala-mathan*), obtained the kingdom, and won fame in keeping with the ancestors of the lineage. Upon the earthly sphere, going to Madhyadesh Malava, he attained renown; of a movement conforming to [his] branch, a god-worshipper, having performed religious austerity (*dharma-tapasya*), he built temples furnished with water-reservoirs."

The fifth shloka says — "A great giver of gold and grain to the Brahmins, having deemed Udayaditya worthy, he made [him] king, and amassed fame."


Verma pauses here to analyze. He says that the mention of this 'Pata' or 'Jnata' as the father of Udayaditya is a point that makes new gestures. One meaning of this is that Udayaditya was not coming down from the lineage-tradition of Sindhuraj, but was related to a junior branch (*avara-shakha*) of the Paramara dynasty. On this basis it may be supposed that Udayaditya possibly ruled in the eastern part of Malwa as a feudatory-lord (*samantadhipati*) under Maharaja Bhoj. Within this region lay Vidisha too. It must have been then that Udaipur began to develop as an important centre under it.

It may also be that when the combined armies of the Chalukyas of Kalyani and the Karnatas attacked the capital Dhara and, around 1070 CE, killed in battle Bhojdev's son and successor Jaisingh I — then, in such a situation, in the political emptiness suddenly created, Udayaditya may have come to the throne. This question is even more important: what was Udayaditya's relationship with King Bhoj? There are some writings that give answers to this question. Such creative kings, ruling in every corner of a vast empire, had countless temples built. About them, these inscriptions can prove helpful in joining the links. For a clear picture of Udaipur, this too is a necessary task.


One more such link joins in, from the Shorgadh inscription of Udayaditya. In its fifth and sixth lines it is said that: "Parama-bhattaraka Maharajadhiraja Parameshvara Shri Bhojdev's *padanudhyata* [one who meditates at his feet], Parama-bhattaraka Maharajadhiraja Parameshvara Shri Udayadityadeva, [is] well (*kushali*)."

The word *padanudhyata* is like a speed-breaker. In the Gupta period, earlier historians believed that the word '*tat-padanudhyata*' speaks directly of the successor — as, for instance, Chandragupta I, Samudragupta, Chandragupta II Vikramaditya, Kumaragupta, Skandagupta, and, further along the lineage-tradition, every later ruler addresses himself as the *tat-padanudhyata* of his predecessor.

But in the writings of Kakanadabot (Sanchi) and Udayagiri, respectively, Amrakardava *[the source reads "आत्रकादवर्द" — garbled]* and Virasena *["शाब" is unclear in source]* — who were the emperor's trusted commanders — were called the *tat-padanudhyata* of Chandragupta II Vikramaditya. From this it was concluded that the literal meaning, "meditating upon the feet," is more apt. This is a eulogy of praise, which has nothing to do with succession. Therefore, here, the claim — from the Shorgadh inscription — that Udayaditya received the succession directly from Bhoj is weakened.


In the famous Udaipur Prashasti there is the most detailed description of the Paramara dynasty, up to Udayaditya. This writing was itself got prepared by Udayaditya. Look at the 21st shloka—


> **तत्रादित्य प्रतापे गतवति सदनं स्वरीणां भर्गभक्तो व्यासो धरेव धात्री ।**
> **रिपुतिमिर भैरमौ ललोकस्तदाभूत् ।**
> **विश्वस्तांगो निहत्योद्विपुतिभिरभरं खड्गदंडासुजाल रन्यो**
> **भार्वानिवोधन्युति मुदित जनामोदयादित्यदेवः ॥ 21 ॥**
>
> *[Sanskrit as in source; the OCR contains numerous errors.]*
>
> That is — "The Shiva-devotee (Bhojdev), whose splendour was like the sun, having departed to heaven — when the whole earth, like the city of Dhara, became pervaded by the darkness in the form of enemies, and his traditional warriors grew weak — then, like the sun, Udayadityadev, destroying the risen enemy-darkness with the rays of his sword-staff (*khadga-danda*), and gladdening the minds of all people, rose (*udit*)."


Look at shloka 32 of the Nagpur Prashasti of Narvarman, the successor of Udayaditya—

> **तस्मिन्वामसव व(ब)न्धुतामुपगते राज्ये च कुल्याकुले भगनस्वामिनि तस्य**
> **व(ब)न्धुरुदयादित्यो भवद्भूपतिः ।**
> **येनोद्भूत्य महार्णवोपममिलत्कर्णाट कर्ण प्रभृत्युर्वीपालकदर्शिता भूमिमा**
> **श्रीमद्वराहपतिः ॥ 32 ॥**
>
> *[Sanskrit as in source; the OCR contains numerous errors.]*
>
> That is — "On that one's (Bhojdev's) attaining the kinship of Indra (i.e., passing away), and the kinsmen (*kautumbik*) of the kingdom's lord being immersed in the water of conflict, his kinsman (*bandhu*) Udayaditya became king — who, having arisen, rescued this earth (tormented jointly, like the great ocean, by kings such as the Karnata, Karna, and the rest) and thereby imitated the Varaha (Boar) avatar."

In this, Udayaditya is described as the "*bandhu*" (kinsman) of Bhojdev. This is a word worthy of note. In the Dharmashastra there are three kinds of *bandhu* — *atma-bandhu*, *pitri-bandhu*, and *matri-bandhu*. Besides these, relatives, brethren-of-community, connections, and family relatives too are called *bandhu*.


The Nagpur inscription is that of King Narvarman, son of King Udayaditya, who, after Udayaditya, became the next king of the Paramara dynasty in 1094 and ruled until 1133 CE. In it is the complete information of the Paramara dynasty up to Narvarman — who came to the throne after whom. In the 56th shloka it is clear that this prashasti was composed by King Narvarman himself. Centuries later, this is the question: why did King Narvarman keep so blurred the relationship between his father, along with his grandfather, and Maharaja Bhoj — the greatest in the lineage-tradition? One argument is that King Narvarman knew everything about the relationship of his father and grandfather, and so, naturally, it turned out this way!


In shloka 6 of the Jainad inscription of Jagdev, the youngest son of Udayaditya, is—


> **यस्योदयादित्य नृपः पितासी, देवः पितृव्यः स च भोजराजः ।**
> **विरेजतुर्यौ वसुधाधिपत्य, प्रास प्रतिष्ठानिव पुष्पवन्तौ ॥ 6 ॥**
>
> *[Sanskrit as in source; the OCR contains errors.]*
>
> This means — "He whose father was Udayaditya, and whose *pitrivya* — that is, paternal uncle — was Dev Bhojraj: these two, flourishing, soon by their own splendour attained the sovereignty of the earth and became established (like the sun and the moon, the *pushpavantau*)."


In shloka 5 of Jagdev's own Dongargaon inscription it is mentioned—

> **ततो रिपुत्रयस्कन्दैर्मग्नां माल(व)मेदिनीम् ।**
> **उद्धारन्नुदयादित्यस्तस्य भ्राता व्यवर्द्धत ॥ 5 ॥**
>
> *[Sanskrit as in source; the OCR contains errors.]*
>
> That is — "After him (Bhoj Dev), his brother Udayadity rose to eminence — he who rescued the land of Malava, which had been [ground into the dust] / laid low by the attack of three enemies."


In the Jainad writing, Jagdev wrote King Udayadity as his father and King Bhojdev as [his] uncle (*pitrivya*), whereas in the Dongargaon inscription Jagdev has written his father Udayadity as Bhojdev's 'brother' (*bhrata*). Jagdev was the third and youngest prince of his father Udayadity (as his inscriptions tell us). Older than him were two brothers, Lakshmadeva and Narvarman.


Prince Lakshmadeva did not survive even his father's lifetime. Prince Narvarman became the Paramaras' next ruler after [his father] Udayadity's death. It appears that after this political change, Jagdev went off to the Kuntal king, where Prince Jagdev was entrusted with the rule of Vidarbha — situated to the north of the Godavari — and its surrounding region. Jainad and Dongargaon fall within this very region. It is possible that Jagdev's declaring King Bhoj to be his father's *bandhu* (kinsman) in one inscription, and then '*bhrata*' (brother) in the next, was perhaps a matter of some advantage in some politics of the time.


Now the last knot that remains is this: if King Bhoj and King Udayadity were both "*sahodar*" (uterine brothers, born of the same mother), then Udayadity's birth must have occurred before the death of their father King Sindhuraj, or within just a few months of that death. The exact date of King Sindhuraj's death is not known, but his son King Bhojdev appears as a king in Dhar in 1011 CE. If they were uterine brothers, then Udayadity ought, in the ordinary course, to have become king after King Bhojdev's death in 1055 CE. Why did he have to wait until 1070 CE? There is also this argument: had they indeed been Bhoj's kinsmen, then in some inscription or book of Bhoj's own time, somewhere at least, there ought to have been a mention of them!


As an honest, investigative historian, Verma has written a long account of the ideological tug-of-war among the historians before him regarding the relationship of Udayadity and Bhoj. In it comes the name of Professor Franz Kielhorn, who held this very view [that they were brothers]. Then, on this issue, there is the mention of D.C. Ganguly, who brings a detail from the *Ain-i-Akbari* in favour of this same conclusion. Ganguly's opinion was opposed by H.C. Ray in his book *Dynastic History of Northern India*. He does not consider *bandhu* to mean brother.


My own view is that to get entangled in these disputes is to beat a staff about in the fog. Why do such a thing at all? Such arguments and counter-arguments should stay confined to research-treatises and the seminars of closed halls — this alone is proper in the public interest. The tedious cross-examination of an old, complicated case dragging on in some court drives a man away from the law itself. Whatever is useful from the standpoint of history should be explained in detail, in easy language. It is fine, only for reference, [to record] which scholar holds which opinion and who has done the refuting and the affirming. In the search for the ultimate truth, the search for new facts should forever keep going on.


If, about Udaipur, something was read a hundred or a hundred-and-twenty-five years ago, then that is a very big discovery. Even if no one in Udaipur itself knows it, yet it is not the final [word]. Even today, much such [material] will be found in Udaipur that will be read afresh. In the vast kingdom of the Paramaras, everywhere, much that is new will come to the fore, whose threads, joining one with another, will keep making altogether new revelations. But in the matter of history, our interest ought then to be this: how to explain things to local people in very simple language; how to keep books updated with every new discovery; and — most important of all — how to set moving the minds that have stalled in the jobs of the history departments of universities and of the Culture and Archaeology department.


Consider that if, in a splendid city like Udaipur — which has remained neglected with the passage of time — even such precious facts have survived, then one ought, giving thanks to God, to regard it as nothing short of a miracle. In most parts of the country, the temples in which these inscriptions have been seen and read have all fallen into the clutches of cruel Islamic invaders. Several were ruined in such a way that even to look at them requires a very hard heart. Go from Nalanda and Vikramshila in Bihar all the way to Vijayanagara, far off in Karnataka. There is much such [ruin] scattered in every corner of the country, [enough] to mourn over. We do not know how many precious inscriptions there must have been in these developed settlements before they were laid waste — inscriptions badly effaced and ground into stone-powder. In Vidisha itself, the Vijay Mandir built by Narvarman is the most painful example of this. These are such links: broken in one place, scattered in another; and yet, in a sequence, they narrate the whole story. To understand, as a whole, the past that India has undergone, this is enough.


This is a story from across ten long centuries, in the middle of which lies a long, stormy and deadly interval. This is the dust and mist of the centuries that fell into the grip of Islam's barbaric expansion — despite the passing of which, we have managed to know this much that is left over. Doubtless, in bringing them to us in this form, the contribution of the India-loving young men of the English rule is not small. Otherwise, in independent India there is a separate, sorrowful story of illegal occupations and governmental indifference. Udaipur is not untouched by this.

Verma brings the next question: who, in fact, built Udaipur? An inscription of Udayadity's time (later than the Udaipur Prashasti, but undated) gives some precious clues about this. But more than half of it, having become extremely damaged, has not survived worth reading. On it there is no date either. It is possible it was on that portion which was ruined. Its importance is just this — that, pausing, it gives us some clues about the town and temple of Udaipur.


This inscription was obtained not in Udaipur's temple precinct, but from a house in the Chatua Darwaza locality. This precious piece of stone is today in the Archaeology Museum of Gwalior. In it there are twenty-seven lines in all. Like the Udaipur Prashasti, its language is Sanskrit. It is entirely in verse. The stone is quite worn; only with great difficulty could anything be read. Whatever was read shows a splendid glimpse of Udaipur's past.

Its mention is in the *Annual Report of the Archaeological Department in Gwalior State, 1925–26*, serial no. 71, pages 12–13.

In the first to sixth lines is a description of some important military victories of Udayadity. Among these vanquished ones, the only name [given] is that of the Chedi ruler of Dahalamandala, the "Dahaladhish." It is recorded in the inscription that the Dahaladhish was defeated and killed. Dahal was originally the region around Jabalpur, which is considered the original home (*mulasthan*) of the Kalachuris.


As far as Udaipur is concerned, it is evident from its very name that it is related to King Udayadity. The fact that no inscription of the earlier Paramara kings is found here tells us that this town came into existence only in Udayadity's time. In Vidisha district, Jaisingh is probably the first Paramara ruler whose writing is found here. In this one too, the genealogy of the Paramara kings goes only up to Udayadityadev. In the later lines of this inscription is a description of certain local kings of some "Nemak" dynasty under the Paramaras. Since Udaipur was directly under Paramara rule, how is it possible that the mention of some other dynasty should come up? It is possible that King Udayadity had entrusted this area to some feudatory of the Nemak line who was subordinate to him. In this incomplete inscription there may possibly have been some further details about the Nemaks too, but they have not survived worth reading.

From the knowers of the Nagari script it is learned that its primary ruler was possibly one Rudraditya. This inscription tells that the Nemak-line rulers were worshippers of Shiva. There was another feudatory named Shudrak, who had won a victory over some Gurjar king. This inscription describes that it was the son of this very feudatory named Shudrak — whose name is unclear — who had the temple built in Udaipur. We can only surmise that at the time the construction of the Udaipur temple must have begun, this feudatory-family of the Nemak dynasty was the local administrator here, and it was under its supervision that this temple was built.


At the very last, let us talk about the Udaipur Prashasti. We know well that the fame of the ambitious Paramara kings is not only on account of their valour and their war-victories. In their time, all the disciplines of knowledge found shelter. Matchless monuments of architecture went on being built for centuries. Around these monuments were built great knowledge-centres — Ujjain, Dhara, Mandu, Bhojpal, Vidisha, and right up to Udaipur — which these kings carried to [great] heights. Apart from inscriptions, their proofs and their testimony lie filled also in documents — such as the documents of that era: Padmagupta's *Navasahasanka Charita* and Merutunga's *Prabandha Chintamani*.

The most important source of information on the Paramara dynasty is the 'Udaipur Prashasti'. It presents a description of their achievements from the origin of the Paramara dynasty up to King Udayadity. This inscription is fragmentary. This stone, found in the courtyard of the Nilkantheshvar temple of Udaipur, is the most important testimony of Udaipur's history. This most precious stone of Udaipur is kept in the museum of Gwalior. Its language is Sanskrit, and its script the Nagari script of the 11th century. No date is inscribed on it, but according to the information obtained from this inscription, it appears to be of the time of King Udayadity, the successor of King Bhoj. King Udayadity's reign lay between 1070 and 1093. In it are many Sanskrit verses. It is famous in history by the very name of the Udaipur Prashasti.


In the period following the tripartite struggle that ran, in the eighth century, among the Pala, Pratihara and Rashtrakuta dynasties, great political changes occurred in North and South India. Some new dynasties emerged and came to the fore, recording their splendid presence in history. Among these, the most notable were the Chalukyas of Kalyani in the south, who were the successors of the Chalukya dynasty of Vatapi. Even more influential than they were the Cholas, who — ruling with Tanjore in Tamil Nadu as their centre — brought under their banner not only South India but [regions] from North India up to the Shailendra empire; and the Bay of Bengal actually came to stand forth as the 'Chola Lake' (*Chola Sarovar*).

In North India too, new blossoms opened. When the Pratiharas' power weakened, three powers emerged. Among these, the Chedis of Kalachuri, the Chandelas of Jaijakabhukti, and the Paramaras of Malwa rise upon the horizon together. In their long span of five centuries, the ambitious Paramara kings extended their boundaries over a large territory. Their kingdom was spread — along with Malwa — up to Vidisha and Udaipur beyond Bhopal, and in the west up to areas like Hansol, Modhasa and Mahudi. In the south, the Paramaras were ruling over regions from the Narmada's coastal tracts, such as Hoshangabad, up to Kota in the north. Some branches of the Paramara dynasty of Malwa had succeeded in establishing their kingdoms in the Arbund Mandal — the Paramaras of Chandravati, and [those of] Bhinmal, Kiradu, Jalore and Bagar.

In no way do they have any connection with any magic-and-sorcery, or with any buried, hidden treasure — as is a common superstition among the village people. Not only village people, but a large section of the cities too, has kept its vulture-gaze fixed on the search for treasure buried in any such old settlement. Around us many forts, palaces and ruins will be found dug up here and there, where, coming in the dark of night or into the desolation, people arrive in the hope of brightening their fortunes. We do not know how successful these attempts to steal something from history turn out to be!

This much is certain: that if beautiful, clean translations of these inscriptions were available in the temple precinct itself, they would not only help improve the understanding of the village people, but would also prove useful to the tourists coming here, in terms of the knowledge of local history. It is strange that, when these inscriptions have [already] been read, they should be kept locked away in government vaults. Whose intent was it that they should not be shown outside, to common people — and that, if by luck they have survived somewhere, a veil should simply be left lying over them? Why did it never enter the mind of some leader or some officer that the translations of the inscriptions found in temples, palaces and forts ought to be mounted right there, and that their presentation, too, ought not to be in a hackneyed manner on wretched scraps of tin?

These, after all, are the priceless writings of the history around us. They ought to be inscribed, with much refinement and respect, on precious metal or strong stone alone. In Udaipur they are so numerous that they are enough to detain, for a long while, those who take an interest. In the language of television, the deadly struggle to hold [viewers] on the screen for a longer time is called TRP. The very material fit to raise Udaipur's TRP has been left behind by Udaipur's own original builders, and it has survived despite very many calamities. But after independence, a deliberate ideological miserliness, a political malice and a sightlessness gave us not even the opportunity to pay attention to them and to think about them.


### The God-of-Gods of Udayeshvar Dev!

From ancient times themselves, temples and *viharas* have been active and prosperous centres not only of the religious but also of the educational and social activities of Indian society. They may even be regarded as the oldest axis of our social system. The construction of temples and their management has been a matter of public responsibility, in which the state's role was the most important. The Saraswati Kanthabharan[s] built in the Paramaras' time were nothing less than the finest knowledge-centres of their day, where there was a tradition of teaching subjects of every kind. For centuries this knowledge-tradition endured. Even today we learn from documents that in the hostel connected with the Saraswati Kanthabharan of Mandap Durg (present-day Mandu), there was an in-charge, one Govind Bhatt by name.

The construction of vast temples was one task; the economic and administrative responsibilities of running them as an institution were another important task. For the economic maintenance of the students and teachers of Nalanda University, the revenue of two hundred villages — grain, fruit, flowers, milk and the like — was assured. These, as a free donation from the state, served for the running and upkeep of these vast campuses, for the oversight of which a machinery of officers and staff worked. For the running of Nalanda, several of its founder-kings had made their contributions. From Kannauj, King Harsha too had lent a hand.


Santosh Kumar Verma tells us that the provisions of the *akshayanivi* (permanent-endowment) system for the management of religious institutions appear in the Satavahana period. Evidence of their extensive allotment of agricultural land, too, begins to be found — the first mention of which is in the inscriptions of Gautamiputra Satakarni and in the accounts of the Chutu rulers, who gave such donations to the *viharas* of Nagarjunakonda and Amaravati. In the Gupta period their number began to grow, where various Smriti-writers and text-authors mentioned *akshayanivi* in the form of wealth and, in later times, *nidhi*, *nyasa* and *nikshepa*. In the south, too, it was in vogue. After the Gupta period, land-donations were given to temples on a large scale — and that with special privileges such as '*sa-dandashapa-aparadha*' and '*bhutopata-pratyaya*', which was, in one way or another, indeed to some extent a transfer of the absolute exclusive rights of the royal power.

In the inscriptions of the famous Udayagiri near Vidisha, we see Amrakardava *[the source reads "आत्रककार्दव"]*, the commander of Chandragupta II Vikramaditya, giving such a donation to the Kakanadabot (the old name of Sanchi) Mahavihara. In Kumaragupta's period, this royal patronage had a role in the establishment of a Mahavihara and University like Nalanda — which Harsha contributed to expanding by donating several hundred villages. Not only Nalanda, but many other world-famous institutions of that era depended on this land-donation — from Vallabhi in the west to the Big Temple of the Chola kings in the south. These donations were called *agrahara* (given to persons of scholarly or religious importance), *devadeya*, or *mathapura*.


If we look at Udaipur's vastness and grandeur, then — in view of its economic and administrative aspect — its budget too must have been quite considerable. Since Udayadity was himself the ruler, the arrangement of resources for the temple must certainly have been made by this kind of vision. We have not found any writing giving detailed information especially about this. Whenever some such writing is found, some help may be had in understanding the economy of this temple.

The proof of one extremely subtle *devadeya* is obtained from a single inscription in the temple itself. This inscription is on a pillar to the right of the temple's eastern gate.

In 1889 it appears in the *Indian Antiquary*, Volume 18, page 342, and Volume 20, page 83, serial no. 2; in the *Annual Reports of the Archaeological Department, Gwalior State, Samvat 1974*, serial no. 121; at serial no. 483 in Bhandarkar's list of Northern Inscriptions; and at serial no. 207 in Kielhorn's list.


This inscription is of 14 lines. The pillar portion is somewhat broken in the middle. Also, since the corner above its first line is broken off, the last part of the date has been erased. Its language is Sanskrit. It is in the fort (*gadh*), and the formation of the letters is thirteenth-century Nagari. In letter-formation too these are quite crude. On account of having become dilapidated, in some places the text is not clear. On this inscription the date is written as Samvat 1286 (1229 CE), Kartik Sudi ___, Friday. The last part of the date is broken. The purpose of this inscription is to record the donation of 4 *biswa* of land for the temple of Udayeshvardev by the treasury-officer Dhamdev, in the reign of Maharaja Devpaldev.

Worthy of note, too, is that long before the date of the inscription, Devpaldev had already begun sitting upon the royal throne in the capital Dhar and using the full royal titles. Before becoming king, he must have held some military or administrative responsibility in Udaipur itself — and that is why, in this inscription, he is addressed only with the title of *Mahakumar*. If he was in Udaipur before becoming king, then this fact enhances the importance of Udaipur; for, about a hundred and fifty years before him, Udayadity too must have been in some such role. Now this also becomes clear — that Udaipur was such a centre from which at least two crown-princes came forth to become kings in the Paramara dynasty.

In this inscription of Devpal Dev, the measure of the land given in donation is written as 4 *biswa*, which at present is the twentieth part of one *bigha* of land — a very small plot. This unit of measuring land was locally in vogue in the Bhopal region. Whatever portion of the inscription is available — the text that, even after centuries, has come before the eyes fit to be read — manages to hand us only a few threads from across a time filled with deep fog; and, holding on to these, we can at the very least take a splendid stroll through a world of imagination.


This much is clear: that some venerable artisan of Udaipur is, by some royal order, recording a donation of land inside the temple. What a historian ought to do with the help of these threads, Santosh Kumar Verma is doing today. Catching hold of just a few words of the writing, he carries us still further along. For instance, the mention of "*panchakul*" in the eighth line. He tells us that this word has often been used for the village-elders' assembly (*gram-vriddha-sabha*) as well. In the states of the south, it was also known by the name of "*pancha muvadu*." This was an institution rather like today's panchayat, which handled the local administration.


The *pancha-mahashabda* was one of the privileges [of a king], in the context of the king and the royal family. The tradition of the royal orchestra's playing of five instruments would signify, in the royal court, the king's presence. These five instruments included the *shring* (horn), *tamat* (kettledrum), *shankh* (conch), *bheri*, and *jayaghanta*. There was a tradition of playing these only in the presence of the king, the royal family, or those officers upon whom this honour was conferred by the king's special grace. The filmmaker Ramanand Sagar, in the next part of his popular serial *Ramayan* — the *Uttar Ramayan* — brought a scene onto the screen. Ram has returned after conquering Lanka. Now he is the king of Ayodhya. It is the hour of dawn; a redness is rising on the horizon. Lord Ram lies asleep in deep peace in his bedchamber. Devi Sita's skilled attendants are, with great devotion, performing her adornment (*shringar*). Sita must now go to the bedchamber and wake Ram; it is growing late. Against the backdrop of this delightful scene, the composer Ravindra Jain has fashioned a regal sweetness for this very occasion. On the palace roof, the orchestra and the singers, with mellifluous voice, are announcing the coming of dawn — "*Jaagiye Raghunath nath, bhor bhayo aangana*" (Awaken, Lord Raghunath; dawn has come to the courtyard). Reaching the bedchamber, Sita says — "Awaken, Raghunath; even the sun is waiting for you to rise…!"


Now the question: after building so vast a temple as Udaipur — whose construction went on for two decades — what could be the meaning of a king donating to the temple only such a small piece of land as 4 *biswa*? Verma tells us that this seemingly trivial land-donation is not the only example of its kind. In that era, in Rajasthan and Gujarat, in some writings of the Chauhans of Nadol and Shakambhari and of the Chalukyas of Gujarat, there are records of the donation of small parcels of land for religious works. From this writing, it is not possible today to identify that precious piece of land somewhere in Udaipur or its vicinity. Would that we could see where it is, and in what condition! We do not know where it might be, or what might have become of it now — under whose occupation it might be, or who might be its owner.


Nine hundred and fifty years later, today we can only estimate what was so remarkable about the 4 *biswa* of land that it was donated to the temple, and that its mention, too, was engraved upon the temple. It is possible that this piece of land — appearing very small in size — had some special quality. It may have been full of a water source, so that the water-supply for this temple could be well ensured. It may have been given for the residential use of the royal priest or the chief manager; or upon it there may have been some splendid stepwell (*baoli*), whose water was specially preserved for worship, ablution (*abhishek*) or rituals in the sanctum! If the mention of so little land is inscribed upon so vast a temple, then its use, too, must have been of a very special kind.


Dr. Suresh Mishra kept working, continuously and with concentration, on these inscriptions deposited in the ASI's archive and on their translations. Whenever something new was found, he would pass on the information by phone and send the full details by email. When the two of us met the ASI's Regional Director, Dr. P.K. Mishra, in March 2021 at his Bhopal office, the suggestion of us both was this: that in Udaipur's vast temple precinct, the Hindi–English translation of selected inscriptions ought to be displayed — ones that would give new information to tourists. As it is, people come; they take *darshan*; they wander about a little; and, without any information, they simply drift off into the void. Besides this, one's most skilled employees ought to be posted at such places — those who have their own interest in enhancing the lustre of such sites. They should not be like the unskilled labourers of MGNREGA. This is a field in which there is scope to do much that is new.

I remember that Dr. Mishra had given the assurance that he himself would like to go to Udaipur once, and would do whatever was possible at the ASI's level.


### 42 monuments, 100 inscriptions


In first bringing forth the story of Udaipur beyond Udaipur, and in combing the dust of Udaipur's desolate lanes for its detailed documentation, the role of the Indian National Trust for Art and Cultural Heritage (INTACH) has been the most important. Its convener in Bhopal, Dr. Madanmohan Upadhyay, is a sensitive history-lover and a retired IAS officer of Madhya Pradesh itself. The moment our solitary efforts in Udaipur came to his notice, he deployed an expert INTACH team in Udaipur. This team, for the first time, combed through every nook and corner of Udaipur. They went into every lane many times over, and went and peered into every ruin. They identified the remnants from the time of every ruler who came and went across the long span of a thousand to twelve hundred years, counted the inscriptions, and documented them. Now, a year later, the publication of this most recent research work — centred on Udaipur — is also about to take place.


In Madhya Pradesh, INTACH has, over the past years, done several noteworthy works in the direction of tending our historical heritage. Among these, bringing Bandhavgarh National Park onto documents from a historical viewpoint, and tending a neglected Vishnu idol, is the newest work — about which much has been printed in the media too. They have documented the Sidhi, Singrauli and Narsinghpur districts. Work has been completed at Ginnorgarh and Jawra. Work is ongoing on the heritage scattered in the forests of Chanderi.


On the basis of his experience so far, Dr. Upadhyay says of Udaipur that it can truly become an exemplar. Udaipur has the capacity to be brought forth, at the international level, as a magnificent tourist spot. "So many diverse monuments in one lot, I have seen nowhere in the world. Historical places of ten times less capacity than this are, merely because of the policies of governance, far more thronged with the bustle of tourists. Udaipur can become a model in itself. Understand it thus: that in Udaipur we are sitting upon a treasure of history." The Nilkantheshvar temple, whose fame extends far and wide, is recorded in Udaipur's inscriptions in the form of the Udayeshvar temple. In the contemporary inscriptions the name Nilkantheshvar appears nowhere. In which century it became famous, no one knows.


This is the only such place where there are old havelis, palaces, old markets, fort walls, temples, ancient tanks and stepwells. Every remnant of a developed town-formation is present here. Most important of all is that around a hundred inscriptions are available, which have survived despite the buffeting blows across centuries of history. These are, besides Sanskrit and Persian, in Devanagari. There are even inscriptions in which the writings of both Sanskrit and Persian stand together. But the INTACH team, in its ground work, has identified 42 new monuments whose care is very necessary, and which are being ruined with the passage of time. Five or six ancient stepwells have survived, in much better condition, until now.


Like Gwalior and Chanderi, the presence of Jain idols upon the hilly rocks tells us that this whole region held within itself religious traditions of every kind. After [the region's] coming into the grip of the Sultans and emperors of Delhi, the mosques of the Muslim period, too, add some new pages to the story of Udaipur. Upon this statement of Dr. Upadhyay, my own belief is that every broken idol of Udaipur — whether of Hindu gods and goddesses or of the Tirthankaras — tells that Udaipur too had heard the hoofbeats of the horses of the obstinate-minded followers of Islam.


On the basis of this intensive ground work, INTACH's future plan is that, to raise Udaipur into prominence, the Archaeological Survey of India, the State Archaeology Department, the district administration, the panchayat, private institutions, corporate companies, and the aware local society be brought together. In the past year Upadhyay went to Udaipur several times. He believes that the local people have a separate world, and that their living connection with their priceless heritage has not been such that they would take any effective initiative from their own side. It has been a distance filled with a kind of indifference. Until they feel an attachment to it, the expected results cannot come from external efforts or governmental attempts alone. The government, in PPP mode, can make towns like Udaipur shine like the historical places of Rajasthan and South India. Here there is so much worth seeing and understanding.


On the basis of the Udaipur experience, he says that if the state government were to have an extensive record-making (documentation) of the panchayats done, it would be a big initiative. "We are doing this work in 10–12 districts. If the government has this work done in all the panchayats of the state, then it is certain that, for the very first time, many priceless things will come into our knowledge all at once. What is of archaeological importance in the panchayats and villages will be known. Nor does this work take too much time. It is work that can be done in a week. There is just a need to inspire the local voluntary organizations in this work. With a little training alone, they will be able to catalogue everything. Before going to Udaipur, we did not know that inscriptions are present here in such large numbers, everywhere. In the temple alone, a whole PhD could be done on the inscriptions."


There are some deficiencies over which one can only lament. Upadhyay draws attention to the fact that in Madhya Pradesh there is a famine of experts who can read inscriptions. In government departments and educational institutions too there are no people who can read new inscriptions. To them, black letters are as [good as] a buffalo *[the idiom "kaala akshar bhains baraabar" — utterly illiterate of them]*. What is written on which stone, they will not be able to say.


About the clear difference between the common society of South and North India, he says that in the School of Planning and Architecture, the presence of the youth of Madhya Pradesh will be found to be low, in comparison with other states. Compared to South India, the lack of interest in history is plainly visible. Even for the documents preserved in the National Archive at Bhopal, there is no one in Madhya Pradesh itself who reads them — whereas this record is boundless. There is much for us to know. In present-day Madhya Pradesh there must have been more than 30 princely states (*raje-rajwade*), contemporaries of the English rule. Most of their heritage is turning into ruins; its upkeep is not right. The successors of the kings have gone off to other places; in many places they have not survived at all. In such a situation, to know the untouched chapters of history in detail, a social campaign is needed — and its good fruit is not possible without the active participation of the local society.


### Sonari Village and the Garhi of Gyasi

During the very stir of Udaipur, I also happened to go to the surrounding villages. Such as Muradpur, where a huge idol of Varaha must once have stood in an ancient temple. Now it is a desolate sort of place. Even closer than that is Sonari village, which is settled on the bank of a local river and is also talked about for the idols that come forth from its womb. My going there did not come off, but I got a good deal of information from Praveen Sharma and Govind Saxena. I had received a photograph of one idol, which I had sent to the archaeologist Dr. Narayan Vyas. It was not adorned with crown and horses like some deity-idol. Dr. Vyas told me that it is the idol of some king or feudatory.


The small railway station nearest to Udaipur is Baretha. About five kilometres from here, on the bank of a river named Sonari Kevtan, is a very old village. When the water dries up, the river spews out idols, broken and whole. In the village houses, the idols found here have been kept safe. Govind tells us that most of the idols are of the avatars of Lord Vishnu. Several idols have been placed on a verandah, and some are present in the premises of the District Archaeology Museum, Vidisha. Among these is a masterpiece of the Varaha avatar too, which by luck survived whole. It was in a pair-form (*yugal*), the second part of which went to the museum. A Vishnu idol named Vishwa Mohan was rescued from smugglers about 15 years ago; this too is in the Vidisha museum. For idol-smugglers, this area has been extremely fertile. The village people often oppose the taking of these frequently-found idols outside the village. They say that their preservation should be here, so that people coming from outside may take note of Sonari village as well.


The finding of Vishnu idols from Muradpur all the way to Sonari links the threads of history to the Gupta rulers — who were worshippers of Vishnu — even further back than the Paramaras. From this we can surmise that even five hundred years before Udayadity, the process of fashioning architectural marvels here was continuous. Now, to the matter of the idols that come out of a river.


It is now hidden from no one that, in the centuries following the Turk Muslims' capture of Delhi at the end of the 12th century, a threat began to hover over the ancient heritage all over the country. Wherever they went, they proved destructive for temples and idols. The tradition of building ghats, temples and gurukuls on riverbanks was not new. Temples were broken, and the broken idols were thrown into the rivers. The shattered idols of Sonari village are proofs of that same faith of centuries ago — about which nothing was ever taught in our schools and colleges. They are proofs of the destructive power of Islam, which, by its very birth, has held idol-worship to be a sin.


The "*sona*" (gold) fixed in the name of Sonari village is a worn-down fragment of some ancient golden city (*svarna-nagari*) or Swarna Puri — as if, with time, solid gold, worn away, turns worse than brass! These idols are extending an invitation for researchers to come here and probe the missing threads of history's story. We can only imagine how this whole region, a thousand years ago, was setting up exemplars of architecture. The availability of stone had, for centuries, given artisans opportunity in village after village, and they had found monarchies that were the nurturers and inspirers of [their] traditions.


And now let us go off in an altogether different direction from Udaipur. Once I happened to visit a place that lies about 15 kilometres from Udaipur, toward Patari, amid scenic, verdant hills. If you join Udaipur to Patari on Google Maps, this place is exactly in the middle. From the ruins of Patari it is plainly evident that it must have been a developed town in the time of the Mauryas and the Gupta kings. But the place I went to — direct access there, by any vehicle, is not easy even today. It is a hilly forest. On this hill — surrounded by dense, tall grass grown up in the rains, and by a thick forest of teak (*sagwan*) — there is some old little fort, which is not visible even from ten feet away. In the surrounding villages they call it "Garhi." This too is in the Ganj Basoda tehsil of Vidisha district. People told me that here there is only the bustle of greedy searchers after treasure buried in the ground, who have tried their fortunes digging inside the Garhi.


Who had this Garhi built? Of what date is it? Any inscription, any story?

The answers to all these questions are hardly to be found anywhere in all the documents written on this region; but in folk [memory], everywhere, there are many stories about any such place. Here too there are. Stories heard from the elders in the family. Tales heard across generations. From these tales you can weave the warp and weft of the original story. In the Garhi of Gyasi — who is this Gyasi? Below the hill, encircled on every side by dense greenery, there stands the splendid establishment of a Hanuman temple. The temple, in a rustic style, is only some forty years old, but a few idols bear centuries-old testimony. They are broken in such a way as to tell that they have been [deliberately] broken. They are but a few — pieces of some ancient temple that stood in some part of the Garhi. Someone brought them here and set them down. The idol of Hanuman is old; some local official established it and gave the place the shape of a temple. Now, here, groups from the villages gather to do the recitation (*paath*) of the Sundarkand. Should some crisis ever come, [they say,] just call the "Garhi-wale Babba" to mind, and nothing [ill] will befall. Such is the unshakeable faith.


Reach an old wall of the Garhi, more than four feet wide. Everything is lost in dense forest. On the ground the grass is so thick that nothing can be seen. The strong rampart of the Garhi peeps out here and there. A cracked bastion (*burj*) — who knows when it may collapse. Someone says that in the Garhi there are some storeys below as well. All this — who had it built here; of whose rule are these anonymous remnants; in search of what treasure do people come? Who is this Gyasi? The village people have answers to every question. In documents they are scarcely to be found anywhere. As many mouths, so many versions — like the many threads of some story. Catch hold of any thread, and you may end up anywhere.


**The first thread:** This was a Garhi of dacoits. From here orders would go out that so much loot was wanted, and at once the demand would be met. That is, some ancestors of Gabbar Singh ruled from here. Their edict would go to Ramgarh, and whatever they wished, they would get. Theirs was an organized power.


**The second thread:** There was a village named Daulatpur below, which is now desolate. There were other such villages too, of which there is no trace. There are only the names; there are no villages. Once, markets used to be held here. But how did the villages become desolate?

**The third thread:** In the rule of the Muslims, the force of attacks and loot was strong. To save their life and honour, Hindus would encamp in such safe mountains. This was just such a safe place.

**The fourth thread:** "Bagdi" was a branch of local Rajputs who, by rebelling or revolting, did not obey the orders of the Muslim rulers. They lived here in an organized way. Their descendants are in the region even today, but after independence, for the benefit of reservation, they got their names registered among the Scheduled Castes. In Raisen district, the Bagdis of these branches are in the General category. Basically, these are remnants of the era of slavery under Muslim rule. The broken idols are proof of this. Meaning: the firm-in-faith invaders, who had set out to "do the welfare" of the *kafirs*, must have come here too.


**The fifth thread:** Greedy people from far and wide came to the Garhi in search of treasure. They dug a great deal. Most went mad. In the surrounding region there are stories of many such beliefs, [about those] who had "kept an eye on" the Garhi. Fortune, too, had been kind to some. There are also tales of those who found loot here.


The question remains unanswered: who is Gyasi? Was this some dacoit? If this was a single dacoit, then from the loot of his whole life he could by no means have built such a Garhi. Is this a worn-down fragment of some "Gyasuddin," and was some ruler (*hakim*) of that name ever an occupier here? In that case there are two possibilities: this must have been some Rajput's principality, and after the Muslim occupations at Chanderi, Raisen or Mandu, some petty ruler may have come to seize the principality here. Fit one piece of the story here: that this very Gyasuddin used to send decrees to collect tax from the surrounding villages. Obviously, in the eyes of the village people he would be a looter or dacoit, not any king. There must once have been kings, upon whose Garhi there was now an encroachment — just as encroachments happened all over the country. Invading looters, who called themselves *badshah* and *sultan*, *nawab* and *nizam* — whom historians of the "Irfan [Habib] and Habib–Romila [Thapar]" type *[the source garbles the names as "इरफान थार और हबीब रोमिला"]* wrote history-books actually accepting as *badshah* and *sultan*, in accordance with those [rulers'] self-proclaimed claims.


All over the country, so many such forts and Garhis are collapsing in a state of anonymity, whose written histories are nowhere [to be found]; where there are no inscriptions, and which are entirely lost in forests and wildernesses. Such strong constructions generations must have raised. For centuries, life must have flourished here — where there would have been businessmen, markets, and cultural liveliness too, surely. The broken idols are witnesses that there must have been temples. There must have been knowers of architecture, who were building such splendid temples. There must have been dynasties, and generous merchants, who invested wealth in them. That there were no poets and scholars — how can that be?


There must have been a history before the tenth century, and then the storms must have made their attempts to wipe out [its] existence. Old stories must have gone on merging into [mere] memories. New occupiers must have been writing new texts. Some powerful, organized people among the Kshatriyas must have become "Bagdi." The chapter of the occupiers too must have ended, for some unknown reasons, and the Garhi spread out, across the generations, in the fragments of its share of the story. Wealthy principalities had drawn outside looters here. Greedy people still "keep an eye on" the Garhi. They do not come in a band with guns and swords to fight anyone; they come stealthily with pickaxe and hoe. To try their luck, they dig up rubble anywhere and go off. It is possible that someone even finds something. But the story of its being an object of faith is a strange one. In any case, being so greedy is itself a kind of madness — and they were ignorant brutes anyway, those who kept coming to loot and to slaughter.


In every such Garhi and fort there are countless memories of India's jewel-adorned history. Cast your gaze anywhere around you. This is a challenge to the passionate youth who take an interest in history — that they make such regions the subject of their study and research. The responsibility of generating, in school and college students, the strength to accept such challenges belongs to their teachers. The departments should give every possible help in this, so that today's youth not only connect with history but also gather ever-new information and carry out research. We do not know into whose hands something may fall, and when — which story's strings may join with which tale.


Who knows what stir there would have been inside the Garhi when Udaipur was at its splendour, and what would have been going on all the way up to Pathari? After looking at these places with a single glance, it feels that, at one time, there must have been a tremendous liveliness of life here. Across this whole region, far and wide, construction works must have gone on for centuries. Now all this is [but] scattered points, twinkling in a dark sky spread out far away. If we cannot save their light from being extinguished, then such a freedom should be reckoned worthless.


We have also suggested to the government that heritage walks ought to be made compulsory in schools and colleges at the state and district level. College and university students should go and see the great heritage present throughout the country. Students of the higher school classes should be taken to such places of their own state, and to the heritage at the district or block level students of the lower classes should be taken at least twice a year. For this, the Tourism Department can develop easy tourist circuits, in which a route of visiting two or three monuments in a single day is laid out. From the government down to the panchayat, from the minister down to the *panch* of a village, everyone can make an active contribution to this. This is mission-mode work — not the mere filling of the belly of files with papers and orders.


I believe that there is no other recourse for connecting the new generation with the nation's great heritage. They will have to be set in motion toward it by every possible means. For this, top-class publications — both brief and detailed — should be produced on local history and archaeology, to be kept in the schools and colleges themselves. Short films should be made and circulated in the digital world. In writing and speech competitions among children, our heritage should be made compulsory as a central theme. Through this, they will grow eager to read their history, and to go and see it. By technical means too, if they wish, they can create something. They can make videos; they can put out audio. By doing so, will they not learn to take pride in their great creative ancestors — who, centuries ago, fashioned so much, [enough] to last generations, and left it for us?


I often say that whatever we may do in life for our livelihood — whether we become a teacher, scientist, doctor, engineer, farmer, businessman, leader or officer — the reading of history ought, everywhere, to be a compulsory subject for us. If we are becoming anything at all without knowing history, then we are standing like a building that has no foundation!


### The Consciousness Awakening from Udaipur


— **Shivkumar Vivek**

Stones do not speak. The rocks of Udaipur, too, do not speak. Had they spoken, they would have told the whole story of their time — the story that some scholars, twisting and distorting, recounted to the coming generations; the story whose layers the turning of time wrapped up. These rocks would state history impartially. They have no ideology. They have no prejudices.


But the silence of these stones speaks. It carries one close to the fragmentary tale. What is needed is one who can hear and understand the language of that silence. Some people are reading the silence of the town of Udaipur. Turning over the scattered stone-blocks (*prastar-khand*) there and asking after their story, they are examining the neatly-arranged stones and grasping their remaining tale.


A band of just such curious ones, students and explorers, comes to Udaipur every other day — to that town situated near Ganj Basoda in Vidisha district, which has recently emerged from the darkness of ignorance and neglect. Had divine coincidence (*devyog*) not brought them here, this darkness — who knows how much longer it would have lain spread.


In some era, certain tyrants had come here. Upon the unprecedented sculptures made by some soul devoted to art, they had wielded hammers. Upon the adored [deities] they had struck chisel-blows. They were not merely of another faith (*vidharmi*); they were people devoid of the refinement (*sanskar*) of any dharma. Just such people we have seen, with our own eyes, destroying the Buddhas of Bamiyan.


Udaipur, Udaydit had fashioned with his whole heart. These descendants of King Bhoj were the governor of this last edge of the kingdom. He had the Udaysagar built. He raised the Nilkantheshvar temple — one of the most beautiful shrines in the country, a matchless specimen of architecture. From base to spire (*shikhar*), several replicas of the temple itself have been arranged, one atop another. No place is left empty of idols and carving.


Look at some of the idols a little carefully, and one has a broken hand, another a missing head. On many, the marks of blows are clear. They kept resisting the blows with steadfastness. They could not be broken. The weapons of the hundreds of short-tempered, heartless, discernment-less and witless attendants of Muhammad Tughlaq must have gone blunt.


They were crazed. By breaking temples, they wished to establish the house of Khuda. They had set out to break the place of worship of thousands of people and to make of it their own house of worship. They broke the idols, turned them over, and had them built into the walls. Upon them they wrote the messages of Khuda in Urdu. Was that Khuda, then, an enemy of Ishvar (God)?


The work of making a mosque from the temple's remnants began. Peer behind the stones of the mosque, and some idol or other will be stuck [there]. A blueprint was drawn up to cover over the temple. Standing in the temple precinct and looking, the half-finished walls of the mosque bear witness to this. This is the very place where the process of destroying the places of worship of one religion, and erecting over them the monument of another religion (which is, by mistake, called a religion), can be plainly seen.


But who is there to look at this evidence? Our policy-controllers said — do not dig up buried corpses [let bygones be bygones]. For some people, the tyrant-barbarians became worshipable ancestors. Do not disturb the ancestors. Zahiruddin Babur, who came from Uzbekistan, became the greatly venerable. The Fatiha in their name kept being read.


On Udaydit's thousand-year-old palace, a madrasa took possession. On the Udaysagar, cultivation began. The Nilkantheshvar temple was gripped by encroachment. People got no place to dwell apart from the splendour and pride of their past. Perhaps it was such people who must have smoothed the path of Tughlaq and Khilji.


Our friend Vijay Manohar Tiwari — who is a journalist and the Information Commissioner of [Madhya Pradesh] — is from this very district. So his eye fell upon it. He resolved to bring it out of the dark lanes. Taking with him the history-expert Dr. Suresh Mishra, he went, and — weaving together the threads of the past's course of events — grasped the vicious cycle of time. A band of a few enthusiastic youths got organized. Since then they have been engaged in making people alert toward this heritage.


Every month, taking along some presiding figure, connoisseur or curious soul, their band goes to Udaipur. I too, carrying just such curiosities, reached here last Sunday, and found that, in the direction of preserving this heritage, several people have become active. Why do village panchayats and village-groups remain heedless toward tending the great heritage that lies within their own bounds — this thorn pricks. Tiwari gives the happy news that, in memory of Dr. Suresh Mishra — who departed to the abode of the gods in the recent corona period — a plan is under consideration to do some grassroots work for cherishing the heritage around us and for awakening a consciousness of history. Vijay Manohar says: Udaipur is now [awakening] from the deep sleep of history. *[A word is missing in the source here — apparently "जाग रहा है" (is awakening).]*

## The Path of Udayeshvar Mahadev Made Broad

— **Ajit Vadnerkar**


- It seems a sleeping settlement is coming out of its drowsiness… The days of Udaipur (Vidisha) are about to turn [for the better].

- People interested in the conservation and promotion of history, archaeology and cultural treasures are coming here continually. There is enthusiasm among the local people that the monuments of Udaipur — victims of centuries of neglect — will now come alive… This humble one himself examined and appraised it over two journeys to this region.


- It seems that Udaipur is now determined to play the role of a representative exemplar (*pratidarsh*) of Paramara-era art. If only the eminent men (*shreshthigan*) of Vidisha, Ganjbasoda and Mandi Bamora were to wish it, they could themselves come forward as the guardians of these ownerless monuments scattered between Malwa and Bundelkhand.


- Khilji… Tughlaq… Lodi, and then the Mughals…. The splendid districts (*janpad*) of Malwa–Bundelkhand endured the torments of the ill-intentioned attacks of all of these….. Today's Malwa, in the centuries before Christ, was known by two names — Dasharna and Avanti. This vast region was, collectively, under Ashoka. Later it came to be called Malwa. Vidisha was the centre of eastern Malwa, and Ujjayini of western Malwa.


- Udaipur, about seventy kilometres from Vidisha, cannot even be called a town (*kasba*) today. A sleeping sort of village, that awaits the moment of waking from its past… A thousand years ago from today, when it was in its prime *[the source reads "सुमावस्था," apparently for "सु-अवस्था" — good/flourishing state]*, its splendour would have been that of the temple-city of the ancient Dasharna kingdom.


- In the foundation of almost the whole of Udaipur, history lies buried. Much of it shows in the ruins, but many of the ruins have already been converted into pucca houses. To recognize these patches of the ancient and the modern, one needs no expert's eye; even the eye of any ordinary tourist can recognize them.


- Udayeshvar Mahadev is considered the finest specimen of Paramara-era construction. In the classical terminology of architecture (*vastu*), it is called "Bhumija" architecture — a distinctive style of constructing upon level, sloping ground. In it, the walls were made of bricks and stones.


- Scholars say that the birth, elevation and development of the Bhumija style of architectural construction took place in Malwa — particularly in the Paramara period of the ninth–tenth centuries. From here, this style spread to Rajasthan, Malwa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh as well.


- At the beginning of this year, the renowned historian Dr. Suresh Mishra and State Information Commissioner Vijay Manohar Tiwari were on a heritage-walk in this historic town. What this pair saw there was astonishing. In the course of it they found that, upon the ruined remains of the old royal palace, a notice of "private property" had been pasted.


- Not only this — Dr. Mishra was also taken aback to see an extraordinary greenness here and there in the town. This greenness was not of the "*shasya-shyamala*" kind [the green of ripe fields], but that of green-coloured sheets (*chaadar*) with gilt lace-borders (*gota-kinari*), spread untidily over the stones amid the ruins.


- In every inch of India's soil, a history of thousands of years breathes. But our pseudo-secular apparatus has remained adept at looking past that history. Here, the eyes of our historians never learned to read the intent of the religious messages printed upon these green sheets. Jauhar and the sati-tradition they duly tied to the feudal disposition of Hindu society — but they could not learn to read, behind these, the barbaric text of the Turk-Mongol rulers greedy for luxury (*vilasita*).


- In any case, seeing the marks of identity-erasure upon the Paramara-era temple, royal palace and other sites of this obscure town, the historian Mishra was astonished. When such matters — the testimony of a so-called worship-place of another religion in the temple courtyard; the direct proof of a madrasa being run in the royal palace; the conjuring-trick of the green sheets, and so on — came into the media, then the administration awoke from its slumber.


- Last year, when I came here, the access-road to the vast temple was extremely narrow on account of encroachment. From there, the grandeur of the temple was not even visible.


- In any case, Vijay Manohar Tiwari did a serial writing on his Facebook page… "Historian Dr. Suresh Mishra had said that day, while returning from Udaipur, that the whole town of Udaipur itself could be developed like a heritage town. Our ancestors could not save the Vijay Mandir; let us at least save Udaipur. So now is the time to remove the illegal kiosks (*gumatis*)…!"


- …And truly, some aware citizens sprang into action… the enthusiastic Praveen Sharma, Kaustubh Shirhonkar, Kamlesh Sharma, Gagan Dubey and Amit Shukla of Ganjbasoda;

- Raja Miyan of Udaipur;

- Ramakant Srivastava of Mandi Bamora… and many other people. On the initiative of all these, it was, first of all, the local people themselves who began removing the encroachments. After that, they took up the task of clearing the obstructed path leading to the temple. …And, in no time at all, in a small town, through public cooperation, a nearly three-foot-wide cemented road was built and made ready.


- As if Udaipur had risen to its feet to bring back the glory and splendour lost over the centuries.


- Seeing this newly-built, broad, expansive path, made through public cooperation, one feels as though it was just such a broad splendour of the road to the Deccan that, again and again, kindled the greed of the outside invaders.


- That path, connecting the Uttarapath (northern route) to the Dakshinapath (southern route), was not merely a highway of transport but a great highway of devotion (*bhakti*), upon which various dynasties such as the Chera, Chola, Pandya, Chalukya and Rashtrakuta had built who-knows-how-many god-cities and *puris*, for the sake of keeping India's Sanatan culture unbroken — [cities] from which the stream of devotion illuminated the populace on every side.


- This was the very great highway through which the devotion-stream that "arose in the Dravida [land]" (*Dravid upji*) had, in North India, set aglow one beacon-tower brighter than the next. Just such an awakened beacon-tower was the Udayeshvar Mahadev temple of Udaipur.


- It seems to me that Udaipur is about to receive the importance due to an ancient heritage. This will not happen in the "here-and-now"; yet certainly the history that had slept for ten centuries is at least turning over lightly [in its sleep]. The residents of this sweet town — who, even while keeping a faint whiff (*sunagun*) of Udaipur's history, are unaware of its ancient glory — have now themselves begun girding up to become tourism-attendants (*paryatan-parichar*).


- In the roots of our ethnic memories, it is religious consciousness that has lain. This, too, is the very basis of our present cultural identity. From these, the whole compound (*samaas*) of our collective life is seen to flourish and bloom.


- The state government, if it wishes, can do something new in the field of tourism. If, for the conservation of the 'Paramara-era temples,' a tourism circuit were made by combining the northern, central, eastern, western and southern regions, then, for large-scale religious tourism, domestic tourists would surely come, and the number of the occasional, study-minded, curious tourists too would increase. Not only that: the foreign wanderers who come to Khajuraho could also be brought here.


- The time has now come to begin the Udaipur chapter of the Paramara-era tourism circuit. Bhojpur has already been established. The concept of Public–Private Partnership is now no longer new; it is known as the PPP model.


- The government, if it wishes, can begin this with local cooperation too. But the matter will not be settled merely by increasing the facilities in the temple. In the whole Udaipur town-area and around it, within a radius of five km, important archaeological wealth lies scattered. Even within a 500-metre radius of the temple, the houses that stand are built from ancient antiquarian material (*purasamagri*).


In passing: the famous Udayagiri caves near Vidisha; Udaipur, seventy km from Vidisha; and, in this same town, the Udaysamudra situated about five hundred metres away, in front of the Udayeshvar Mahadev temple — these names are all named after Udyadity, who is mentioned in Paramara-era history as the grandson (*pautra*) of Bhoj. In some places he is mentioned as Bhoj-*bandhu* (Bhoj's kinsman) too. However, the fifth-century-CE caves near Sanchi were not built by Udyadity; he [merely] gave the hill his name.

## The Last Dream


In mid-March 2021, a brother of Dr. Mishra passed away in Mandla. He went there with his family members. This is an incident from just before the second wave of corona — the wave that proved the most fatal for the country. He stayed there more than two weeks. During this time we talked on the phone almost regularly. In our conversations, Udaipur was a permanent subject. We were deciding what to do next, whom to meet, whom to take along. He had prepared a list of some people of like interests, who were to become the future guests of Udaipur's coming journeys. We were deciding what all requests could be made to the state government, and for which favours we could spread out our bag (*jholi*) before the central government.


He returned to Bhopal in early April — but with a cold and cough. This was the difficult time of corona's second, most deadly wave. He had come by car in the summer season. He had to be admitted to Chirayu Hospital one day. This was the time when corona had become the second name for death, and, in hospitals across the country, for the first time one was seeing a desperate scramble over beds, over an injection named Remdesivir, and over oxygen. The news, every single day, of people of every age departing forever had thrown the people safe in their homes into terror. Dr. Mishra was in the hospital, but was in contact with us all on the mobile phone. I remember the last one or two conversations. His throat was hoarse, as though a cold had settled in.


One day he told me that he had had a laptop brought to the hospital too, and would work from there itself. Whatever I had by then written of this real-time diary of the waking town, I emailed to him, so that in his free time he could read it and pass his time, and also give his suggestions. We could not even go and see him — let alone meet him. Only the mobile phone was the sole means of contact between us. We do not know what was going on inside the hospital, or among which people he was in his surroundings, or what he was seeing and hearing all the while. On the mobile, he too must surely have been getting the news of the devastation caused by corona across the country. Nor do we know what must have gone on within him on hearing that news. He was a magnificent human being of very strong resolve, and, apart from good wishes for his healthy return, we could not so much as think of this or that — of what was going to happen two days later.


On 22 April, a message came from Sachin Jain, a social worker of the voluntary organization Vikas Samvad. It was a sorrowful piece of news — that Dr. Suresh Mishra was no longer among us. Personally, for me this was a heavy blow. In my own world, he was the one person with whom, together, I was weaving some dreams. With his joining, destiny had placed a great work in the hands of us both. My interest in history had bound me to him permanently. He was the one such person to come into my life who met me for only a very short while, and yet every day it felt as though some unfinished episode of a past life was now being completed. Over a life-journey longer than eight decades, many people had met him decades earlier too — and whoever had once met him had, until now, remained vividly connected with him.


I came into his life the very last of all. His least time is what I got. The whole one year after our meeting, corona had snatched away; and the moment its effect lessened, we went somewhere together for the first time. That was Udaipur. That thrilling experience had filled me with even more thrill toward the proposed journey to Bundelkhand. In the longing to do heritage walks and to see history in a new way, one more companion had joined us. He is Dr. Mukesh Mishra, who had said that he too was ready for this journey-experience with Dr. Suresh Mishra. Dr. Mukesh Mishra — originally of Uttar Pradesh — said that he would by no means let go of this opportunity to know Madhya Pradesh by travelling to far-flung parts, and that we would set out only with advanced-technology cameras and the like.


The moment Sachin Jain's message came, I first of all contacted Mukesh ji himself. Helplessly, sitting at home, we kept sharing this news with each other, and kept recalling when, and what, had last been discussed with him on the phone. He had, after all, been planning to get out of the hospital. Our resolve was to do something together for Udaipur. But now Dr. Mishra was not among us. He was consigned to the fire in the terrible atmosphere of corona. Deepak Sharma of Pragya Pravah went to the entrance gate of the Bhadbhada crematorium for his last *darshan*. Dr. Mishra had, by his skill in relationships and his readiness to be of use to all, fashioned his own extensive world. After bidding farewell to three daughters, one day his wife too had left him — and he used to tell her tales with a full heart. In his small dwelling, in a small covered campus on Kolar Road, Dr. Mishra was now entirely alone. But at all times cheerful, and forever ready to do something new, in the world of his books.


The journalist Abhilash Khandekar, once when he went to his house, returned very happy and said: what great experts live in Bhopal — we ought to sit among them, and visit them, regularly. Abhilash ji is a lover of the environment and of wildlife, and Dr. Mishra too had spent some of his life in a national park; he had even written a book. He could talk with Abhilash ji for hours. History was not his last frontier. He could hold long sessions with Sachin Jain and Rakesh Diwan on the displaced people of the Narmada valley, on malnutrition, and on the civilization of the Narmada valley. His creative world was as extensive as his interests, and as alive as his contacts.


The news of Dr. Mishra's departure reached every such person interested in Udaipur who had connected with us during the efforts of just the last few months. For everyone, it was, in a way, a shock. Whatever [one may say], this is the very reckoning of the world of the stars. It leaves us all somewhat cheated.


Udaipur is now, for us all, a resolve. A resolve of awakening. A resolve to do something. A resolve to connect with our history. I have, in the photographs of Udaipur's heritage walk, the last images of Dr. Mishra. Whatever now comes of the future plans for Udaipur, it will be dedicated to his blessed memory. A true tribute to a true historian can come only through this very awakening of Udaipur. Udaipur is waking. In its memories, too, there has now been recorded a human being who came just now and departed forever. Udaipur, like a mute witness, has treasured within itself the memory of this selfless well-wisher of hers. That Udaipur become a developed heritage town, teeming with the bustle of history-lovers and tourists — this alone was his dream, which he has gone leaving to us. The last dream of a historian…
