## Abstract
The Parmar (Paramara) dynasty occupies an important and prominent place in the history of India. Numerous references, genealogies, and historical notes related to the Parmars are found in a wide range of sources. A critical historical study of these materials provides deeper insight into the origins and development of the Paramara lineage. Based on ancient relics, inscriptions, and corroborated historical evidence, the Parmars are traditionally regarded as belonging to the Agnikula (fire-born) lineage. The narrative of their emergence from the sacrificial fire pit (*yajna-kunda*) performed by sage Vashishta appears consistently in inscriptions and literary traditions. According to Dr. Dasharatha Sharma, *"No dynasty other than the Paramaras should be regarded as an Agnikula lineage; only the Parmars can be identified as the true Agnikula clan."* Archaeological findings from Vasantgarh, Udaipur, Nagpur, Harthal, Delwara, Patnarayan, Achaleshwar, and other sites further support this view. Through this research paper, I seek to present the historical facts concerning the origin, growth, and lineage of the Paramara dynasty based on available archaeological and literary evidence.

## Introduction
In the accounts of the origin of the Parmar dynasty, a common feature is found in all the writings basically. A large number of Parmar branches are found to be of the Agni dynasty. Furthermore, it is noted that most historical events concerning the origin from the fire pit of Rishi Vashishta are consistently documented. The origin of the Paramara dynasty is described in the *"Navasahasankacharitra"* composed by the contemporary poet Padmagupta during the time of Sindhuraja, the father of the Paramara king Bhoja. Kavishri Padmagupta, describing it in *"Navasahasa Charitra"*, says, *"Mount Arbudachala (present day Mount Abu) is similar to the pillars of the mandalas of the universe. On this holy land is the tapo land of sage Vashishtha, a royal priest of the Ishvaku dynasty."* After that, an idea is also given about the Paramara.

Ancient and historical tools for studying the emergence of the Paramaras are found in Vasantgarh, Achleswar, Udaipur, Nagpur, Patnarayan, etc. In the *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari*, the famous Muslim writer Abul Fazl also describes the origin of the Paramaras. Even in Abul Fazl's description, the Paramaras are considered to be of the Agnivamsa. According to Abul Fazl, a sage named Mahabahu was doing penance by giving offerings in the fire place of his ashram 2,350 years prior to the Elahi Samvat (noted in contexts as corresponding to certain historical timelines like 818 AD / 762 AD). *(Note: In Ain-e-Akbari, Abul Fazl has mentioned a sage named Mahabahu. Elsewhere, sage Vashishtha is mentioned everywhere. Based on this, it can be meaningfully said that Abul Fazl must have named Vashishtha as Mahabahu Rishi).* The followers of Buddhism created obstacles in this penance through the influence of the local king. As a result, the people prayed to God, and God was pleased; a great, powerful man was born from the Agnikund, who put an end to the disturbances and restarted fire worship.

In many translations of *Prithviraj Raso*, this point is also validated. *Prithviraj Raso's* text in the Nagari script mentions Akbari themes in some contexts and ancient inscriptions in others. Based on all these references, it can be argued that the Parmara dynasty must have originated at the Agni Kund on Mount Abu. Mainly, all historical sites, studies, and source instruments point toward the same direction. Sages used to perform sacrifices on Mount Arbudachal (present-day Mount Abu). The number of heathens creating obstacles in the yajnas started increasing day by day. To destroy all these heathens, Rishi Vashishta, by his spiritual fervor, made offerings to the Agni Kund and produced the Chauhans, Pariharas, Parmars, and Solankis (Chaulukyas). Although specific historical information is not completely synchronised about all four dynasties together from early epigraphy, all four are considered fire dynasties in bardic traditions. While the early inscriptions do not always group the four together as an Agni dynasty, there is a separate mention in the Barot text by the Barot poet Chand. Still, deep scholars argue that Parmar Vamsa holds the primary claim as the true Agnivamsa because contemporary writers like Abul Fazal in *Ain-e-Akbari* and Padmagupta in *Navasahasika Charitra* distinctly focus on the Parmar dynasty as the fire dynasty.

Apart from the above, there are many mentions and statements describing the origin of the Paramaras. Among them, in the *Ramayana*, an ancient and important epic of Hinduism, Vishwamitra takes the cow named Kamadhenu from sage Vashishtha in Chapters 54 and 55 of Balkand. Sage Vashishtha gets angry over this and starts making offerings in the fire pit. By the influence of his austerities, four heroic men were born from the fire pit, in which successively Chauhan, Parihara, Parmar, and Solanki (Chaulukaya) are mentioned as prominent figures. In other words, the roots of the Parmar dynasty are also traceably aligned with this narrative structure.

## Rulers of the Early Parmar Dynasty
Below is a brief chronological and historical review of the major rulers of the Paramara dynasty, highlighting their administrative deeds and military campaigns. The original primary seat of the Parmaras was Malwa, and the branch established there is believed to be the main branch of the dynasty. The Parmar rulers of Malwa played an important part in the historical events of ancient India. Sometimes the Paramars were called **'Malvapati'** (Lord of Malwa) and sometimes they were honored with the title of **'Avanti Naresh'** (King of Avanti/Ujjain). Their capitals mainly included Ujjain and Dharnagari.

As far as the boundaries of their kingdoms are concerned, at its height, the empire extended to the present-day states of Kota and Bundi in the north, parts of Bhilsa, Hoshangabad, and Sagar Janapada in the east, up to the Godavari river and the territory of Khandesh in the south, and up to the Mahi river in the west (Ganguly, 2013, p. 11).

### Upendra (AD 800 to 818)
Among the names of the first kings in the Paramara dynasty, the lineage begins historically with a king named Upendra. Both the *'Udaipur Prashasti'* (a Sanskrit inscription) and *'Navasahasankacharitra'* (Shastri, 1867, Sarga 11, Śloka 14) mention King Upendra as the first great king. He held a prominent place for performing sacred rituals (home havan), and his praises were traditionally preserved in "Sita's song". According to the narration of *'Prabhandhchintamani'*, there was a poetess named Sita in the court of King Bhoja who recorded these traditions. It may also be that in *'Navasahasancharitra'* (Shastri, 1867), the poet formalized the reputation of King Upendra in textual verses (Shastri, 1867, p. 20).

The *'Udaipur reference'* indicates that after Upendra, rulers like Vairisingh I, Siyak I, and Vakpati I came to the throne, though the exact records for some intermediate figures like early Siyaka are sparse in certain charts. It is highly probable that several rulers governed during the timeline between Upendra and the later prominent kings.

### Vairi Singh I (AD 836 to 843)
After the end of King Upendra's immediate succession era, Vairisingh I came to the throne, appearing to have consolidated power around AD 836. This king played a prominent role in tax collection and the fortification/protection of borders. Specific detailed historical epigraphy about Vairisingh I remains limited.

### Siyaka I (AD 843 to 893)
Historical information is sparse regarding Siyak Pratham (Siyaka I), but traditional accounts record that he achieved multiple military victories over surrounding regional powers (Shastri, 1867, p. 22).

### Vakpati I (AD 893 to 918)
Regarding Vakpati Pratham, it is stated in verse 10 of the *Udaipur Prashasti* that the eyes of the princesses of Avanti were equal to the sun, indicating his undisputed sovereignty over this region. Court poets praised his military strength by metaphorically writing that his army drank the waters of the Ganges and the oceans, emphasizing the far-reaching impact of his campaigns (Shastri, 1867, pp. 22-23).

### Vairi Singh II (AD 918 to 948)
Following the reign of Vakpati I, his son Vairisingh II took over the reign, also known in records as *Vajrat*. During certain intervals, the Parmar territories faced severe pressure from the Gurjara-Pratiharas of Kanauj. However, Vairisingh II successfully won back the trust and control of his people through his personal prowess and strategic recovery (Shastri, 1867, pp. 23-24).

## Great Kings of the Parmar Dynasty: Vakpati II, Sindhuraj, and Bhoj
The Parmar dynasty stands out as an original, illustrious indigenous Rajput clan of India.

### Vakpati II (Vakpati Munj) and Sindhuraj
Vakpati Munj reigned supreme from AD 973 to 995. He was the adopted son and successor of Raja Siyak II. Vakpati Munj was a phenomenal warrior who defeated the Kalachuri ruler Yuvraj II and the Chalukya king Tailap II in battle. Tailap II was defeated by Vakpati Munj approximately 16 (sixteen) times. However, during the seventeenth campaign, Vakpati Munj was unfortunately captured and killed in enemy territory, an event mentioned across multiple inscriptions and highlighted in the *Ain-i-Akbari*.

Vakpati II brought great pride to the Parmara dynasty, assuming high titles like **'Srivallabh'**, **'Prithvi Vallabh'**, and **'Amodhavarsha'**. Based on the Kauthem copperplate grant, it is known that he also successfully defeated the Hunas. Apart from being an accomplished general, Vakpati Munj was a legendary patron of poets and scholars. Celebrated literary figures such as Padmagupta (author of *Navasahasankacharitra*), Dhananjaya (author of *Dasarupaka*), and Halayudha (author of *Yashorupavlik* / grammatical commentaries) lived under his state patronage, portraying the glorious journey of the Parmar dynasty in their literature (Ganguly, 2013, p. 23).

Vakpati Munj built a massive, famous lake named *Munj Sagar* in his capital, Dhar. He was succeeded on the throne by his younger brother, **Sindhuraj**, who assumed the titles of **'Narayan'** and **'Sahasank'**. Sindhuraj ruled in the late 10th century and was the father of the legendary king Bhoja. A detailed historical account of Sindhuraj's campaigns is preserved in Padmagupta’s *Navasahasankacharitra* and substantiated by the stone inscriptions of King Bhoja (Jain, 1972, p. 341).

#### The War with the Huns
During the expanding Parmar reign, Siyaka II had earlier initiated a robust campaign in the north-west against the Huna princes. Balavarman, the father of Yogaraja of Saurashtra, fought alongside and defeated the Huna prince Jajapa in battle. According to the descriptions in the *Navasahasankacharitra*, Siyaka crushed the enemy prince, devastating the opposing kingdom. These conflicts with the Hunas extended down towards the end of the tenth century. Additionally, according to the excavated inscriptions of Khajuraho, the Chandela kingdom had extended close to the borders of Malwa. Siyaka invaded the Chandela territories, though records from Khajuraho (925-950 AD) note that Chandela Yashovarma put up a fierce resistance (Shastri, 1867, Sarga 11, Śloka 90 & Ganguly, 2013, p. 21).

Historical facts show that Siyaka II was among the most glorious, independent, and sovereign (Chakravarti) rulers of the Paramara kingdom. According to Padmagupta's text, in his later years, Siyaka handed over the kingdom to his successor Vakpati Munj, renouncing worldly ties to become a sage and perform ascetic penances (Shastri, 1867, Sarga 11, Śloka 99).

### Great King Bhoj (AD 1000 to 1055)
Sindhuraj's son, Bhoj, ascended the throne of Malwa immediately after the passing of the previous era (Dwivedi, 1930/1986, p. 22). Traditional lore states that after the birth of Raja Bhoja, an astrological birth chart was prepared by the Raj Gurus, predicting that Raja Bhoja would rule the southern and central parts of the land gloriously for fifty-five years, seven months, and three days. Fearing that his own lineage might lose succession, Vakpati Munj initially felt threatened and ordered officials to secretly dispose of the young prince. However, recognizing the child's brilliance, the officials moved him to a safe, fixed location instead, sending a poetic prayer letter back to the King:

> *"King Mandhata, who was the ornament of Satyuga, has passed away. Where is Lord Ramachandra, the enemy of Ravana who built a bridge over the ocean? And by your time, Pandu's son Dharmaraj Yudhishthira and many other great kings came, where did they go? All these great men have left the world, but this earth remains right here; it did not go away with anyone. But I believe, it must definitely go with you."* (Dwivedi, 1930/1986, pp. 22-23)

Reading this profound message, the king realized his deep error, felt immense remorse, and immediately brought back and formally adopted the child. This account finds echo in sections of the *Ain-i-Akbari* as well, stating how the child was temporarily estranged due to an interpreted omen but swiftly restored to his rightful place. King Bhoja is associated with at least six major verified historical inscriptions (*Abhikles*) discovered so far.

When Raja Bhoj was alive, a famous court verse proclaimed:
> **अद्य धारा सदाधारा सदालम्बा सरस्वती।**
> **पण्डिता मण्डिताः सर्वे भोजराजे भुवि स्थिते ॥**
> *Meaning: Today, while Raja Bhoj is present on this earth, the city of Dhara is securely supported, the Goddess Saraswati has a permanent baseline protector, and all the Pandits and scholars are beautifully adorned and respected within the court.* (Ganguly, 2013, pp. 60-65)

Conversely, upon his death, the grieving scholars lamented:
> **अद्य धारा निराधारा निरालंबा सरस्वती ।**
> **पण्डिताः खण्डिताः सर्वे भोजराजे दिवं गते ||**
> *Meaning: Today, when Raja Bhoja departed for heaven, Dharanagari became an orphan; the shelter of the worshippers of Maa Saraswati was lost, and all the Pandits and scholars felt broken and fragmented.* (Mathur, 1969, p. 319)

Raja Bhoja was the ninth major ruler of the Parmar dynasty. Historical evidence confirms that the overall Paramara line managed regional governance from the 8th century down to the first half of the 14th century. Raja Bhoj fought numerous expansionist wars, elevating the dynasty to its zenith. He was also a legendary builder, commissioning magnificent temples, lakes, and universities across Malwa.

Raja Bhoj was not just a patron of education but an extraordinary polymath himself. He authored approximately **84 authoritative books** covering a vast range of subjects, including religion, geography, classical art, cell/organic design, civil architecture (Vastu Shastra), poetry, grammar, and medicine. His reign (1000 to 1055 AD) became synonymous with peak Indian golden age heritage, immortalizing him in popular Indian folklore through the famous proverb: *"Kahan Raja Bhoj, Kahan Gangu Taili"*.

#### Key Epigraphic Evidences from Raja Bhoja's Reign
* **Abhilekh from Banswara (Jan 7, 1020 / Vikrami Samvat 1076):** Records an annual festival celebrated following Bhoja's successful conquest of the Konkan region. It details the celebratory donation of land grants to learned Brahmins.
* **Abhilekh of Betma (1020 AD / Samvat 1076):** Contains crucial records regarding Raja Bhoja's administrative employees, local Brahmins, and village layouts. It mentions explicit land donations made by the King on the death anniversary of his parents and to commemorate the Konkan victory.
* **Ujjain Abhilekh (1021 AD / Samvat 1079):** States that the officer Veeranaka facilitated a village land donation to Dhanpati, the son of a Rigvedic Brahmin, under the direct authority of Parmar Raja Bhoje.
* **Abhilekh of Saraswati Pratima (Samvat 1011 / British Museum):** Inscribed on the stone base of a magnificent statue of Mother Saraswati. It mentions that the statue for Raja Bhoj's sacred spaces was crafted by Manthal, the son of the master sculptor Seehar.
* **Tilakwada Copperplate (Samvat 1103):** Proclaims the donation of land in the villages of Viluhaj and Ghantipalli for the maintenance and structural expansion of the Gareshwar temple, executed under the governance of Bhoja's feudatory, Rajpal Jashoraj.
* **Kallavan Abhilekh (Nashik, Maharashtra):** Records information regarding six town units and notes a major land gift during a solar eclipse, tracking closely with King Bhoja’s conquest of the Konkan kingdom around 1019–1020 AD.

#### Major Military Confrontations of King Bhoja
* **Battle with Indraratha:** According to the *Udaipur Prashasti*, while managing multi-front challenges alongside dealing with Chalukya Jayasimha, King Bhoja engaged in a fierce battle with a prince named Indraratha, achieving a decisive victory.
* **War with Lat:** King Bhoja launched campaigns into the Lat region (southern Gujarat), establishing strong administrative control over this highly prosperous trading zone.
* **War with Invading Frontiers:** When Raja Bhoja was consolidating power, Mahmud of Ghazni was conducting devastating raids into northwestern India. Evidence suggests that the Paramaras actively sent logistics and military contingents to assist Shahi ruler Anandapala against these foreign aggressions, finding an honorable mention in medieval historical chronicles.

Raja Bhoja emerged in history as an exceptionally beloved emperor. He endeavored to unite diverse territories under a centralized umbrella of cultural and political excellence, making Dhara Nagari a world-renowned capital of learning.

## Fall of the Parmar Dynasty
The golden era of the independent Rajput blocks faced severe internal shifts towards the late 12th century. In neighboring Gujarat, the powerful Chaulukya rule deteriorated rapidly following the death of King Kumarapala in AD 1172. His successor, Ajayapala, lacked administrative competence, and his tyrannical governance triggered rapid decline. Within a short reign of four years, Ajayapala was assassinated in 1176 AD following conflicts with Pratihara factions (Dwivedi, 1930/1986, p. 164).

Meanwhile, in Malwa, successive Paramara rulers like Arjunavarman and Jayasimha engaged in exhaustive, bloody wars with the Chalukyas of Gujarat, which eventually ended in weakening treaties. Seizing this opportunity of exhaustion, the Yadavas of Devagiri launched massive invasions into Malwa from the south, emerging victorious and structurally breaking the back of Paramara military power.

Compounding these internal mutual wars among regional Indian kingdoms, external Islamic invasions gained rapid momentum. During the era when Devapala Paramara was entangled in regional counter-offensives against Gujarat, Delhi Sultanate forces managed to breach the lower eastern borders of Malwa, seizing critical strategic outposts. To make matters worse, Virdhavan's son, Visaladeva of Gujarat, ascended the throne in AD 1243 and launched a retaliatory invasion into Malwa. According to the Dabhoi inscription (*Dabhoi Utkirna article*), Visaladeva achieved immense success by overwhelming the joint defenses of the rulers of Malwa and the Deccan.

Thus, as a direct consequence of continuous multi-front wars with neighboring states, economic depletion, and failure to forge a permanent unified front against foreign incursions, the once-mighty empire of the Parmara dynasty was gradually dismantled. Nonetheless, the Parmar dynasty remains immortalized as one of the most culturally, architecturally, and politically significant lineages in the rich history of India.
