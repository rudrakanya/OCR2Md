## Author's Note
Sacred architecture seeks to express the ineffable through material resources. Its spatial and temporal settings provide a connective between the human and the sacred. How is such architecture to be evaluated? Is it to be viewed as an art object or a site of religious practices? Is the art meaningful as a symbol of a metaphysical vision, or does the language of design evoked by a world of sensory richness and material resources create meaning of its own? Does awareness of the religious meanings embedded in the architectural composition of the buildings alter the ways of seeing?

The unresolved tensions between sacred and aesthetic tropes fashion the critical discourse for interpreting monuments with religious significance, often characterizing and objectifying it in technical detail or codifying it into a system of signifiers deriving its meanings from a transcendental source. The challenging task in studying sacred architecture is co-situating perspectives. In sacred architecture like temples, autonomous fields such as art, religion, and architecture intersect; their meanings lie in these intersections. Interpreting these intersections implies knowledge of multiple domains and the ability to see connections.

The study of a temple is an opportunity for exploring the way multiple perspectives converge to create a state of mind, evoke a certain emotion, articulate a belief system, and inspire faith in it. Technical choices serve specific evocative functions. The larger context from which the temple derives its meaning is the metaphysic of divinity—the philosophical discourse that seeks to understand God and the First Principles of Being. The temple manifests the un-manifest. Since a temple is primarily a religious edifice, it is important to take into account its religious and sectarian affiliations along with its architectonic, aesthetic, and iconographic facets.

This book is the result of more than a decade of studying the Udayeśvara temple in a holistic manner from primary textual sources, including prominent *Śilpaśāstras* and *Śaivāgamas*.

## I. Architecture and Sculpture of the Udayeśvara Temple

### Introduction
The Udayeśvara temple at Udayapur, situated in the Malwa region of Madhya Pradesh (modern-day Vidisha), ranks as one of the most important early medieval temples of North India. It is a Śaiva temple built in the second half of the 11th century CE by Udayāditya Paramāra, a scion of the illustrious Paramāra dynasty that ruled over Malwa.

Udayāditya ascended the throne in 1070 CE and faced many challenges in consolidating his empire. The *Udayapur Praśasti* highlights his military might, describing him as:
> "....another sun, as it were, destroying the dense darkness in the form of his magnanimous foes with the rays issuing from his strong sword and thereby gladdening the hearts of his people by his splendour."

During Udayāditya's reign, Malwa witnessed political stability and flourished as a leading center of religious and cultural activities. He founded the city of Udayapur and constructed several temples, the most famous being the Udayeśvara temple.

### The Bhūmija Style of Architecture
The Udayeśvara temple is built in the distinctive **Bhūmija** style of architecture. The *Samarāṅgaṇasūtradhāra* of Bhoja Paramāra is one of the earliest *Śilpaśāstras* to refer to the Bhūmija style. The style appears so well established during this period that it clearly had earlier antecedents, though the Paramāras of Malwa developed it to its full maturity. This is supported by textual references as well as the high concentration of Bhūmija temples found in Malwa, Rajasthan, and northern Maharashtra.

#### Etymology and Concept of Bhūmija:
1. **Earthly Origin:** Literally, *Bhūmija* means "produced or born from the earth," connoting regional affiliations as the native style of Malwa.
2. **Secular/Terrestrial Evolution:** It can be interpreted as a terrestrially evolved form as opposed to other styles believed to have a divine or supernatural origin.
3. **Born from Storys (*Bhūmis*):** It connotes the arrangement of *kūṭas* (miniature spires) arranged vertically one above the other from the ground to the *śikhara*, giving the appearance of rising directly from the ground upwards.

#### Key Features of the Style:
* A *śikhara* adorned with four *latās* (flat vertical spines/bands) containing an intricate lace-work of *gavākṣa* (caitya-arch) motifs in the four cardinal directions.
* Miniature *śikharas* of diminishing heights (called *kūṭastambhas*) arranged in the four quadrants between the *latās*. These are positioned in rows of 3 to 5 vertically and 5 to 9 horizontally.
* At the base of the *latās* on the north, south, and west sides are prominent sculpted medallions within a large caitya arch known as a *śūrasenaka*.
* The eastern *lata* terminates in a projecting *śukanāsa* (fronton) masking the front face of the spire.
* Temples of this class are invariably *nirandhara* (without an ambulatory path).

### Layout and Ground Plan
The temple is built of red sandstone and faces east. Its plan features a unique geometric layout where the plan is **stellate-cum-circular**, being *saptaratha* (seven-faceted) on plan and *saptabhūmi* (seven-storied) in elevation.

Unlike standard designs that subdivide a square plan like a grid, the Bhūmija plan rotates the square around its center (*parivartanā*), stopping at regular intervals. The corners of the rotating square map out a circle, dictating the alignment of the exterior walls.

The complex consists of:
1. **Garbhagṛha** (Sanctum sanctorum)
2. **Antarāla** (Vestibule)
3. **Gūḍhamaṇḍapa** (Covered hall for devotees) with three projecting balconies/**Mukhamaṇḍapas** (porches) on the east, north, and south sides.
4. **Vedī** (A low, separate, flat-roofed hall aligned on the same axis in front of the temple).
5. **Jagati** (A lofty, extensive platform supporting the main temple), originally surrounded by eight subsidiary shrines (*aṣṭatāyatana* complex), quite a few of which are now broken.
6. **Entrance:** The original eastern entrance featured a grand stairway flanked by over-life-size figures of *Śaiva pratīhāras* (door-keepers). Modern access is from the western side.

### Architectural Mouldings and Ornamentation

#### 1. The Pīṭha (Base/Plinth)
The base consists of five distinct structural mouldings:
* **Bhiṭṭa 1:** The lowest course of the plinth, plain in execution.
* **Bhiṭṭa 2:** Ornamented with leaf (*patra*) and garland (*mālā*) designs, capped by a shoulder (*skandha*) decorated with lotus petals (*padmadala*).
* **Jādyakumbha:** A prominent inverted cyma-recta moulding shaped like a lotus, detailed with rosettes on the sides and *thakāra* (caitya-window motifs) along the top.
* **Karṇaka:** An arris/knife-edge moulding decorated with *gagarapaṭṭi* (an arrow motif framed between two spirals).
* **Grāsapaṭṭi:** A decorative band composed of gorgon heads (*grāsa* or *kīrtimukha* frames).

#### 2. The Maṇḍovara (Middle Wall Portion)
The *maṇḍovara* comprises the section above the base but below the roof lines, divided into the *vedibandha* and the *jaṅghā*:
* **Vedibandha Mouldings:** Consists of *khura* (decorated with a *ratnapaṭṭi* diamond band), *kumbha* (pot-shaped moulding ornamented with miniature shrine niches called *khaṭṭikās* housing deities, a *maṇibandha* diamond band, and lotus-petal shoulders), and *kalaśa* (torus moulding hemmed between two *karṇikās* and decorated with *gagarapaṭṭi*).
* **Jaṅghā (Upper Wall):** The buttresses take the appearance of faceted pillars decorated with large, beautifully carved niches (*khaṭṭakas*) housing major gods and goddesses. Deep recesses separate these pillar-like buttresses.
* **Crowning Members:** The buttresses terminate upwards with capital-shaped mouldings, topped by an ornamental fillet, a deep recess, and a roof-shaped eave moulding (*chādya*).

The vertical alignments of these pillar-like buttresses extend seamlessly from the ground into the *śikhara*, transforming into the rows of *kūṭastambhas*. One below the other, they resemble long pearl strings suspended from the spire.

### Key Structural & Sculptural Compositions

#### The Śikhara & The Flag-Bearer
The *śikhara* rises as a majestic arrangement of *kūṭastambhas* that gradually decrease in size towards the *skandha* (shoulder of the spire), echoing the star-shaped plan with a five-pointed serrated edge in each sub-quarter. This theme is repeated in the corrugated rim of the heavy crowning *āmalaka*. On the north-west face of the *śikhara*, an unusual sculpture depicts a human figure climbing the spire; this represents a flag-bearer hosting the victory flag (*dhvaja*) atop the temple.

#### The Śukanāsa (Eastern Fronton)
The massive *śukanāsa* over the eastern *latā* forms a precisely balanced, unitary composition focused around a large top *kīrtimukha* flanked by *makaras* (crocodiles) and thick, serpentine foliage containing human figures playing musical instruments.
* **Upper Part:** A large caitya-gavākṣa window hosting a multi-armed figure of **Naṭeśa Śiva** performing the *tāṇḍava* dance in the *ūrdhvajānu karaṇa*. He features a conical *jaṭā-kalāpa* (matted locks), balanced facial structure, and is surrounded by dancing devis and *gaṇas*.
* **Lower Part:** Separated into three segments. The central bay houses an ornate framed niche containing a syncretic image of **Hariharārkāpitāmaha** (combining Śiva, Viṣṇu, Sūrya, and Brahmā).
* **Flanking Goddesses:** * *Right Arch:* A dancing devi imitating Śiva's *ūrdhvajānu* posture. She wears a knotted breast-band (*stanapaṭa*), a three-tiered *karaṇḍa mukuṭa*, and holds a *ḍamaru* (hour-glass drum).
  * *Left Arch:* A rare representation of **Dancing Sarasvatī** (Goddess of Learning). Her vehicle, the swan, is carved near her right leg. Though rare as a dancer, her inclusion reflects the patronage of learning by King Bhoja Paramāra (who consecrated the Bhojaśālā of Dhara to Sarasvatī). She performs the *ūrdhvajānu karaṇa* and holds a *ḍamaru*, wearing a notable necklace mimicking long lotus buds.

#### Celestial Mansions of the Śukanāsa
The *śukanāsa* displays a five-storied celestial mansion layout on its side faces, populated by seated images of Śiva and various forms of *Sāktā* or *Śaiva* goddesses:
* **North Side Devis (from top to bottom):** Identified as Mahālakṣmī, Sarvamaṅgalā, Rāmbhā, Maheśvarī, and Gaurī. They carry attributes such as snakes, swords, arrows, tridents, bowls, skulls, bows, and water-pots. *Nandī* is carved at the bottom right.
* **South Side Devis:** Contains a notable image of **Śreyā/Śrī** seated on a *preta* (corpse/ghost) while holding an iguana (*godhā*) in her lower right hand, symbolizing the destruction of foes. Another tier depicts a Natesa image looking up at Śiva.

#### The Three Śūrasenakas
At the base of the other three *latās* are the smaller *śūrasenaka* compositions:
* **West Latā:** Features a powerful central image of **Śiva Naṭeśa** dancing in *ūrdhvajānu*. Below him sits a six-armed **Mṛtyuñjaya Śiva** in a meditative *yogamudrā* pose, adorned with snake ornaments (*sarpa-kuṇḍala*, *sarpa-yajñopavīta*). This figure was specifically invoked to grant victory and overcome the danger of death on the battlefield.
* **South Latā:** Houses a similar image of Śiva dancing in *ūrdhvajānu*, with a seated Śiva below in a pyramidal shrine.
* **North Latā:** Depicts Śiva dancing the *tāṇḍava*, accompanied by a seated Śiva on a bell-shaped cushion layout holding a trident and a *khaṭvāṅga* (skull-topped staff).

#### Garbhagṛha Exterior Walls (Sanctum Facets)
* **South Latā / South Kapilī:** Houses **Gaṇeśa** and his female power **Gaṇeśānī** (four-armed, elephant-headed, holding an axe and a bowl of sweetmeats). Nearby is an image of **Virabhadra** holding a *vīṇā*.
* **West Latā (Rear Facet):** Features a headless figure of **Virabhadra** strangling a figure (likely Dakṣa Prajāpati). Also displays **Nandīśvara** (a human body with a bull's head) and **Bhṛṅgī** (emaciated, three-legged dancer).
* **North Latā / North Kapilī:** Features classic divine couples including **Umā-Maheśvara**, **Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa** (Viṣṇu encircling Lakṣmī's waist), and **Brahmā-Brahmāṇī**.

### Internal Architecture and the Sanctum

#### The Balconies and Doorways (Mukhamaṇḍapas)
The doorways leading to the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* are elaborately carved:
* **Doorway Elements:** Feature *patraśākhā* (foliage scrolls), *gandharvaśākhā* (flying celestial figures), and *stambhaśākhā* (miniature pilasters). At the bottom of the jambs are the river goddesses **Gaṅgā** and **Yamunā**, alongside *Śaiva* guardians and Gaṇeśa.
* **Lintels:** The upper lintels host panels of the **Navagrahas** (Nine Planets). The south porch displays distinct carvings of *Rāhu* (represented as a large face/head without a body), *Ketu* (with a snake tail), and *Saturn*.
* **Ceiling & Roof:** Supported by dwarf pillars of the *ghaṭapallava* (pot-and-foliage) class. The roof of the *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* is a grand **samvaraṇā** (bell-roof) built of stepped tiers.

#### The Interior Structure
The *gūḍhamaṇḍapa* interior is organized around **four massive central pillars** that shift in geometry from square bases to polygonal mid-sections, ending in circular ring capitals topped by bracket figures of *yakṣas* or *vidyādharas*. The ceiling is a spectacular circular dome featuring deep concentric bands carved with *kīrtimukhas*, lotus patterns, and hanging pendants (*padmaśīla*).

#### The Sanctum (Garbhagṛha)
An *antarāla* (vestibule) leads to the square sanctum. The doorway features standard guardian figures and a central panel of Gaṇeśa on the lintel. Inside sits the **Śivalinga**, the primary deity. It is encased in an intricately chased brass cover containing a *mukha* (carved face), dedicated historically during later restoration efforts (gifted by Khanderao Appaji, a general of Mahadji Scindia, in 1775 CE).

### The Vedī (Sacrificial Hall)
In front of the temple complex, situated on the same structural axis, stands the **Vedī**. It is a low, flat-roofed, square stone building (measuring roughly 23 feet square) supported by four massive pillars.
* **Design:** The structure is enclosed by a stone balustrade (*vedikā*) detailed with diamond patterns and open stone grills (*gavākṣa jālā*) for ventilation.
* **Purpose:** According to archaeological reports by Alexander Cunningham and local tradition, the *vedī* was constructed immediately after the temple's completion to serve as a public hall for the preaching of the Vedas, performance of *yajñas* (sacrifices), and sacred rituals.

### Historical Changes and Later Modifications
The Udayeśvara temple complex reflects the turbulent history of the Malwa region:
1. **14th Century Incursions:** During the reign of the Delhi Sultanate (Muhammad Tughluq/Alauddin Khalji), Muslim armies captured the Malwa region. In 1325 CE, an order was issued to dismantle or repurpose parts of the complex.
2. **Mosque Conversion:** The western rear side of the outer *jagati* and the *vedī* area were modified to incorporate a mosque layout. A *miḥrāb* (prayer niche) was constructed on the western wall using stone blocks salvaged from dismantled subsidiary shrines. Two Persian inscriptions on the mosque record these architectural conversions.
3. **The Aurangzeb Period:** Local legends note that Emperor Aurangzeb later visited the site, ordering further defacement of the exterior sculptures (resulting in the high number of broken limbs/heads observed on the lower *vedibandha* niches).
4. **Maratha Restoration:** During the 18th century, the Maratha Scindia dynasty (*Maharajas of Gwalior*) re-established Hindu worship at the site, installing the protective brass *mukhaliṅga* cover over the ancient Śivalinga and securing the complex under state protection.
