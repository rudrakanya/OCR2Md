### CHAPTER I: How Madhya Bharat Was Formed
With the formal declaration of independence on the 15th August, 1947, the paramount power of the British in India vis-a-vis the States ceased to exist automatically. The relations of these States, numbering over 500, to the Government of India, politically and constitutionally, were, therefore, a problem of the greatest consequence not only for the stability of hard-won independence but also for the continued progress and well-being of the whole country.

The problem had become more complicated and difficult since some of these States, like Hyderabad, Mysore, Gwalior, etc., were large enough to be considered as viable units while quite a large number of them were so small that they could hardly be considered as self-contained administrative units. Even under the paramountcy of the British, attempts were made to group them suitably, but the manner in which these States were later welded together into sizeable groups or unions of States goes a great deal to reflect on the political sagacity of the late Sardar Patel, who was mainly responsible for this momentous achievement.

Madhya Bharat was the Sardar's first successful experiment. The Union here was of 22 States of which Gwalior was the largest, covering more than one half of its area and population. The second major state was that of Indore, while amongst the other 20 minor States, Dhar may be said to be largest.

Historically, the existence of most of these important States may be traced to the conquest of the whole territory by the Marathas of the Deccan in the 18th century. The confederate chiefs of the Marathas, such as the Scindias, Holkars, and the Pawars, had carved out principalities for themselves north of the Vindhyas under the central control of the Peshwas of Poona on the decline of the Mughal Empire of Delhi. With the final emergence of the British as the only paramount power in India in the early 19th century and with the fall of the Peshwas, the confederacy came to an end and the Maratha Chiefs were now obliged to pay homage to the new sovereign power of India. The States of Gwalior, Indore, Dhar, and Dewas (Senior and Junior) were therefore ruled by Maratha Princes, while the other smaller States such as Barwani, Rajgadh, Narsingarh, etc., were headed by local Rajput Chiefs, excepting a few, such as Kurwai and Jaora, ruled by Muslim Chiefs.

On April 22, 1948, the rulers of the 22 States signed a covenant which ushered into being the "United State of Gwalior, Indore and Malwa" which was included later on in the constitution of the Indian Republic under the name of Madhya Bharat. The rulers of Gwalior and Indore were elected as the Rajpramukh and Up-Rajpramukh for life, and the rulers of Dhar and Khilchipur as Junior Up-Rajpramukhs respectively. The formal inauguration took place on May 28, in the historic Jai Vilas Palace of Gwalior at the hands of the Hon'ble Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru. On June 16, the rulers of Gwalior and Indore formally handed over to the Rajpramukh the administration of their respective States. Other States followed in due course of time, and the process of transfer was completed by September, 1948.

The State of Madhya Bharat covers an area of 46,785 sq. miles with a population of 79,50,494. Of this, the two major States of Gwalior and Indore covered an area of 26,387 and 9,934 sq. miles respectively with a population of more than 55 lakhs. From the point of view of administrative convenience, the States of Rajasthan and Madhya Bharat exchanged the villages lying in the enclaves of the other's territory. This resulted in a more compact territory for both, though the enclave of Sironj went to Rajasthan. Madhya Bharat is surrounded by Uttar Pradesh, Vindhya Pradesh, and Bhopal on the east, by Madhya Pradesh on the south, Bombay on the south-west, and Rajasthan on the west and north. Under the award of Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, Gwalior is the winter capital of Madhya Bharat for 7 to 7½ months (September to April), while Indore is the summer capital for 4½ to 5 months (April to September) every year.

### CHAPTER II: The Geographical Setting of Madhya Bharat
Madhya Bharat occupies a central position in relation to the whole country. It consists of an elongated stretch of land disposed towards the north-east and south-west, bounded in the north and north-west by the river Chambal and demarcated in the south by the Satpura ranges. At the centre, the stretch is much narrowed down by the protruding territories of Rajasthan and Bhopal, leaving the districts of Mandsor and Bhilsa projecting in isolation towards the north-west and east respectively. A narrow strip hangs down from its north-eastern end into the territories of the adjoining Vindhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. A considerable portion of the north and north-east follows the course of the river Betwa, while the south is watered by the sacred Narmada.

The physical features of this land are characterized by the three river systems of the Chambal, Betwa, and Narmada, and by the mountain systems of the Vindhyas and the Satpuras. These features divide the State into five natural divisions:

1. **The Northern Madhya Bharat (Gwalior, Morena, Bhind, Shivapuri, and Guna districts):** Most of it is hilly and mountainous due to the northern offshoots of the Vindhyas encroaching upon it, leaving a small flat low-lying tip at the north for the rivers to join the great Gangetic plain. These physical aspects are well reflected in the numerous fortresses such as Gwalior, Narwar, and Chanderi, and in the numerous stone-built temples, materials for which were available from the local sandstone hills. In ancient geographical nomenclature, this region formed part of the country of the Shurasenas, the Vatsas, or the Matsyadesha. The country about Narwar (Nalapura) is believed to be the land of Nishadha where the Epic king Nala ruled, while the region of Chanderi is said to have formed part of the Chedi kingdom of Shishupala. In late historical times, it was divided into *ghars* or houses of various Rajput clans (e.g., Tonwarghar, Bhadarwar, Sikarwar, Kacchavaghar, Khichiwada).
2. **The North-Eastern Plateau (Bhilsa district falling in line with the Bhopal territory):** A narrow out-stretch of the central plateau of Malwa made distinctive by the wooded ranges of the Vindhyas encircling it from the east, north, and south. Proximity to the fertile Gangetic plains made the region about Bhilsa prosperous—a fact reflected in the richness of its monuments, second to none in Madhya Bharat. In ancient geography, it was called Dasharna, known from the days of the Buddha to those of Kalidasa (c. 5th century A.D.). The name lives on in the modern Dhasan river. In late historical times, it came to be known as part of Gondwana, ruled by the Gonds. Ancient Vidisha and its successor Bhilsa have always been the focal points here.
3. **The Central Malwa Plateau (Rajgadh, Shajapur, Dewas, Ujjain, Indore, Ratlam, and Dhar districts):** With an average height of 1500–1700 feet above sea level, making its climate soothing and temperate, it is one of the most fertile tracts in India. The black cotton soil encouraged intensive agriculture but afforded little ready stone for temple building, except on its mountainous southwestern tip at Mandu hill. This plateau was anciently called Avanti; later, the Malavas, who made it their home, gave it the name of Malwa. The holy city of Ujjain (Avantikapura) was the historic pride of this region.
4. **The North-Western Plateau (Mandsor and part of Ratlam district):** Projecting into the rugged country of Rajasthan, its physical features are hilly and rugged. In the early centuries of the Christian era, the republican tribe of the Malavas migrated and settled here after leaving their original homeland in the Punjab, where they had resisted Alexander the Great. The town of Dashapura (Mandsor) was their mainstay, and the rugged tract provided building stone for edifices whose ruins survive in the countryside.
5. **The Narmada Valley or Nimar Plain (Nimar and parts of Dhar, Indore, and Dewas districts):** This low-lying narrow valley enclosed by the Vindhyas and Satpuras to the north and south contains some of the earliest remains of civilization in India. It was anciently called Anupadesha. Within its limits on the banks of the river stood the ancient township of Mahishmati (modern Maheshwar), a cultural and political hub from antiquity down to late historical times.

### CHAPTER III: Peoples and Languages of Madhya Bharat
The total population of the State borders on 8 million, scattered across 21,900 villages and a few large urban centers, giving a density of about 170 souls per square mile. The highest concentration is around Indore (350+ per sq. mile) and the well-irrigated northern tip near Gwalior along the Chambal and Sindh rivers. The state claims three major cities with a population over a lakh: Indore (3,10,859), the premier industrial trade emporium; Gwalior (2,41,577), an admixture of ancient royal history and modern governance centered around its towering fortress; and Ujjain (1,29,817), the sacred locus of antiquity.

On a religious basis, the population includes 73,33,021 Hindus (90%), 4,96,153 Muslims (6%), 1,00,234 Jains (1.2%), 12,621 Sikhs, and 219 Buddhists. Culturally, the Muslim population shares deep regional ties with their Hindu neighbors, featuring a prominent mercantile section of Bohras concentrated around Ujjain. The Jains have traditionally played an outsized role in the industrial and commercial life of the territory.

The Hindu population contains nearly 11,11,716 Adivasis (scheduled aboriginal tribes) and 13,23,981 Harijans. The aboriginal tribes inhabit the hilly tracts of the Vindhyas and Satpuras in the south and south-west. Among them, the Bhilalas claim Rajput-Bhil lineage and follow mainstream Hindu customs. The Bhils constitute the bulk of the tribal populace, divided occupationally into settled cultivators and forest hill-men. The typical Bhil is short-statured, dark-complexioned, strong-built, and broad-nosed. Their dialect is a mixture of Gujarati, Malwi, and Marathi, with Munda words. Other tribes include the Korkus, Mundas, Gonds (inhabiting the eastern belt near Bhilsa/Gondwana), and Meenas (in the northern Chambal tracts of Shivapuri). The agricultural and landed gentry in the countryside includes various Rajput families (Tomars, Bhadorias, Kacchawahas, Jadhos, Sikarwars, Khichis, Chandellas, Bundelas, Pawars, etc.), alongside the Gujars, Jats, and Ahirs in the north.

Linguistically, the entire state belongs to the Indo-Aryan family, categorized under the Inner Sub-branch (Central Group of Western Hindi and Rajasthani). Northern Madhya Bharat speaks Western Hindi, with its sub-dialects of Brij-Bhasha in the extreme north and Bundeli in the central-northern parts. The central plateau uses Malwi (the southern variety of Rajasthani), though standard literary Hindi is increasingly adopted through rising literacy. Mandsor and Ratlam fall within the Rajasthani sphere, while the Nimar plain uses Nimadi, which shows a clear blend of Malwi, Marathi, and Gujarati.

### CHAPTER IV: Historical Pre-View of Madhya Bharat
Ancient Puranic tradition links Madhya Bharat to the Lunar race of King Yayati, who assigned the territories watered by the Chambal (*Charmanvati*) and Ken (*Shuktimati*) to his son Yadu. His lineage split into the Yadavas in the north and the Haihayas in the south. The Haihayas dominated the Narmada valley, where King Muchukunda founded the city of Mahishmati, later elevated to a great capital by Kartivirya Arjuna. From one of his sons, Avanti, the central region derived its name. Another ancient branch ruled at Vidisha (Bhilsa).

By the 6th century B.C., Avanti emerged as an independent kingdom under the Pradyota dynasty. Its greatest ruler, Chanda Pradyota, a contemporary of the Buddha, ruled from Ujjain, making it a powerful commercial hub linking the Gangetic plains to western ports like Bharukaccha (Bharuch) and Surparaka (Sopara). Ajatashatru of Magadha feared Pradyota's military might so much that he fortified his capital, Rajagriha. The Pradyotas were eventually subverted by the Shaishunagas of Magadha, and Avanti was incorporated into successive Magadhan empires (Nandas and Mauryas). Under the Mauryas, Ujjain served as a key viceroyalty; Prince Ashoka served as Viceroy here, marrying Devi, the daughter of a merchant of Vidisha, who bore him Mahendra and Sanghamitra. Ashoka laid the foundation of the Great Stupa at Sanchi and erected a monolithic column there.

#### 2nd Century B.C. to 3rd Century A.D.
In the 2nd century B.C., the Shungas supplanted the Mauryas. Pushyamitra Shunga's son, Crown Prince Agnimitra, ruled as Viceroy at Vidisha, an event immortalized in Kalidasa's *Malavikagnimitra*. The Shunga hold over Vidisha is verified by the famous Besnagar column inscription of Heliodoros, an ambassador sent by the Indo-Greek King Antialcidas of Taxila to the court of King Bhagabhadra. Heliodoros converted to the Bhagavata sect and erected the *Garudadhvaja* pillar in honor of Vasudeva, proving that Vidisha was an early stronghold of orthodox Vaishnavism.

Following the collapse of the Shungas and Kanvas at the hands of the Satavahanas (Andhras) under Simuka in the 1st century B.C., Malwa came under southern influence. However, by the late 1st century A.D., the Kushans under Kanishka established control over northern Madhya Bharat, governing through western satraps (*Kshatrapas*) like Nahapana. Around 124 A.D., the Satavahana King Gautamiputra Satakarni revived southern fortunes, conquering Anupa (Nimar), Akara (East Malwa), and Avanti (West Malwa) from Nahapana. This territory was soon lost again to the Shaka Kshatrapas under Chastana and Rudradaman; Ptolemy's *Geography* (c. 150 A.D.) records *Ozene* (Ujjain) as the capital of Chastana.

Concurrently, the regions around Gwalior and Bhilsa were ruled by the stable indigenous Naga dynasties, operating from principalities at Kantipuri (Kotwal), Padmavati (Pawaya), and Vidisha, leaving behind vast hoards of copper coins and early Brahmanical imagery.

#### 4th to 6th Century A.D. (The Gupta Period)
In the 4th century A.D., Samudragupta integrated Madhya Bharat into the Gupta Empire. As recorded in the Allahabad Pillar Inscription, he supplanted the Nagas of Padmavati and northern India, receiving homage from the republican Malavas (then settled around Mandsor) and the Sanakanikas of Bhilsa. Chandragupta II consolidated these conquests through his historic campaigns against the Shakas of Western Malwa, a feat recorded in the rock-cut caves of Udaygiri.

This "Golden Age" of prosperity left behind remarkable monuments: terraced brick structures at Pawaya, rock-cut architecture at Udaygiri, inscriptions and columns at Mandsor, and the magnificent murals of the Bagh Caves. The brick temple at Pawaya yielded fine terracotta heads and a large stone *torana* lintel depicting a dance performance and Bali's sacrifice. Classical Hindu imagery (Durga, Seven Mothers, Vishnu, and Ekamukha Lingas) flourished at Besnagar, while Buddhist art reached a technical peak in the tempera paintings of Bagh.

By 468 A.D., Huna invasions under Toramana and Mihirakula fractured the empire, penetrating as far as Gwalior and Eran. The Malavas reasserted themselves under Yashodharman, who defeated the Hunas in 532–533 A.D. and erected twin monolithic pillars of victory at Sondni (Mandsor), making Dashapura his imperial capital.

#### 7th to 13th Century A.D.
Following the brief intervention of Harsha of Kanauj (who destroyed Devagupta of Malwa to avenge Grahavarman), the region split into principalities under various Rajput tribal chiefs. Northern Madhya Bharat was controlled by the Gurjara-Pratiharas of Kanauj (verified by two inscriptions dated 875–876 A.D. in Gwalior Fort), while the Nimar plain and eastern Malwa were contested by the southern Rashtrakutas (verified by the Pathari pillar inscription of 861 A.D. under King Parabala). Shrines from this transitional era survive at Amrol, Kota, Mahua, Terahi, and Dhamnar. The Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang (640 A.D.) recorded visiting *Molopo* (Western Malwa under the Buddhist prince Shiladitya), Ujjayini, and Maheshwarapura.

The decline of the Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas in the late 10th century cleared the way for two major regional Rajput dynasties: the **Paramaras** in Malwa and the **Kacchavahas** in Gwalior.
* **The Paramaras of Malwa:** Originating as Rashtrakuta feudatories in Gujarat, they shifted to Malwa under Siyaka II, who plundered Manyakheta in 971 A.D. and assumed sovereign titles. His successor Vakpati II Munja (974–995 A.D.) was a great general, poet, and literary patron who defeated the Cholas, Chalukyas, and Chedis before losing his life to Taila II. Munja was succeeded by **Bhoja the Great (1010–1050 A.D.)**, whose claim to immortality rests on his profound authorship of architectural treatises (*Samarangana Sutradhara*), literary treatises, and his unmatched patronage of Sanskrit learning. Bhoja established a great college (*Bhoja Shala*) at his capital, Dhar, and founded the city of Bhojpur. In architecture, the Paramara era witnessed the building of superb temples at Udaypur, Nemawar, Un, Jamli, and Badnawar. The dynasty declined after a joint attack on Bhoja's capital by the Chalukyas of Gujarat and the Kalachuris of Chedi. Udayaditya (1058–1087 A.D.) restored family fortunes and built the spectacular Nilakanteshvar temple at Udaypur. The line was ultimately subverted by Muslim invasions in the early 14th century.
* **The Kacchavahas of Gwalior:** Establishing themselves in the late 11th century, they ruled from three branches at Gwalior, Narwar, and Dubkund. The Gwalior branch is famous for the building of the Sas Bahu temples, completed in 1093 A.D. by Prince Mahipala. They executed widespread stone temple architecture at Suhania, Surwaya, Mitaoli, Padhavli, Kadwaha, and Ranod, using the local sandstone quarries.

#### Muslim Invasions and Regional Sultanates
The Kacchavahas faced Muslim incursions early. Mahmud of Ghazni laid siege to Gwalior in 1023 A.D., compelling its chief to pay tribute. Mohammad Ghori reduced the principality, and Qutb-ud-din Aibak captured it in 1197 A.D. Gwalior was temporarily liberated but was reconquered by Shams-ud-din Iltutmish in 1231 A.D. after a desperate 11-month siege. Iltutmish then marched south into Malwa, capturing the fort of Bhilsa and sacking Ujjain. Balban consolidated Delhi's authority over Gwalior, Chanderi, Narwar, and Malwa in 1251 A.D.

The Paramara kingdom was finally destroyed in 1305 A.D. when Alauddin Khilji's general, Ain-ul-Mulk, smashed the Hindu forces at Mandu, killing Mahlakadeva and converting Malwa into a province of the Delhi Empire. In 1401 A.D., the governor Dilawar Khan Ghori asserted independence. His son, Alp Khan, assumed the name **Hoshang Shah (1405–1432 A.D.)** and transferred the capital permanently to fortified Mandu. Hoshang Shah expanded his boundaries to Kalpi and Kherla, constantly warring with Gujarat and Delhi.

The Ghori line was replaced in 1436 A.D. by the Khilji dynasty under **Sultan Mahmud Shah I (1436–1469 A.D.)**, a soldier-sultan under whom the Malwa Sultanate reached its maximum territorial extent. He fought the rulers of Gujarat, the Deccan, and Delhi, and raised a seven-storeyed tower of victory at Mandu to commemorate his successes over the Rana of Mewar. His successor, Ghiyas-ud-din (1469–1500 A.D.), enjoyed a peaceful 31-year reign focused on cultural pursuits and the arts, building the Jahaz Mahal for his vast harem. The state fractured under Mahmud II, who relied on the Rajput chief Medini Rai; Bahadur Shah of Gujarat annexed Malwa in 1526 A.D., and Humayun temporarily overran it in 1534 A.D. Malwa enjoyed a brief independent revival under Shujaat Khan and his son **Baz Bahadur (1554–1561 A.D.)**, celebrated for his musical compositions and his romantic association with Queen Rupmati, before Akbar's general Adham Khan annexed the territory into the Mughal Empire in 1561 A.D.

Concurrently, northern Madhya Bharat centered around Gwalior under the Tomar Rajputs. Their most illustrious ruler, **Raja Man Singh Tomar (1479–1517 A.D.)**, turned Gwalior into a prosperous kingdom, constructing great irrigation works, patronizing the Gwalior school of classical music, and building the magnificent Man Mandir Palace within Gwalior Fort. Gwalior fell to Ibrahim Lodi in 1517 and was incorporated into the Mughal Empire by Babur in 1528 A.D.

#### The Maratha Ascendancy
Mughal authority collapsed in the early 18th century under the weight of internal revolts (such as Chhatrasal Bundela carving out principalities in the east by 1731) and the expansion of the Maratha Confederacy. Maratha light cavalry crossed the Narmada under Peshwa Bajirao I. In 1728, the Mughal governor Girdhar Bahadur was routed and killed near Mandu by Chimnaji Appa. By 1733, Malharrao Holkar and Ranoji Scindia forced Raja Jaisingh of Amber (the Mughal governor) to sue for peace and yield *chauth* (tribute). In 1736, Bajirao I led a rapid sweep directly to the gates of Delhi, intercepting and defeating the Nizam at Bhopal.

The Marathas secured complete sovereignty over the territories between the Chambal and Narmada, and the great houses of Scindia (at Gwalior), Holkar (at Indore), and Pawar (at Dhar and Dewas) carved out their historic principalities. Despite the Maratha reverse at Panipat in 1761, **Mahadji Scindia (1761–1794 A.D.)** arose as the towering military personality of northern India, controlling the Mughal Emperor at Delhi and creating a powerful modernized army. Following the Third Anglo-Maratha War in 1818, the Maratha states concluded treaties with the British, ruling as premier princely states until Indian independence in 1947.

### CHAPTER V: Arts and Architecture

#### (A) Architecture

##### Sacred Architecture (Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain)
The earliest sacred structural remains are eleven brick Stupas excavated at Kasrawad in the Nimar district, datable to the 1st–2nd centuries B.C. They are simple hemispherical drums measuring up to 35 feet in diameter with paved *pradakshinapathas* (circumambulation pathways). Rock-cut architecture emerged in the 4th–5th centuries A.D. at Bagh (Buddhist) and Udaygiri (Brahmanical and Jain). The Bagh caves feature massive pillared monastic halls (*viharas*) with residential cells and rear chapels, displaying richly carved doorframes. The Udaygiri caves consist of small rock-cut cells fronted by structural porticoes, their stone walls covered in classical relief sculptures.

The earliest structural Hindu temples date to the Gupta period (4th–6th centuries A.D.), characterized by an unassuming, simple plan: a small square sanctum (*garbhagriha*) with a flat roof and a small pillared porch in front, lacking any spire (*shikhara*). Examples include False Cave No. 1 at Udaygiri and stone fragments from Pawaya, Mandsor, and Besnagar. In the 7th–9th centuries A.D., sacred architecture evolved: flat roofs were replaced by ornate, tapering *shikharas*, and exterior wall spaces were filled with relief carvings. Masterpieces from this phase include the spectacular **Teli-ka-Mandir** on Gwalior Fort, renowned for its lofty, barrel-shaped South Indian (*Dravidian*) style *shikhara*, alongside the Chaturbhuja temple (875 A.D.) and early shrines at Amrol, Indhar, Badoh, and Gyaraspur.

The medieval era (10th–12th centuries A.D.) represents the golden zenith of temple architecture in Northern India, guided by formal canonical texts on *Vastushastra*. The plan became highly complex, comprising:
1. The **Garbhagriha** (inner sanctum) enshrining the deity under an intricately carved ceiling, surmounted by a towering *shikhara* adorned with medallions and vertical ribs.
2. The **Sabhamandapa** (assembly hall) providing a communal locus, its columns, architraves, and ceilings completely covered in narrative carvings from the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata*, figures of dancing girls, and decorative motifs.
3. The **Mukhamandapa** (entrance porch) fronted by spectacular, freestanding **Torana** gateways.

Stellar examples include the magnificent **Udayeshwar Temple** at Udaypur (built by Udayaditya Paramara between 1059 and 1080 A.D.), the **Siddheshwar Temple** at Nemawar on the Narmada (boasting a tall, perfectly preserved *shikhara*), the larger Sas Bahu temple at Gwalior (1093 A.D.), the Hindola Torana at Gyaraspur, the Gadarmal temple at Badoh, and the structural groups at Un.

##### Sacred Architecture (Muslim)
Islamic architecture in Madhya Bharat developed from the 14th century onward, marked by majestic simplicity, huge proportions, and an absence of figurative sculpture. The first phase involved converting older Hindu and Jain temples into mosques by reusing their carved pillars and architectural blocks, visible in the mosques at Dhar, Mandu, Bhonrasa, and Bhilsa (such as the Bijamandal Mosque built over the 11th-century Vijaya Mandir). The classical phase flourished at Mandu in the 15th century, producing independent architectural masterpieces like the grand Jami Masjid, Hoshang Shah's marble tomb, and the tomb of Mahmud Khilji, which combine solid Pathan masonry with subtle elements of local Hindu ornamentation. Mughal-era structures include the expansive, screen-filled tomb of Muhammad Ghaus at Gwalior and the Alamgiri Mosque.

##### Civil and Secular Architecture
While ancient residential architecture in wood has perished, medieval secular architecture survives in a few stone monasteries (*mathas*) at Kadwaha, Ranod, Kundalpur, and Surwaya (10th–12th centuries A.D.), built of massive stone blocks with heavy slab roofing. Purely Hindu palace architecture is epitomized by Gwalior's **Man Mandir Palace** and **Gujari Mahal**, built by Raja Man Singh Tomar in the 15th century. The Man Mandir features a striking 300-foot-long east facade Relieved by six rounded towers crowned with domed cupolas and decorated with brilliant turquoise, green, and yellow glazed tile mosaics of animals, ducks, and banana trees. Its internal open courts, bracketed pillars, and stone screen-work strongly influenced Akbar's subsequent imperial builders at Agra and Fatehpur Sikri.

Musalman civil architecture is represented by the grand ruins of Mandu, Dhar, Chanderi, and Nalcha. These palaces (e.g., Jahaz Mahal, Hindola Mahal, Baz Bahadur's Palace) feature imposing flights of steps, pointed arches, vaulted ceilings, and large domes, decorated with plaster moldings and colored glazed tiles. Later civil architecture includes the fortified residences (*gadhis*) of regional Rajput chiefs at Ater, Narwar, Gohad, Sabalgarh, and Narsingarh, which adapted Mughal trefoil arches, fluted domes, and projecting balconies.

#### (B) Arts of Sculpture and Painting

##### Sculpture
Positive evidence of plastic art begins in the 3rd century B.C. under the Mauryas. The ancient site of Besnagar has yielded monumental, free-standing colossal stone statues of Yakshinis and a giant figure of Kubera (recovered from the Betwa river), characterized by realistic stiffness, massive volume, and indigenous dress. Narrative relief sculpture flourished during the Shunga period on the gateways and railings of Buddhist stupas (remains from Besnagar are preserved in the Gwalior Museum).

Gupta sculpture (4th–6th centuries A.D.) achieved supreme aesthetic refinement, mastering symmetry and perspective. It conventionalized classical decorative motifs like "the vase and foliage" (*ghata-pallava*), *kirtimukha* (face of fame), and *chaitya*-window traceries. Exquisite relief panels include the "dance and music" scene on the Pawaya lintel and the side panels of the Udaygiri Varaha cave. Medieval sculpture (8th–12th centuries A.D.) became intrinsically tied to temple architecture. Guided by rigid iconographic texts, sculptors covered every inch of temple walls in a bewildering variety of stylized animal forms (lions, crocodiles, snakes) and multi-armed divinities, best observed in the collections of the Gwalior Museum and *in situ* at Suhania, Padhavli, Gyaraspur, and Udaypur.

##### Painting
Madhya Bharat claims a central locus in the history of Indian wall painting through the nine rock-cut **Bagh Caves** in the Nimar district, which preserve matured Mahayana Buddhist tempera murals contemporary with Ajanta. Done on a mud-and-lime plaster coat (*rinfazzo*), the paintings in Cave No. 4 (**Rang Mahal**) represent exceptional decorative beauty, poetry of motion, and subtle line-work. The murals depict scenes of deep sorrow, female musicians playing lutes and guitars, colorful dancing parties arranged around male dancers, and majestic festive pageants of nobles on horses and stately elephants.

Later pictorial traditions survive in the 15th-century glazed tile mosaics on the facade of Gwalior's Man Mandir palace, depicting bands of crocodiles with intertwined tails, rows of ducks, tigers, elephants, and banana trees. These panels drew high praise from Babur for their brilliant coloration. Mandu's Islamic structures feature painted geometric tiles and floral motifs in yellow and indigo blue. 17th-century Rajput mural fragments survive at Narwar and Sheopur, while late 19th-century courtly painting is preserved in the Moti Mahal palace at Gwalior (1872 A.D.).

#### (C) Music
Gwalior played a dominant role in the history and evolution of the Hindustani classical *raga* system, specializing in the development of:
1. *Dhrupad* and *Dhamar* (sung under rigid, orthodox rules)
2. *Khayal* and *Tappas* (highly imaginative variations)
3. *Thumris*, *Dadras*, and *Ledas* (popular regional styles)
4. *Ghazals* and *Taranas* (vocal and instrumental modes)

The **Gwalior School** traces its roots back to the 15th century to the era of Gopal Nayak and Baiju Bawra. **Raja Man Singh Tomar (1486–1517 A.D.)** was a proficient composer and a major patron who systematically developed the *Dhrupad* style. One of his queens, Mriganayana (a Gujari by caste), was herself a gifted musician; four mixed melodic modes (*sankirna ragas*) were invented by Man Singh and named in her honor: *Gujari*, *Bahul Gujari*, *Mal Gujari*, and *Mangal Gujari*. Man Singh authored the landmark Hindi musical treatise **Manakutuhal**, which compiled the proceedings of a great musical conference held at Gwalior (later translated into Persian as *Faqir-ullah*). Master musicians at his court included Naik, Bakshu, and Bhanu, who created new melodies like *Bakshu-ki-Malhar*, *Dhondia-ki-Malhar*, and *Bahaduri Todi*.

The Gwalior school heavily influenced the Mughal court; Akbar's *Ain-i-Akbari* lists 36 premier court singers, of whom 16 hailed directly from Gwalior. Foremost among them was **Mian Tansen**, born at Behat near Gwalior, who studied under Ram Das Swami before joining Akbar's court in 1562 A.D. Tansen is celebrated as a vocal genius who introduced foundational ragas like *Miyan-ki-Malhar* (introducing the E-flat or *Komala Gandhara*), *Miyan-ki-Todi*, and *Darbari Kanada*. His simple tomb stands near the tomb of Muhammad Ghaus in Gwalior. Following the decline of the Mughals, Delhi singers dispersed to the Scindia court, making Gwalior a premier modern seat of classical music, culminating in the establishment of the **Madhav Music College** in 1916. In southern Malwa, Sultan Baz Bahadur of Mandu and his consort Rupmati actively patronized music, specializing in *Raga Bhupali* and regional folk songs.

#### (D) Industrial Arts and Crafts
* **Art of Stone Carving:** Practiced traditionally by the *Pattharphod* class in northern Madhya Bharat. Reorienting their ancient image-carving skills after the 12th-century Islamic conquest, they mastered the art of carving delicate stone screens (*jalis*) in intricate geometric and floral patterns, visible on Gwalior Fort, the tomb of Muhammad Ghaus, and at Mandu. This tradition survives in the trellis work on modern buildings in Lashkar and Gwalior.
* **Ceramics:** Dating back to the 15th-century glazed tiles of the Man Mandir. The **Gwalior Potteries** continue this legacy by utilizing local clay deposits to manufacture high-class ceramic ware and commercializing structural reproductions of 10th-century archeological sculptures (e.g., the "Lady of the Lotus") and Bagh Cave frescoes.
* **Textiles:** Celebrated across two ancient regional cottage centers:
  * **Chanderi:** Renowned from the 10th century for weaving fine, gossamer muslins, aided by the natural humidity of its surrounding hills and a local sizing root called *Koli Kanda*. The weavers traditionally grew long, slit nails to judge thread fineness, producing delicate nature-inspired gold and silk borders.
  * **Maheshwar:** Located on the Narmada in southern Malwa, specializing in weaving unique borders suited to Maharashtrian tastes, initiated under Queen Ahilyabai Holkar.
  * *Cloth Printing:* Delightful regional designs in manual block cloth printing flourish at Ujjain and Jawad.
* **Lacquer-ware:** Traditional centers exist at Sabalgarh, Sheopur, Ratlam, Gujri, and Khachrod (the latter specialized in lacquered bedstead legs). Shivpuri produces unique decorative bamboo baskets covered with cow-dung paste and lacquer coatings.
* **Metal Work:** Utility articles and weaponry (spear-heads, arrow-heads) were forged from local indigenous iron at Karera and Bajranggarh.

### CHAPTER VI: The Archaeological Monuments of Madhya Bharat
The official registry of the State Archaeology Department documents **1,783 monuments** spread across 467 places. Of these, 145 premier structures are declared as monuments of National Importance under the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act—the highest count among all Part "B" States.

The geographical distribution of monuments across the 16 districts of the state is detailed below:
* **Shivapuri District:** 381 monuments across 89 places (e.g., Mahua, Ranod, Surwaya) — the highest count due to the ready availability of building sandstone from local hills.
* **Guna District:** 277 monuments across 58 places (e.g., Budhi Chanderi, Chanderi, Kadwaha, Terahi).
* **Mandsaur District:** 206 monuments across 70 places (e.g., Dhamnar, Khor, Sondni).
* **Dhar District:** 204 monuments across 42 places (e.g., Bagh, Dhar, Mandu, Sadalpur).
* **Gwalior District:** 165 monuments across 38 places (e.g., Amrol, Gwalior, Pawaya).
* **Bhilsa District:** 142 monuments across 26 places (e.g., Badoh, Besnagar, Bhilsa, Gyaraspur, Pathari, Udaypur, Udaygiri) — the highest density of premier monuments in the state, centered around ancient Vidisha.
* **Ujjain District:** 125 monuments across 39 places.
* **Morena District:** 116 monuments across 36 places (e.g., Mitaoli, Naresar, Suhania, Padhavli).
* **Bhind District:** 42 monuments across 18 places (e.g., Ater, Kherat).
* **Shajapur District:** 33 monuments across 6 places.
* **Nimar District:** 31 monuments across 14 places (e.g., Un).
* **Dewas District:** 27 monuments across 13 places (e.g., Nemawar).
* **Indore District:** 27 monuments across 15 places.
* **Rajgadh District:** 7 monuments across 3 places.
* *Note: Jhabua and Ratlam districts have no official recorded entries.*

#### Chronological Sequence of Monuments
1. **Earliest Times to 711 A.D. (37 Monuments):** Centers include Gwalior, Pawaya, Tumain, Besnagar, Udaygiri, Pathari, Ujjain, Mandsaur, Bagh, and Kasrawad.
2. **711 to 1206 A.D. (243 Monuments):** The early medieval zenith of Hindu and Jain temple building across Gwalior, Suhania, Padhavli, Mitaoli, Naresar, Narwar, Surwaya, Kadwaha, Mahua, Terahi, Ranod, Chanderi, Badoh, Pathari, Bhilsa, Udaypur, Gyaraspur, Khor, Dhamnar, Un, and Nemawar.
3. **1206 to 1526 A.D. (269 Monuments):** Marks the impact of Islamic architecture, concentrated across the sultanate cities of Mandu, Dhar, Nalcha, Sadalpur, Sarangpur, Chanderi, Bhilsa, and Bhonrasa, alongside late defensive Rajput constructions inside Gwalior Fort and Narwar.
4. **1526 A.D. and Later (437 Monuments):** Dominated by civil palaces, forts, and *gadhis* built by regional chiefs at Gwalior, Gohad, Ater, Sheopur, Narwar, Karera, Chanderi, Bajranggarh, Raghogarh, Shajapur, and Mandu.
* *Note: Approximately 800 recorded monuments remain undated.*

#### Structural Typography

##### Religious Edifices
The state contains nearly 240 historic Shaiva temples, including classical monuments like the Udayeshwar temple at Udaypur, the Siddheshwar temple at Nemawar, and the monolithic shrines at Kadwaha, Un, Suhania, and Surwaya. Monasteries of the **Mattamayura Shaiva order** ("the mad peacocks") stand at Kadwaha, Terahi, and Ranod. Vaishnavism is represented by 87 shrines, including the rock-cut Chaturbhuja temple (875 A.D.) on Gwalior Fort, the Pathari column (861 A.D.), and the Varaha relief at Udaygiri. Jainism claims 89 recorded monuments, spanning the rock-cut cells at Udaygiri, medieval complexes at Badoh, Gyaraspur, Budhi Chanderi, and Dubkund, and the 15th-century colossal cliff figures at Gwalior Fort and Barai. Islam is represented by 113 historic mosques and tombs, primarily at Mandu, Dhar, Chanderi, Sarangpur, and Gwalior.

##### Forts, Gadhis, and Civil Palaces
The archeological registry lists **157 defensive Forts or Gadhis**, occupying strategic eminences and hills. They are characterized by thick battlemented masonry or mud walls, massive circular bastions, deep moats fed by natural reservoirs, multi-gated zigzag approaches, and a core fortified stronghold known as the **Bale Killa**. The highest gate is traditionally named the *Hawa Paur* (wind gate), while others faced important towns. Inside these fortresses stand nearly 100 civil palaces, ranging from pure Hindu designs (Man Mandir and Gujari Mahal at Gwalior) and Pathan sultanate styles (Jahaz Mahal and Hindola Mahal at Mandu) to late regional Rajput mansions modeled after Mughal residences (at Ater, Narwar, Sabalgarh, Sheopur, Gohad, and Raghogarh).

##### Funerary Monuments
Includes **145 Sati monuments**, consisting of vertical flat stone slabs under 6 feet high, divided into relief panels. They depict the husband and wife standing hand-in-hand or worshipping a *linga*, backed by relief carvings of the Sun, Moon, and stars to symbolize eternal fame. The oldest is the later-Gupta slab from Hasalpur (Shivapuri); others at Terahi record deaths in battle against the *Karnatas* in the 7th century A.D. There are **59 Memorial Pillars** (*Kirti-stambhas*) depicting a warrior's death, his ascent to heaven escorted by celestial nymphs, and his final crowned deification bust holding a rosary and citron. Other panels depict rows of cows, indicating death during a cattle-lifting raid. **56 Rajput Cenotaphs** (*Chhattris*), dating from the 17th century, stand as open domed pavilions on columns over the cremation sites of royalty (e.g., the Scindia and Holkar cenotaphs at Gwalior and Maheshwar). There are **110 Muslim Tombs** (*maqbaras*), primarily at Mandu, Chanderi, and Gwalior, and a dozen historic Christian tombs.

##### Public Utility Works (Wells, Tanks, and Sarais)
The state records **211 old tanks, ghats, and wells**. Medieval Islamic architecture produced massive, elaborately planned stepped wells (*baodis*) containing internal living apartments and structural flights of stone steps down to the water, visible at Mandu, Chanderi, Baodipura (Morena), Bhonrasa and Burro (Bhilsa), and Dhakoni (Guna). Great masonry tank dams composed of large stone boulders without mortar were constructed at Depalpur (built by King Devapala Paramara in 1216 A.D.), Badoh, Gyaraspur, and Narwar. Extensive bathing ghats line the Kshipra at Ujjain and the Narmada at Maheshwar.

The trade and military routes connecting Delhi and the Gangetic plains to the Deccan tableland are marked by a line of fortified, bastioned resting compounds (**Sarais**) built at convenient 10-to-15-mile intervals by Sher Shah Suri and the Mughal emperors. This network runs through Nurabad (12 miles north of Gwalior on the Asan river), Sagar Tal, Gwalior town, the Sandalpur ghati pass, Barqui, Magroni, Narwar, Budha Dongar, Sesai, Kolaras, Chanderi, Nai Sarai, Kachnar, Sironj, Bhilsa, Pauvanala, and Nemawar. The Mughals constructed large stone causeway bridges across the Asan at Nurabad and the Sindh at Magroni and Narwar.

## PART II: BRIEF DIRECTORY OF PREMIER ARCHEOLOGICAL SITES

### (A) THE NORTHERN MADHYA BHARAT

#### 1. Gwalior
* **Chaturbhuja Temple:** A small Vishnu shrine entirely hewn out of the living rock halfway up the eastern fort approach. It features a square cell surmounted by a *shikhara* in the early medieval style. A foundational inscription records its excavation in **875 A.D.** by Alla, a local governor under King Ramadeva of the Imperial Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty of Kanauj.
* **Sas Bahu Temples:** Two structural Vishnu temples standing near the eastern fort rampart. The larger temple was completed in **1093 A.D.** by the Kacchavaha Rajput Prince Mahipala, as recorded on its entrance porch inscription. Raised on a high platform, its plan comprises a *garbhagriha* (the spire and outer facing of which have disappeared) and a massive three-story *sabhamandapa* with three projecting porches. Both exterior and interior are profusely carved with traditional floral and figurative reliefs. The inner door lintel displays projecting panels of Shiva, Vishnu, and Brahma, flanked by the nine personified planets (*Navagrahas*). The jambs are carved with the river goddesses Ganga and Yamuna. The central hall ceiling features a intricate *kirtimukha* layout. The smaller Sas Bahu temple belongs to the same style.
* **Teli-ka-Mandir:** The tallest structure within Gwalior Fort, rising over 100 feet. Originally dedicated to Vishnu, its architectural style places it between the 8th and 10th centuries A.D. Its outstanding feature is its lofty, barrel-shaped *shikhara*, which is uniquely Dravidian (South Indian) in style, while all its decorative relief details are purely Indo-Aryan (North Indian). Its ground plan is oblong, consisting of a large shrine room fronted by a simple, vigorous porch.
* **Jain Colossi:** Gigantic nude figures of the 24 Tirthankaras carved into the sheer cliff faces of the fort hill, concentrated heavily near the Urwahi Gate. They are remarkable for their colossal scale rather than fine modeling; one standing figure rises 57 feet high. Dated inscriptions place their execution in the **15th century A.D.** under the Tomar Rajput princes.
* **Man Mandir Palace:** Built by Raja Man Singh Tomar (1479–1517 A.D.), it is the premier surviving example of an un-perished palace built in purely Hindu structural style. Perched on a 300-foot cliff, its east facade is 300 feet long and 80 feet high, relieved by six round towers crowned with domed cupolas (originally copper-gilt when viewed by Babur in 1528 A.D.). The exterior walls are decorated with brilliant, indestructible glazed tile mosaics in blue, green, and yellow, depicting rows of elephants, ducks, tigers, panthers, and uniquely Indian banana trees. The interior comprises two open courts surrounded by two storeys of living apartments adorned with balconies and intricate stone screens. Its style heavily influenced Akbar's imperial buildings at Agra and Fatehpur Sikri.
* **Gujari Mahal:** A massive, two-storeyed palace built by Raja Man Singh for his favourite queen, Mriganayana. It features a spacious internal court and now houses the state **Archaeological Museum**, containing a world-class collection of sculptures and inscriptions from regional ancient sites.
* **Other Monuments:** The Karan Mandir and Vikram Mandir palaces; the early Mughal tomb of the divine Muhammad Ghaus, crowned with a large dome and enclosed by fine pierced stone geometric *jalis*; the adjacent flat-roofed tomb of the musical genius Tansen; the Alamgiri Mosque (built under Aurangzeb); and the modern Samadhi of Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi.

#### 2. Naresar
A wooded valley situated 10 miles north-east of Gwalior, containing a cluster of nearly 20 early medieval temples built on different hill levels without a unified plan. They consist of a small shrine and an ante-chamber, lacking an entrance porch. One group lines a rock-cut tank and features low pyramidal roofs, a rare architectural feature in North India. Another individual temple features a barrel-shaped Dravidian *shikhara* over an oblong sanctum, closely resembling the Teli-ka-Mandir. Inscribed pedestals place their construction in the **11th–12th centuries A.D.**, dedicated to Shaiva divinities, from which a series of mother goddesses with unique regional names (*Viara*, *Bhayayavati*, *Maghali*) have been recovered and deposited in the Gwalior Museum.

#### 3. Pawaya
The site of the ancient city of **Padmavati**, capital of the Naga kings (3rd–4th centuries A.D.), situated 40 miles south of Gwalior at the confluence of the Sindh and Parvati rivers. Its physical layout is described in Bhavabhuti's *Malati-Madhava*. Excavations of its large mounds revealed an extraordinary terraced brick temple of the Gupta period, consisting of three platforms receding sequentially. The middle platform walls feature recessed brick panels flanked by pilasters with vase-shaped pedestals and a upper line of miniature brick *chaitya* arches, once covered in lime plaster. The top platform supported a perishable timber-and-brick sanctum, and the lower platforms served as a brick *pradakshinapatha*. The site has yielded classical Gupta terracottas (human heads and busts showing varied facial expressions and hairstyles, comparable to Rajghat), a life-size free-standing statue of Yaksha Manibhadra (1st century A.D.), a stone palm capital, a two-faced sun-capital, and a large *torana* door lintel carved with a complex relief of a dancing performance and Bali's sacrifice. Nearby stands the 17th-century temple of Dhumeshvara Mahadeva, built by Raja Birsingh Deo of Orchha over a roaring waterfall on the Sindh river.

#### 4. Ater
A massive, irregular fortress situated in the deep, undulating ravines of the Chambal river, built of mud, brick, and lime. Five internal inscriptions record that its construction was initiated by the Bhadoria Rajput chief Badan Singh in **1644 A.D.** and completed by Maha Singh in **1068 A.D.**, who designated the stronghold as *Devagiri*. Alarmed by its structural strength, Aurangzeb demanded assurances from the chief. The fort walls are high and bastioned, fronted by an outer mud defense work and accessed through three gates. The inner court is enclosed by the large pillared halls of the *Kacheri* (Private Audience Hall) and *Diwan-i-Am* (Public Audience Hall). In the center of the western apartments rises a lofty **seven-storeyed watchtower** providing a panoramic military view of the ravines. The upper residential storeys (*Raja's Mahal* and *Rani's Mahal*) are decorated with stone screens and fine floral plaster moldings.

#### 5. Suhania
Anciently called *Simha-paniya*, its historical importance in the 11th century is attested by the Sas Bahu temple inscription at Gwalior. It contains the spectacular **Kakanmadh Temple**, a colossal structural shrine built by the Kacchavaha King Kirtiraja around **1000 A.D.**, traditionally attributed to Queen Kakanvati. Raised on a spacious stone platform, the temple comprises a square sanctum housing a worshipped *linga*, a large pillared assembly hall (*sabhamandapa*), and a projecting entrance porch reached by flights of steps. The skeleton framework of its central *shikhara* rises nearly 100 feet high; its outer decorative and sculptured stone facings have collapsed and are arranged on the ground. The hall is supported by tall, massive, majestic columns covered in deep figurative reliefs, surmounted by a shattered two-storeyed pyramidal spire. The site preserves a medieval temple of the goddess Ambika, a monolithic stone pillar, and a loose colossus of Hanuman.

#### 6. Padhavli-Mitaoli
* **Padhavli:** Contains a medieval fortified compound (*Gadhi*) built by the Jat Ranas of Gohad in the 18th century over a high, earlier temple platform. The original 10th-century temple inside has lost its *shikhara* and doorframe, but the interior architraves and friezes of its *sabhamandapa* are intact, covered in some of the densest figurative relief panels in northern India. These panels depict scenes from the *Ramayana* and *Bhagavata Purana*, including Krishna's life, the Hindu Trinity, Surya, and an extensive northern frieze of the goddess Kali. Nearby lies the *Chhau Kua*, a large roofed stepwell fronted by an 8th-century shrine. The adjacent **Bateshvara Valley** is a spectacular archeological site containing an extensive, confused assemblage of hundreds of minor, small structural stone Shiva shrines clustering around the large standing temple of Bhuteshwar Mahadeva.
* **Mitaoli:** Features a unique circular temple structure on an isolated hillock, popularly called the *Ekottarso Mahadeva Mandir* (Temple of 101 Lingas). Its ground plan is highly rare: it consists of a central circular structural shrine on a raised circular plinth, placed at the center of an open circular courtyard. This court is enclosed by a continuous range of small structural cells (numbering just under a hundred) fronted by a circular pillared verandah, each cell enshrining a worshipped stone *linga* of Shiva.

#### 7. Narwar
The ancient *Nalapura*, traditional capital of King Nala of the *Mahabharata*. The fort is situated on a flat hilltop rising 500 feet above the plain, its circuit walls running 5 miles along the ridge. It was held by the Kacchawahas, Tomars, and Jajapellas. The eastern approach features a series of historic gates, culminating in the *Hawapaur*, built under Daulat Rao Scindia. The internal area contains four fortified enclosures: *Maj-loka* (the central core), *Madar Ahata*, *Gujar Ahata*, and *Dhola Ahata*. The *Maj-loka* contains the large stone palaces of the Rajput chiefs built over the last 400 years, including the *Kacheri Mahal* (perched over the Sindh valley), *Ladau Bungalow*, and *Chhip Mahal* (containing a large monolithic stone cistern). Water was supplied by the *Makaradhvaja Tal* tank, supplemented by a network of wells (*Atha Kua* and *Nau Baodi*) sunk into its bed. The fort contains Sikandar Lodi's mosque, the tomb of Madar Shah, a Roman Catholic chapel and cemetery of 17th-century European gunners, and massive old inscribed cannons (e.g., *Shatru Samhar*, *Ram Ban*). In the town below stands the *Jait Khamba* pillar, bearing a Tomar genealogical inscription, and a unique underground cellar concealing about a hundred un-mutilated medieval Jain images.

#### 8. Surwaya
Anciently called *Sarasvatipattana*, it features a small, fortified stone compound (*Gadhi*) enclosing a well-preserved medieval structural monastery (*matha*) and a group of three temples built on a common paved plinth. The monastery was built of massive blocks of dry stone masonry with heavy stone slabs for roofing, surrounding a small open court with corridors and residential rooms. The spires of the three structural temples have collapsed, but the eastern temple is exceptionally well preserved. It consists of a square sanctum and an attached porch, its entrance doorway covered in rich figurative carvings. The flat ceiling of its porch is one of the masterpieces of medieval Indian stone chiseling, containing deep concentric floral and geometric patterns. Lintel reliefs indicate it was originally dedicated to Vishnu.

#### 9. Kadwaha
Anciently called *Kadambaguha*, it was a major hub of the **Mattamayura Shaiva order** ("the mad peacocks"). It contains a large 10th-century stone monastery built of huge blocks of sandstone with large roofing slabs, later enclosed by a 15th-century fortified *gadhi*. A contemporary Sanskrit inscription recovered from the site provides a genealogy of the Shaiva ascetics of the order. The monastery was attached to a major temple of Shiva, now partially buried. Scattered around the village within a one-mile radius are over a dozen medieval structural temples (*Murayatas*) built near an old silted tank. One is completely intact, consisting of a square sanctum fronted by a highly ornate porch with a well-carved pyramidal roof, built on a raised platform. Another major structural ruin is known as the *Chaudai Madhi*.

#### 10. Ranod
Anciently called *Ranipadra*, it contains the **Khokhai Math**, a massive structural stone monastery built for Shaiva ascetics. A large Sanskrit inscription on its wall records that it was built by Purandara, the preceptor of King Avantivarman, and later extended in the **10th century A.D.** by the master Vyomashiva, who also built twin shrines in front. The structure is two-storeyed, built of large blocks of dry sandstone without mortar. Nearby stands a monolithic serpent image of *Naga Deva*, depicting two cobras coiled around each other, on the bank of the Airavati river, and the *Jhiri-ki-Masjid* mosque, featuring an exquisite graveyard screen of stone *jali* work.

#### 11. Terahi
Anciently called *Terambhi*, it sits on the Mahuar (*Madhumati*) river. Its premier monument is a freestanding, perfectly preserved **Torana Gateway** belonging to an 11th-century ruined temple of the goddess Mohajamata. Heavy at the top, the archway is a superb specimen of decorative architecture, its upright pillars and crossbeams covered in miniature carvings. In its compound stands an inscribed memorial pillar of a warrior killed in a local battle in **903 A.D.** (*V.S. 960*), alongside two 7th-century memorial stones. The village fort encloses a stone monastery and an 11th-century Shaiva temple, while the surrounding jungle conceals an eight-faced *Shivalinga* and a large image of Ganesha.

#### 12. Mahua
A small hamlet containing the ruins of three early structural temples. The small Mahadeva temple consists of a square sanctum and a porch, lacking its spire. Its exterior faces are adorned with vigorous, early arabesque relief carvings and figurative sculptures. A Sanskrit inscription on the porch lintel places its execution in the **7th century A.D.** on paleographical grounds. It is flanked by a larger, contemporary Mahadeva temple and a ruined shrine housing a life-size stone image of the goddess Kali.

#### 13. Chanderi
Anciently called *Chandrapuri*, it is surrounded by hills and lakes. Its oldest remains are at *Budhi Chanderi* (Old Chanderi), a deserted site in a dense forest 9 miles north, containing Hindu and Jain ruins from the 10th–12th centuries A.D. The present town features a prominent fort hill (*Kirtidurga*) built by the Pratihara King Kirtipala. It became a major *Shikka* (provincial capital) under the Malwa Sultans in 1401 A.D.
* **The Fort:** Enclosed by a massive stone wall 1½ miles long, featuring the *Khuni Darwaza* (Bloody Gate) and the upper *Hawa Paur*. It houses a ruined *rest house* and a small pavilion marking the spot where the Rajput ladies committed *Jauhar* (self-immolation) during Babur's siege in 1527 A.D.
* **The Town and Outskirts:** The town is enclosed by a wall pierced by the monumental **Delhi Gate (1411 A.D.)**. Internal structures include the family tombs of Nizamuddin, enclosed by fine stone screens, the spectacular **Badal Mahal Gateway** (a 50-foot-high structural entrance flanked by tapering circular bastions), and the large Jama Masjid. Outer monuments include the *Shahzadi-ka-Roza* tomb (featuring a square chamber under a dome with wavy stone brackets and blue inlaid enamel geometric tiles), the *Madrasa*, and the *Battisi Baodi* stepped well (containing 32 structural landings). To the south stands the **Kati Ghati Gateway**, a monumental arched pass cut directly through the solid rock of the hill by order of Juman Khan in the late 15th century. To the north is the **Koshak Mahal**, a large, imposing four-storeyed palace square in plan, built in 1445 A.D. by Sultan Mahmud Khilji I.

### (B) THE NORTH-EASTERN MALWA PLATEAU: BHILSA DISTRICT

#### 1. Bhilsa
The modern successor of ancient Vidisha, named in the 11th century after the great sun-temple of *Bhellasvamin* noted by Al-Biruni. Its premier monument is the **Bijamandal Mosque**, a massive Islamic structure built over the foundations of the 11th-century **Vijaya Mandir** (a temple to the goddess Charchika or Vijaya). The temple was demolished under Delhi Sullanate rule, and the mosque was constructed on the site by reusing its original, highly carved structural pillars; a Sanskrit temple inscription survives on one column. The town features the **Lohangi Rock**, a lone sandstone peak housing the tomb of Lohangi Pir, an old covered masonry tank, and a ruined mosque built under Sultan Mahmud Khilji I (1457 A.D.).

#### 2. Besnagar
The physical site of ancient **Vidisha**, situated in the fork of the Betwa (*Vetravati*) and Bes rivers. It was a major trading capital from the time of the Buddha down to the Guptas. Its mounds have yielded premier free-standing colossal sculptures: three *Yakshinis*, a colossal structural figure of *Kubera*, and early Gupta images of Durga and Vishnu. Its central monument is the **Heliodoros Pillar**, a free-standing monolithic stone column known locally as *Khamb Baba*. The column bears a clear Brahmi inscription recording its erection as a *Garudadhvaja* in honor of the god Vasudeva by Heliodoros, a Greek citizen of Taxila who came as an ambassador to the court of the Shunga King Bhagabhadra, explicitly styling himself a *Bhagavata* (convert to Hinduism).

#### 3. Udaygiri
A long sandstone hill containing **20 rock-cut caves** excavated during the Gupta period.
* **Cave No. 1:** A Jain cave representing the earliest phase of structural Hindu-Jain temple evolution, consisting of a small rock-cut cell fronted by a structural porch on four columns.
* **Cave No. 4:** Enshrines a *Shivalinga* and is called *Bina Cave* due to a porch relief of a man playing the lute (*veena*).
* **Cave No. 5 (The Varaha Cave):** The premier artistic monument of the Gupta era, housing a colossal rock-cut relief of **Vishnu's Boar Incarnation**. The god is represented in animal-human form with a boar's head, his left foot treading upon the hooded head of the Naga king, while his right tusk raises the slender, personified figure of *Prithvi* (the Earth goddess) from the primeval ocean waves (carved as horizontal wavy lines). The background is filled with rows of celebrating Devas and celestial musicians. The flanking side walls depict the descent of the personified river goddesses Ganga and Yamuna from the heavens, holding water vessels, and pouring their streams into the sea-god Varuna.
* **Caves 6, 7, and 20:** Contain major historical dated inscriptions of the reigns of Chandragupta II and Kumaragupta I. Cave 18 houses a large relief of *Sheshashayi Vishnu*.

#### 4. Udaypur
A premier archeological town located 4 miles east of Bareth station.
* **The Udayeshvara (Nilkantheshvara) Temple:** The absolute masterpiece of the *Bhumija* style of temple architecture in India, built entirely of red sandstone. Contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions on its porches record that it was founded by the Paramara King **Udayaditya**; construction commenced in **1059 A.D.** (*V.S. 1116*) and the flagstaff was hoisted in **1080 A.D.** (*V.S. 1137*). The temple stands in a spacious square courtyard enclosed by a low compound wall with stone seating. The main temple comprises a square *garbhagriha*, a central *sabhamandapa*, and three entrance porches (*mukhamandapas*) facing north, south, and east. The sanctum enshrine a large *Shivalinga* covered in a historic brass face plate presented in 1775 A.D. by Khande Rao Appaji (a general of Mahadji Scindia). The exterior walls are covered in multi-figured relief carvings of the Hindu Pantheon (Brahma, Vishnu, Ganesha, Kartikeya, and Durga), with Shaiva forms predominating. The assembly hall features a low pyramidal roof, while the sanctum is surmounted by a spectacular, perfectly balanced **seven-storeyed Shikhara** decorated with vertical bands (*latas*) and delicately carved medallions. The pinnacles are climbed by a carved human figure representing either the architect or the royal builder. The compound originally housed eight minor attendant shrines; a mosque was built at the back of the main temple by Sultan Muhammad Tughlaq between **1336 and 1338 A.D.** (*A.H. 737–739*) using stone blocks from a dismantled attendant shrine, recorded on two Persian inscriptions.
* **Other Monuments:** The *Bijamandal* (a contemporary two-storeyed stone house of the temple time-keeper); the *Bara-khambi* (the 11th-century surviving pillared hall of a ruined temple); the *Shahi Masjid and Mahal* (a large Mughal mosque and governor's palace built under Jahangir and Shah Jahan in 1632 A.D., featuring fine stone *jalis*); and the *Sher Khan's Mosque (1488 A.D.)* outside the Moti Darwaza gate.

#### 5. Gyaraspur
A major medieval hub containing ruins from Buddhist, Brahmanical, and Jain heritages.
* **Maladevi Temple:** The largest monument at Gyaraspur, picturesquely cut into the slope of a hill on a massive platform held by a dry stone retaining wall. It is a large, fully developed *Bhumija* temple consisting of an entrance porch, a closed hall, and a sanctum enclosed by a *pradakshinapatha*, crowned with a high *shikhara* covered in deep carvings. Originally a temple to a goddess (marked by a female divinity on its outer lintel block), it was later appropriated by the Jains and houses images of Tirthankaras.
* **Athakhamba:** A group of eight magnificently carved columns constituting the sole surviving remnants of a grand 10th-century temple, preserving the doorframe of the sanctum and the columns of the hall carrying a delicate trefoil arch. A pilgrim's inscription is dated **982 A.D.**
* **Hindola Torana:** A spectacular freestanding ornamental gateway belonging to a lost Vishnu temple. It consists of two upright pillars and double crossbeams completely covered in relief panels depicting the ten incarnations (*Dashavataras*) of Vishnu. Nearby stand the four central pillars of the *sabhamandapa* featuring lion and elephant capitals.
* **Bajra Matha:** A rare structural temple consisting of three sanctums built in a single row, now housing Jain Tirthankara images but originally built as a Brahmanical shrine to the Hindu Trinity, with the central shrine dedicated to Surya.
* **Other Shrines:** Ruined dry rubble masonry platforms of late **Buddhist Stupas** on a southern hill, yielding a worn seated figure of the Buddha; and the *Mansarovar Tank* built by the Gonds.

#### 6. Badoh-Pathari
Two adjacent historical villages containing a vast cluster of ruins around a common tank, anciently named *Vatanagara*.
* **Gadarmal Temple:** A massive structural temple visible from miles away due to its height. It is composite, consisting of a lower 9th-century basement and porch of the original temple, surmounted by a later *shikhara* built of heterogeneous stone pieces collected from nearby ruins. The plan is oblong, consisting of a wide sanctum and a porch without a hall, resembling Gwalior's Teli-ka-Mandir. A row of pedestals runs inside the sanctum, showing it originally enshrined a group of female *Yogini* divinities, popularly attributed to a shepherd (*Gadaria*). The platform wall is decorated with molding niches, originally accessed through a ruined *torana* gateway facing the tank.
* **Solah-Khambi:** A structural sixteen-pillared hall with a flat roof standing on the bank of the tank, built as a 9th-century pleasure resort.
* **Jain Temple:** A large compound consisting of 25 separate cells and shrines built at various times between the 9th and 11th centuries A.D. around an oblong courtyard with a central *vedi* platform, sheltering colossal images of the 24 Tirthankaras.
* **Pathari Monuments:** Includes a monolithic column standing *in situ* in the village, recording its erection as a *Garudadhvaja* in **861 A.D.** (*V.S. 917*) by a minister of the Rashtrakuta King Parabala; a 5th-century rock-cut panel of the *Sapta Matrikas* on the hillside accompanied by an inscription of King Jayatsena; and a colossal unfinished rock-cut figure of the *Varaha* boar in a nearby boulder.

### (C) THE MALWA PLATEAU

#### 1. Ujjain
* **Kaliadeh Water Palace:** Built around **1500 A.D.** by Sultan Nasir Shah Khilji of Malwa on an island in the Kshipra river, anciently known as *Brahma Kund*. The palace is an excellent example of Mandu style secular architecture. Its outstanding feature is an ingenious hydro-engineering network by which the river water is directed through stone conduits into a series of internal tanks and allowed to discharge over sculptured stone curtains into cooling chambers built on a masonry platform. The bridge incorporates carved stone blocks from an earlier demolished Hindu temple.
* **Mahakal Temple:** One of the premier 12 *Jyotirlingas* in India, the ancient focus of the first Hindu meridian. Sacked and dismantled by Iltutmish in 1235 A.D., the present towering structure was raised in the 18th century by Ramachandra Baba, a Dewan of Ranoji Scindia. It attracts lakhs of pilgrims during the *Simhastha Mela* cycle.
* **Astronomical Observatory (Jantar Mantar):** Built in **1733 A.D.** by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh of Jaipur while serving as Mughal Governor of Malwa. It features four major masonry instruments (*Samrat Yantra*, *Nadi Valaya Yantra*, *Digamsha Yantra*, and *Bhitti Yantra*) built to map precise calculations across the prime meridian.
* **Other Sites:** The *Chaubis Khamba Gate* (reused columns of the compound gate of the medieval Mahakal temple); the *Bhartrihari Cave* (remnants of an 11th-century Shaiva temple held by the Nath sect); the *Gopal Mandir* (built in 1833 A.D. by Baija Bai Scindia, featuring silver doors); and the ancient city mounds of *Bhairongadh* (yielding 3rd-century B.C. structures).

#### 2. Dhar
* **Kamal Maula Mosque or Bhoja Shala:** A large Islamic compound constructed out of the remains of the **Bhoja Shala**, the premier Sanskrit college founded by the Paramara King Bhoja in the 11th century. The mosque consists of a large court enclosed by colonnades of reused carved columns and a rear prayer hall with fine temple ceilings. The stone pavement of the prayer hall contains numerous slabs of black slate whose surfaces were scraped; several retrieved slabs preserve long, beautifully engraved Sanskrit and Prakrit poetic texts (such as the *Parijata Manjari* drama and the *Kurmastotra*), confirming its original collegiate identity.
* **Lat Masjid:** Built by Dilawar Khan Ghori in **1405 A.D.**, utilizing carved columns and brackets from older temples. It derives its name from three large fragments of a historic iron pillar (*lat*) lying outside.
* **The Fort:** A massive stone fortress raised in 1344 A.D. by Sultan Muhammad Tughlaq during his campaigns in the Deccan, later serving as the birthplace of Peshwa Bajirao II (1775 A.D.).

#### 3. Mandu
The premier hilltop fortress of the Malwa Sultans, situated on an isolated ridge of the Vindhyas. Sacked by Alauddin Khilji in 1305 A.D., its extensive 23-mile stone wall fortifications and gates were engineered primarily by Hoshang Shah Ghori from 1405 A.D. onward. The northern approach is guarded by a series of gates, including the *Alamgir*, *Bhangi*, and the monumental **Delhi Darwaza**. The southwestern approach is climbed via the **Tarapur Gate**, a double-arched entrance with zigzag passages enclosed by bastions, strengthened with a third gate by Akbar.

The fort houses over 75 monuments, divided into discrete historic groups:

##### 1. The Royal Enclave
* **Jahaz Mahal (Ship Palace):** A long, double-storeyed palace building engineered lengthwise on a narrow strip of land between the waters of the Munj and Kapur tanks, looking like a ship anchored between lakes. Its grand open terrace is crowned with small cupolas and kiosks, built by Sultan Ghiyas-ud-din for his harem.
* **Hindola Mahal (Swinging Palace):** A large audience hall characterized by massive side walls that slope outwards at a steep angle, creating the optical illusion of a swing.
* **Champa Baodi:** A highly elaborate, deep stepwell containing structural subterranean cooling chambers for summer use, fronting the ruins of the royal apartments, the *Nahar Zaroka* (Tiger's Balcony), and the mosque of Dilawar Khan (the oldest mosque at Mandu, 1405 A.D.).

##### 2. The Central Group
* **Jami Masjid:** The grandest Pathan monument in western India, completed by Mahmud Khilji I. It features an imposing domed porch, a spacious open courtyard enclosed by multiple rows of pointed arches, and a deep western prayer hall containing intricately carved marble *mihrabs*.
* **Asharfi Mahal (The Madrasa):** Located in front of the mosque, originally built as a columned college. Mahmud Khilji filled up its central court to act as a heavy foundation for his marble tomb and elevated its northeastern tower into a magnificent **seven-storeyed tower of victory** (only the basement survives).
* **Tomb of Hoshang Shah:** The earliest marble tomb in India, constructed entirely of white sandstone and marble, crowned with a large dome and fronted by a domed porch. It served as a template for the builders of the Taj Mahal.

##### 3. The Rewa Kund Group
* **Palace of Baz Bahadur:** Built on the slope of a hill overlooking the *Rewa Kund* tank, featuring spacious courts and domed pavilions.
* **Rupmati’s Pavilions:** Perched on the sheer edge of the southern fort ridge, 1,200 feet above the Nimar plain. Originally built as a square, bastioned military observation post, it was later crowned with twin domed pavilions from which Queen Rupmati had her daily *darshan* (view) of the sacred river Narmada flowing on the southern horizon.

### (D) THE NORTH-WESTERN MALWA PLATEAU

#### Mandsaur (Ancient Dashapura)
The capital of the Malavas under Yashodharman (6th century A.D.). Its premier archeological monuments are **Yashodharman's Pillars of Victory**, twin colossal monolithic stone columns discovered half-buried at the village of Sondni, 3 miles away. They are now arranged on a raised masonry platform. Each pillar is 3½ feet in diameter, over 40 feet high, and weighs nearly 200 tons. They bear matching Sanskrit inscriptions recording Yashodharman's victory over the Huna king Mihirakula in 532–533 A.D. The logistics of cutting, carving, and moving these massive single blocks across 100 miles in antiquity remains an archeological marvel. The town preserves a large Gupta image of Shiva and a fragment of a giant doorframe.

### (E) THE NARMADA VALLEY OR NIMAR PLAIN

#### 1. Bagh Caves
A series of **nine rock-cut Buddhist caves** excavated into a sandstone cliff on the Bagni river, serving as a Mahayana monastery from the 4th to 6th centuries A.D.
* **Cave No. 2:** A large structural *vihara* consisting of a central pillared hall surrounded by 20 residential cells for monks, fronted by a portico; the rear sanctuary enshrine a stone *Stupa*. The central columns are massive, covered in spiral fluting.
* **Cave No. 4 (Rang Mahal - Palace of Colours):** The largest and best-preserved cave, containing an extensive pillared hall for 38 monks, its entrance doorways carved in the classic Gupta style. It is celebrated for its extensive **tempera wall paintings**, executed over a local earth-and-lime plaster coat (*rinfazzo*). The open verandah preserves a continuous painted narrative 45 feet long, depicting:
  * A scene of deep sorrow showing a weeping lady consoled by a friend.
  * A group of figures engaged in an open-air religious discourse.
  * Personified male figures issuing from the clouds, viewed by Buddhist *arahats*.
  * Two colorful **Indian classical dance assemblies**, featuring female musicians playing drums, cymbals, and lutes, dancing in circular formations around a central male dancer with long hair.
  * A grand pageant procession consisting of a cavalcade of nobles moving on horses, and a row of stately elephants carrying a royal figure with a fly-whisk and umbrella.

#### 2. Maheshwar
The site of ancient **Mahishmati**, capital of the Puranic Haihayas under Kartivirya Arjuna. It was visited by Hiuen Tsang in 640 A.D. and served as the venue for the philosophical duel between Shankaracharya and Mandana Mishra. Its present architectural grandeur is due entirely to **Queen Ahilyabai Holkar (1767–1795 A.D.)**, who made it her permanent capital. She engineered the extensive, beautiful pink stone **bathing ghats** lining the northern bank of the Narmada, constructed out of dry stone masonry. Her memory is honored by a magnificent **Chhattri (Cenotaph)** on the riverfront, designed in the architectural style of a classical Hindu temple, featuring an entrance porch, a spacious hall, and a tower with a lofty *shikhara* covered in fine decorative carvings.

#### 3. Nemawar
Situated on the northern bank of the Narmada, it was a prominent trading port noted by Al-Biruni. It contains the **Siddheshvar Mahadeva Temple**, a perfectly preserved masterpiece of Paramara architecture built in the 11th–12th centuries A.D. Raised on a large stone-paved plinth along the river, its plan comprises a square sanctum and a large pillared assembly hall (*sabhamandapa*) fronted by three projecting porches. The exterior walls are decorated with rows of multi-armed relief sculptures depicting *Shivaganas*, Bhairava, Shiva performing the *Tandava* dance, Brahma, and *Mahishamardini*. The hall roof is supported by columns with lotus carvings and brackets shaped as female figures in graceful poses. The temple is crowned with a spectacular, tall **Bhumija Shikhara** decorated with vertical rows of miniature spires. To the north stands an unfinished medieval Vishnu temple, its exterior and doorframe covered in Vaishnava reliefs.

## Saiva & Jaina Shrines of Village Un: A Technical Architectural Analysis
*Based on the Monographs of the Directorate of Archaeology, Archives, and Museums, Bhopal, by Swati Mondal Adhikari*

The village of Un is situated on the northern fringe of the Satpura ranges in the West Nimar District, 14 kilometers west of Khargone. It contains an extraordinary cluster of **ten standing medieval structural temples** belonging to the **Bhūmija order** of the Nagara temple style, developed under the direct patronage of the Paramara Kings of Malwa (11th–13th centuries A.D.).

### Core Architectural Mechanics of the Bhūmija Style
1. **The Śikhara (Spire):** The central offset projection (*bhadra*) of each face of the sanctum cube is carried up vertically to the summit of the spire as an unbroken band known as the **latā**. This *latā* is covered in a dense tracery of miniature *chaitya* arches.
2. **Kūṭastambhas:** The four quadrants between the vertical *latās* are filled with horizontal and vertical rows of miniature spires (*śringas*) placed upon tiny columns, technically designated as *kūṭastambhas* or *stambhakūṭas*.
3. **Śukanāsā:** A large integrated gable or *chaitya*-window design enclosing a sculptured medallion projects from the base of the *latā* on the front face of the spire.
4. **Talacchanda (Ground Plan):** Variegated either by a traditional right-angled rectangular layout (**caturaśra** / orthogonal) or by a stellate layout (**vṛtta** / circular) obtained by rotating a square on its axis around the gnomon, producing acute-angled intermediate projections (*rathas*).
5. **Vedībandha (Basal Wall):** The lowermost horizontal zone of the temple wall, consisting of a fixed canonical sequence of bold moldings: *khuraka* (plain base block), *kumbha* (inverted pot), *antarapatta* (recess), *kalaśa* (torus pot), and *kapotapālikā* (cyma-eaves).
6. **Jaṅghā (Wall Space):** The vertical wall area above the base, hosting large central niches on the main *bhadras* for major divinities, while side *rathas* are styled as decorative pilasters.

### Detailed Directory of Extant Shrines at Un

#### A. Saiva Stellate Group

##### 1. Chaubara Dera No. 1
The largest structural temple at Un, situated within the village habitation area, belonging to the *nirandhara* (lacking internal circumambulatory passage) class, facing east.
* **Plan and Elevation:** It features a *pañcaratha* circular-stellate ground plan (*vṛtta* variety) with an inner square *garbhagriha*, fronted by a rectangular *antarala* (vestibule) and a large square *sabhamandapa* with open side balconies (*parsvamandapas*). The roof and *shikhara* have collapsed, but historic data (including a 1931 Holkar State plate) shows it originally carried a five-storeyed (*pañcabhūma*) spire. The *vedībandha* is well preserved; its prominent *kumbha* molding is decorated with a horizontal band of jewels (*manibandhani*) and minor niches filled with diamond motifs.
* **Interior Elements:** The *garbhagriha* doorway is *pañcaśākha* (fivefold), its jambs carved with figures of *dvarapalas*, attendants, and fly-whisk bearers (*camaradharinis*). The northern wall of the *antarala* preserves the famous **Sarpabandha Inscription** (a single rock-cut serpentine coil enclosing an alphabetical chart and Sanskrit grammatical conjugational terminations), alongside a separate inscription containing the name of the Paramara King **Bhoja**. The central hall roof is supported by four massive columns whose base and lower shafts are square and upper shafts circular, covered in multi-tiered relief panels of dancers, musicians, family couples, and monkeys.
* **Iconography:** The door lintel features a central relief of a dancing **Nataraja** (an iconographic novelty) flanked by a panel of the *Sapta Mātṛkās* (Seven Mothers) dancing in *urdhvajanu* postures with Ganesha. The *antarala* architrave accommodates a *pañcadeva* panel containing Ganesha, Brahma, Shiva, Vishnu, and Sarasvati.

##### 2. Mahākāleśvara Temple No. 2
Situated picturesquely on the bank of the Hathni stream, facing east.
* **Structure:** A five-storeyed (*pañcabhūma*) *pañcaratha* circular-stellate structural temple. The *vedībandha* preserves a complete sequence of moldings: *khuraka*, *kumbha*, *antarapatta*, *cippika*, *kalaśa*, *antarapatta*, and *kapotapālikā*. The *shikhara* retains its side quadrants, showing three vertical rows of five *kūṭastambhas* each, though the crowning *amalaka* is missing. The floor of the square sanctum is underground, reached by a descending flight of steps from the door.
* **Iconography:** The *kumbha* niches enclose miniature figures of Ganesha, Chamunda, and Maheśvari. The main *bhadra* wall niches display Chamunda (north), Nataraja (west), and a powerful figure of **Andhakāsura** (south), shown in a destructive mood piercing the demon with a trident (*triśūla*). The northern *śūrasenaka* gable houses a rare figure of *Mahisasuramardini Durga*. A built-in drainage channel is pierced through the southeastern corner of the sanctum wall for discharging ablution water.

##### 3. Mahākāleśvara Temple No. 1
A large *pañcabhūma* *pañcaratha* circular-stellate temple built on the modern ground level near Chaubara Dera No. 1, indicating a later chronological date.
* **Structure:** The low plinth basement (*pīṭha*) is fully exposed, showing a sequence of *jadyakumbha*, *karnikā* (knife-edge molding), and *grāsapaṭṭikā* (grotesque lion-face bands). The *shikhara* is partially intact, showing that internally it was divided into four cells of diminishing size built one over another.
* **Interior and Iconography:** Features an elaborately carved *pañcaśākha* doorframe guarded by multi-armed *dvarapalas* holding tridents and snakes. The door lintel houses the *Sapta Mātṛkās* dancing in *catura* postures with Ganesha and a central *Vīṇādhara Shiva* playing the lute. The main *bhadra* niches host highly rigid, bejewelled, and elaborate representations of Chamunda (north), Nataraja flanked by four structural musicians (west), and *Andhakāntaka* (south).

##### 4. Nameless Temple of Śiva
A small *pañcaratha* circular-stellate fane located at the northwestern corner of the Chaubara Dera No. 1 compound. It consists of a sanctum opening into a small entrance porch (*dvaramandapa*) with stone seating. The *shikhara* has disappeared, but its intact *śukanāsā* houses a figure of Nataraja flanked by fly-whisk bearers. The square sanctum is empty but contains an old *vedi* pedestal showing that it originally enshrined a freestanding image rather than a standard *linga*. The southern *bhadra* niche hosts a figure of Brahma.

##### 5. Haṭakeśvara Temple
Located within the village area on the left side of the Nilakantheshvara temple, facing west. It has suffered catastrophic devastation; its lower plinth moldings, *vedībandha*, and *jangha* wall are entirely buried underground. The exposed portion reveals a *pañcaśākha* doorway with an empty sanctum, its columns and jambs decorated with diamond and *ghata-pallava* motifs characteristic of the Paramara style.

##### 6. Nīlakantheśvara Temple
A grand, high-built *saptaratha* and seven-storeyed (**saptabhūma**) circular-stellate temple, facing west.
* **Structure:** Raised upon an extensive structural terrace, its elevation features an elaborate *pīṭha* and a *vedībandha* of eight horizontal moldings. The *jangha* wall section is divided into acute-angled vertical *rathas* styled as pilasters, with *kūṭastambhas* carved in relief in the recesses (*salilāntaras*).
* **Interior:** The inner face of every single stone block of the *garbhagriha* wall is incised with a miniature naked female figure or syllable, possibly functioning as an architectural alignment mark for the *silpis* (stonemasons). The door lintel houses the *Sapta Mātṛkās* dancing in *ekapāda* (single-legged) postures. The outer wall contains a deep *kumbha* molding deification of a unique nude male figure dancing with a drinking pot in his left hand. The main *bhadra* niches host Chamunda (north), Nataraja (west), and Andhakāntaka (south).

##### 7. Omkāreśvara Temple
The smallest shrine at the site, built as a minor replica of the larger stellate fanes. Its *vedībandha* is intact, featuring a *kumbha* decorated with a fillet of diamonds and minor niches containing four-handed images of Shiva sitting in *lalitāsana* holding tridents and snakes. Everything above the wall base has lost its facing masonry blocks.

#### B. Jaina Orthogonal Group (Caulukyan Style)

##### 8. Ballāleśvara Temple
A rare **pañcaratha orthogonal (caturaśra)** structural temple, facing east.
* **Structure:** The original *shikhara* spire has collapsed and was replaced in the 18th century by a plain Maratha brick dome. The plinth and base moldings display sharp, well-preserved linear lines. The *garbhagriha* contains a built-in stone water tank in its northeastern corner for storing ablution water.
* **Iconography:** The *jangha* wall contains a single horizontal row of image sculptures representing the *Aṣṭadikpālas* (Guardians of the Eight Quarters), including a bearded, haloed figure of Agni and an image of Varuna. The main west and north *bhadra* niches contain Chamunda and Nataraja. The door lintel houses the *Sapta Mātṛkās* in *lalitāsana* positions with Ganesha and *Vīṇādhara* Shiva.
* **Secular Art:** Renowned for highly dynamic secular panels on its exterior walls depicting sword-fights, three warriors clashing, elephant riders in battle, and a *nṛtyorati* scene showing a female *devadasī* dancer performing an *Aratinṛtya* accompanied by *mridanga* drummers.

##### 9. Chaubara Dera No. 2
An imposing, north-facing *pañcaratha* temple built on a strict orthogonal (*caturaśra*) plan, breaking away from the Saiva stellate tradition.
* **Structure:** Raised on a high platform, its plinth introduces distinctive elements of the **Maru-Gurjara (Caulukyan) style** of Gujarat, including a **gajathara** (molding band carved with a continuous line of elephants) and a **narathara** (molding band carved with a continuous pageant of human figures, battles, and myths) among the basal moldings. The roof has collapsed.
* **Interior:** The closed assembly hall (*gudhamandapa*) roof is supported by an intricate group of eight columns whose shafts are square and bases octagonal, lacking figure sculptures but decorated with spiral bands of stenciled scrolls, *hamsapaṭṭikā* (goose bands), and *grāsapaṭṭikā* (grotesque lion-mask bands). The door threshold (*udumbara*) is conventionally chiseled, and the *lalāṭabimbasthāna* niche houses a figure of a nude Tirthankara seated under an umbrella with a *śrīvatsa* mark on his chest. The *narathara* base displays narrative panels depicting the *Ramayana* story of Rama killing Bali, and the Puranic churning of the ocean (*Samudra Manthan*).

##### 10. Goaleśvara Temple
Situated on a small southern hillock, facing north.
* **Structure:** It features a fully orthogonal plan with a traditional northern *Nagara shikhara* capped with an *āmalaka* and *kalaśa*, thoroughly renovated in later times. The wall elevation lacks the central *bhadra* niches and figure sculptures characteristic of the Paramara style, utilizing instead a series of plain *grāsapaṭṭikā* bands.
* **Interior:** The *garbhagriha* floor level sits below the hall, accessed by descending steps. It enshrines three colossal *Digambara* Jain images standing in *kayotsarga* postures: **Shantinath** (the largest, measuring 12½ feet in height), **Kunthunath**, and **Arhanath**, flanked by stiffly executed fly-whisk bearers. A short votive inscription on the pedestal of the left image states it was dedicated by the Jain Acharya **Yashakirti**, dated in **1206 A.D.** (*V.S. 1263*).

### Masons' Marks and Quarry Sites at Un
The structural stone blocks of the Un temples preserve a significant epigraphical and symbolic record of signatures left by the medieval *silpis* (architects and craftsmen).
* On **Chaubara Dera No. 1**, the names **Ratanu**, **Bala**, **Ilapala**, **Barālaba**, and **Supata** are engraved. The signatures of *Ratanu* and *Ilapala* are carved with extreme calligraphic care on the main hall beams and pilaster capitals, signifying their status as master builders.
* On **Mahākāleśvara Temple No. 1**, a completely separate set of names occurs: **Pradipa**, **Jatā**, **Bakata**, **Matata**, **Ladala**, **Ramapa**, **Paja**, **Ratala**, **Gonā**, **Nananara**, and **Babala**, confirming that an entirely different generation or guild of masons was employed for its construction.
* **Ballāleśvara Temple** features the signatures of *Badanna*, *Badena*, *Sadamata*, and *Pakamu*, while the **Nīlakantheśvara Temple** bears the signature of **Sipana**.

Masons' marks are found incised across the stone blocks, including cross marks, triangles, conch shells (on Mahākāleśvara No. 1), and zoomorphic signs like scorpions and fish. Identical fish marks are carved inside the sanctum walls of Chaubara Dera No. 1 and the Ballāleśvara, indicating overlapping working parties.

The primary resource quarry was **Chirakhan**, a rocky sandstone highland located directly north of Un. The name derives from local words meaning "stone cutting" (*chira*) and "quarry" (*khan*). Marks of medieval stone-splitting are still present on the rock surfaces. The quarry yields a coarse-grained sandstone of relatively low durability, explaining why the Un cluster suffered rapid structural decay compared to temples built with high-quality pink sandstone.

### Technical Exegesis and Epigraphical Texts

#### Nos. 1–2: Two Harsōlā Copper-Plate Grants of Sīyaka (Vikrama Samvat 1005 / 949 A.C.)

##### Historical Context and Discovery
These twin copper-plate grants represent the **earliest known epigraphical records** of the Paramāra house of Malwa. They were noticed by D. B. Diskalkar in the *Annual Report of the Watson Museum, Rajkot (1922-23)*, and subsequently edited in the *Epigraphia Indica (Vol. XIX)* jointly with K. N. Dikshit. The plates were found in the possession of a Visnagara Brahmana named Bhatta Magan MotiRam, a resident of the village **Harsōlā** in the Prantija taluka of the Ahmedabad District, Gujarat. Both grants were issued by the same king on the same day to two Brahmanas who were related as father and son: **Grant A (No. 1)** to the father, Lallopadhyaya, and **Grant B (No. 2)** to the son, Nina Dikshita.

##### Technical Description
Each grant consists of two rectangular copper plates engraved **only on the inner side**. The edges are raised into thick rims to protect the writing. Grant A features a hole (5 cm diameter) cut through the lower margin of the first plate and upper margin of the second, held originally by a copper ring. The first plate contains 16 lines and the second 11 lines of writing. In the lower left corner of the second plate of Grant A is incised a prominent representation of a **flying Garuda** (the royal emblem of the Paramaras of Malwa), depicted with a human body and bird wings, holding a snake in his right arm. The characters are Nagari of the 10th century A.C. The head-strokes of letters show distinct curvatures or zigzags. The language is Sanskrit, composed in a mixture of prose and poetry.

##### Historical Analysis
The grants were issued by the Mahamandalika-chudamani, the illustrious **Sīyaka** (Siyaka II), from his military encampment on the banks of the **Mahi river**, on his return from a successful campaign against **Yögarāja** (identified either as a chief of the Chavda dynasty of Anhilwad-Patan or a Chalukya prince of southern Saurashtra). The records open by invoking the blessings of Vishnu in his Varaha boar incarnation, followed by acknowledging the overlordship of the Imperial Rashtrakūta sovereigns of Malkhed: **Amoghavarshadēva** (Amoghavarsha III) and his successor **Akālavarshadeva** (Krishna III, 939–966 A.C.). Siyaka explicitly styles himself *Mahamandalika-chudamani*, indicating his status as a premier Rashtrakuta feudatory at this date, prior to his later rebellion and assumption of sovereign titles (*Maharajadhirajapati*).

The records trace Siyaka's immediate pedigree within the family (*tasmin kule*): introducing **Bappaiparāja** (the Prakrit equivalent of Vakpatiraja I, ruling c. 900 A.C. as a Rashtrakuta vassal), succeeded by his son **Vairisimha** (Vairisimha II, who pressed into Malwa and held Ujjain), succeeded by his son Siyaka. The grants record the perpetual donation of the villages **Kumbhārōtaka** and **Sīhakā** situated within the **Mõhadavāsaka vishaya** (modern Modasa in the Sabarkantha district, Gujarat) to the Nagara Brahmanas who had migrated from **Anandapura** (modern Vadnagar). The grants were written by the Kayastha Gunadhara and passed on by the *dapaka* Thakkura Vishnu. The decimal date recorded is **Māgha vadi 30, Wednesday, 1005 (Vikrama Samvat)**, which regularly corresponds to **31st January, 949 A.C.**

#### No. 3: The Second Plate of the Ahmedabad Grant of Sīyaka (Vikrama Samvat 1026 / 969 A.C.)
This single copper plate represents the final half of a separate bilateral charter issued by **Siyaka**. It was obtained around 1920 A.C. by a pleader of Kaira, Gujarat, and presented to Muni Jinavijayaji of the Puratattva Mandir, Ahmedabad (now preserved in the L. D. Institute of Indology). The plate measures 34 cm by 19 cm, featuring two horizontal ring holes and raised rims. The lower right corner displays a human-shaped flying Garuda with wings, holding a hooded snake in his left hand. The characters are Nagari of the 10th century. The language is Sanskrit, composed entirely in verse, except for the date and the *dapaka* record.

The first plate containing the pedigree and the specific gift land is lost. However, the existing text provides crucial chronological data. Line 9 explicitly records the date in words: **the fifteenth day of the dark half of Āśvina of the year 1026 (Vikrama Samvat)**, which corresponds regularly to **Thursday, 14th October, 969 A.C.** This proves that Siyaka continued to rule over his consolidated territories up to 969 A.C., and was succeeded by his son Vakpati Munja prior to 975 A.C. (the date of the Dharampuri grant). The *dapaka* who mediated the grant was **Kanhapaika**, the same official who served under Vakpati Munja. The plate concludes with Siyaka's large bold sign-manual: *Śrī-Sīyakasya svahastōyam*.

#### No. 4: Dharampuri Grant of Vākpatirājadēva (Vikrama Samvat 1031 / 974 A.C.)

##### Historical Context
This represents the **earliest known royal charter issued from Malwa** by Siyaka's son and successor, **Vakpatiraja II (Munja)**. The plates were dug out by a cultivator in a field at **Dharampuri**, a historic town on the northern bank of the Narmada river, Dhar District, Madhya Pradesh. They were first translated by FitzEdward Hall in 1861 and edited by N. J. Kirtane in 1877 (*Indian Antiquary, Vol. VI*). They are now preserved in the India Office Library, London.

##### Technical Metrics
The set comprises two plates engraved **on one side only**, measuring 30.5 cm wide by 21.6 cm high, held by two copper rings passing through circular holes. The lower left corner of the second plate houses a rectangular space containing a human-form flying Garuda holding a cobra. The characters are bold Nagari of the late 10th century, featuring triangular top-strokes. The language is Sanskrit prose, framed by two introductory and five concluding imprecatory stanzas.

##### Epigraphical and Historical Exegesis
The charter establishes the sovereign genealogy of the Paramaras of Malwa following their independence from the Rashtrakutas. It introduces the grantor, **Vākpatirājadēva**, who assumed the imperial Rashtrakuta titles of **Amoghavarshadēva**, *Prithvivallabha*, and *Śrīvallabha*. He is designated as the lord paramount meditating on the feet (*padanudhyata*) of the *Paramabhattaraka, Maharajadhiraja, Paramesvara* **Sīyakadēva**, who meditated on the feet of **Vairisimhadēva**, who meditated on the feet of **Krishnarājadēva** (Vakpati's grandfather, also called Upendra/Bappairaja).

The charter records the imperial donation of a *tadāra* (a defined agricultural estate or territory) named **Pipparikā** (identified with modern Pipri in the Manawar tahsil, Dhar District, north of the Narmada), which was connected to the administrative division of **Gardabhapānīya** (modern Gandhvani). The boundaries are explicitly tracked: bounded on the east by the *Agaravāhalā* stream (the modern Man river), on the north by the *Chikhillikā* ditch (modern Chikhli village), on the west by the *Gardabha* river, and on the south by the *Piśāchadēva-tīrtha* (a holy site on the Narmada surviving to this day).

Vakpati issued this permanent charter from his stay at the historic city of **Ujjayanī** (Ujjain), after performing ritual ablutions in the waters of the *Śiva-tadāga* cistern. The recipient was the learned philosopher-Brahmana **Vasantāchārya**, son of Pandita Dhanika, who had migrated to the southern region from **Ahichchhatra** (Rohelkhand). The royal order was passed through the *dapaka* **Kanhapaika** and written by Kayastha Gunadhara. The date recorded both in words and decimal figures is **the fourteenth day of the bright half of Bhadrapada, V.S. 1031**, corresponding to **3rd September, 974 A.C.**

#### No. 5: Ujjain Grant of Vākpatirājadēva (Vikrama Samvat 1036 / 980 A.C.)
This bilateral structural charter was discovered in a ruin near **Ujjain** and edited by Franz Kielhorn (*Indian Antiquary, Vol. XIV*); it is preserved in the India Office Library, London. The two plates measure 32 cm by 24 cm, bound by a copper ring. The Nagari script shows a technical advancement, substituting *parasavarna* class-nasals for the standard *anusvara*. The imperial titles and genealogy of **Vakpatiraja Amoghavarsha** remain completely identical to the Dharampuri record.

The charter records the donation of the village **Sembalapura** (modern Semlia), which was included within the administrative twelve-village-group of **Tiņisapadra-dvādaśuka** (modern Titrod, near Sitamau in the Mandsaur District) under the military jurisdiction (*bhukti*) of the governor (*Mahāsādhanika*) **Mahāïka**. The donation was made by Vakpati from his temporary military camp (*skandhavara*) at **Bhagavatpura** (modern Bhagor, situated on the sacred Chambal river), at the specific request of **Asinī**, the wife of the governor Mahāïka.

The grant was issued on the full-moon day of Kārttika, on the occasion of a **lunar eclipse in V.S. 1036** (regularly corresponding to **6th November, 979 A.C.**). The purpose was to provide permanent resources for the ritual worship, dynamic ablutions (*snana-vilepana*), incense, and extensive structural repairs (*khanda-sphutita-devagriha-jagati-samaracanartham*) of the temple of the goddess **Bhattēśvarī** (the premier goddess *Bhattārikā*, now known as the *Hara-siddhi* temple) at Ujjain. The final execution plate was signed four months later at the camp of **Gunapura** (modern Gunavad near Dhar) on the **9th day of the dark half of Chaitra, V.S. 1036**, following a major victory (*mahāvijaya-skandhāvarē*) won against western adversaries. The *dapaka* was the prime minister **Rudrāditya**.

#### No. 6: Gaonri Copper-Plate Inscription of Vākpatirājadēva (I) (Vikrama Samvat 1038 / 981 A.C.)

##### Discovery and Typography
This extraordinary archeological document consists of **three large copper plates**, discovered on 20th June, 1931, while cutting a drainage channel for a tank at **Gaonri** (*Gonry*), a village near Narwal, 18 kms. southeast of Ujjain. It was edited by K. N. Dikshit in *Epigraphia Indica (Vol. XXIII)*. A spectacular feature is that the outer side of the first plate is a **palimpsest** containing an erased, earlier official record of the Rashtrakuta King Suvarṇavarsha (Govind IV), dated *Śaka 851*, which preceded this grant by 52 years—proving that Siyaka or Vakpati seized the blank or executed plates as booty from the Malkhed treasury.

The plates measure 39 cm long by 26 cm wide, weighing 6.43 kgms. The lower right corner of the third plate is incised with a beautifully modeled flying Garuda holding a snake. The script is a highly cursive, elegant Nagari of the late 10th century.

##### Historical and Socio-Cultural Analysis
The charter records the permanent collective donation of the village **Vaņikā** situated within the **Avaraka-bhōga** in **Hūna-mandala** (identified by Trivedi with modern Bani and Avra in the Mandsaur District, where the Hunas had remained since their defeat by Yashodharman). Vakpati Amoghavarsha made the grant from his capital camp after taking an eclipse bath in the holy *Shivatadaga* waters on the **full-moon day of Kārttika, V.S. 1038** (**16th October, 981 A.C.**). The plates were formally executed and delivered nine months later on the **10th day of the bright half of the second Āṣāḍha, V.S. 1038** (**3rd July, 982 A.C.**), through the minister *dapaka* **Rudrāditya**.

The unique importance of this charter lies in its meticulous enumeration of **thirty-six learned Brahmanas** who received defined shares (*amsa*) of the village. It lists their names, fathers' names, gotras, Vedic recession, and **their original home regions (*bhatta-gramas*)**, proving massive intellectual migration into Malwa under early Paramara patronage from the following distant cultural zones:
* **Magadha:** Donees like Sarvananda, son of Lokananda, from *Kanopa*.
* **Dakshina-Rādha (Bengal):** Donees from *Vilvagavāsa* belonging to the Samaveda Chhandogya recension.
* **Savathikā (Śrāvasti, Bengal):** Donees from *Dardurikā* and *Mitilapāṭaka*.
* **Lāṭa-dēśa (Southern Gujarat):** Donees from *Nandipura* (Nandod on the Narmada).
* **Madhyadēśa & Sthāṇvīśvara (Thanesar, Punjab):** Multiple scholars tracking orthodox lineages.

#### No. 7: Gaonri Copper-Plate Inscription of Vākpatirājadēva (II) (Vikrama Samvat 1043 / 986 A.C.)
Discovered in the same hoard at **Gaonri** and edited by K. N. Dikshit, this set comprises two plates weighing 2.25 kgms, executed in a highly symmetrical, deep Nagari script. The mangala-slokas and the imperial genealogy of **Vakpatiraja Amoghavarsha** are identical to the preceding records.

The charter records that while staying in the strategic eastern territory of **Pūrṇa-pathaka** (modern Punasa, near Mandhata on the Narmada), Vakpati donated the village of **Kadahichchhakā** (modern Karcha, north of Narwal in the Ujjain District), which was situated within the **Maddhuka-bhukti** of the **Ujjayanī vishaya** in the **Avantī-mandala**.

The sole recipient was the same premier Brahmana scholar **Sarvānanda**, son of Dikshit Lokananda of the *Sankriti gotra* and *Asvalayana shakha*, who was the senior partner in the preceding multi-donee grant. Vakpati made this grant after taking a holy bath in the *Punyabhra-sarit* river on the occasion of the winter solstice (*Udagayana*) in the month of Magha. The plates were formally delivered on the **thirteenth day of the dark half of Māgha, V.S. 1043** (**31st December, 986 A.C.**), constituting the latest known epigraphical date for Vakpati Munja's rule.

## PART IV: COMPREHENSIVE SANSKRIT-ENGLISH ARCHITECTURAL GLOSSARY

* **Āmalaka (आमलक):** A massive, ribbed or serrated circular stone disc crowning the summit of a northern (*Nagara*) *shikhara* spire [cite: 6749, 8272].
* **Aṅgabhāva (अङ्गभाव):** The stylized bodily posture, gesture, or stance expressing distinct emotional states (*rasas*) in both classical dance and sculpture [cite: 7957].
* **Antarāla (अन्तराल):** A small intermediate chamber, vestibule, or ante-chamber connecting the dark inner sanctum with the pillared assembly hall [cite: 6750, 6808, 8275].
* **Antarapaṭṭa (अन्तरपट्ट):** A recessed architectural molding band or horizontal channel separating two prominent projecting base moldings [cite: 6816, 7080, 8276].
* **Apsarā (अप्सरा):** A beautiful celestial nymph or female dancer gracing the surfaces of columns and exterior walls in various classical dance poses (*sthanas*) [cite: 6846, 7919, 8277].
* **Baraṇḍिकā (वरण्डिका):** A corniced molding or eaves section forming the uppermost zone of the wall, separating it from the curvilinear spire above [cite: 6747, 6814, 8283].
* **Bhadra (भद्र):** The central major offset projection running up each face of a temple's plan and elevation [cite: 6703, 6709, 8284].
* **Bhūmija (भूमिज):** Literally "country-born"; a highly distinct northern (*Nagara*) regional temple style characterized by a spire filled with vertical and horizontal rows of miniature *kūṭastambhas* clustered between four vertical central spines (*latās*) [cite: 6270, 6701, 8287].
* **Caturaśra (चतुरश्र):** A strict right-angled rectangular or orthogonal ground plan layout [cite: 6175, 6719, 8291].
* **Cippikā (चिप्पिका):** A minor, slender horizontal molding with a distinct convex profile line used in the base structure [cite: 6983, 7402].
* **Dvaramandapa (द्वारमण्डप):** A small, open structural entrance porch or balcony-cum-entrance fronting a hall [cite: 7318, 7320, 8295].
* **Gajathara (गजथर):** A prominent decorative plinth molding carved with a continuous horizontal band of elephants, characteristic of the Caulukyan style [cite: 7665, 7706, 8301].
* **Garbhagṛha (गर्भगृह):** The dark, square inner sanctum chamber where the primary deity is enshrined [cite: 6741, 6805, 8301].
* **Grīvā (ग्रीवा):** Literally "neck"; the cylindrical structural neck portion of the spire supporting the crowning *amalaka* [cite: 6749, 6749, 8213].
* **Grāsamukha / Grāsapaṭṭikā (ग्रासमुख / ग्रासपट्टिका):** A highly stylized decorative lion-like monster mask motif with bulging eyes; a molding band composed of these repetitive monster faces [cite: 7078, 7687, 8304, 8305].
* **Gūḍhamaṇḍapa (गूढमण्डप):** A fully enclosed, walled structural assembly hall preceding the vestibule [cite: 7661, 7719, 8306].
* **Jaṅghā (जङ्घा):** The middle vertical wall section of the temple structure, hosting major divine niches, running between the base moldings and the upper cornices [cite: 6743, 6814, 8308].
* **Kalaśa (कलश):** A bold pot-shaped base moulding; also the final vase-like metallic or stone pinnacle crowning the summit of a spire [cite: 6749, 6816, 8310].
* **Kapoṭapālikā (कपोतपालिका):** A horizontal base moulding carved with a distinct projecting cyma-eaves profile line [cite: 6816, 7080, 8312].
* **Karṇikā (कर्णिका):** A sharp, horizontal base moulding featuring a distinct knife-edge or V-shaped profile line [cite: 7078, 7235, 8315].
* **Kūṭastambha (कूटस्तम्भ):** A miniature structural spire replica supported by a tiny pillar, arranged in vertical and horizontal rows across the quadrants of a *Bhumija shikhara* [cite: 6706, 6736, 8321].
* **Latā (लता):** Literally "creeper"; the unbroken curvilinear vertical band or spine running up the center of each face of a *Bhumija* spire, covered in a rich tracery of *chaitya* arches [cite: 6703, 6710, 8327].
* **Mañcikā (मञ्चिका):** A set of three thin moldings serving as a baseline pedestal or shelf for the vertical *jangha* and its niches [cite: 6819, 6984, 8327].
* **Mandovara (मण्डोवर):** The complete vertical wall portion of a temple structure, comprising the three distinct zones of *vedībandha*, *jangha*, and *barandika* [cite: 6715, 6741, 8331].
* **Narathara (नरथर):** A horizontal plinth molding carved with a continuous pageant of human figures, warriors, battles, and mythical narratives [cite: 7665, 7698, 8338].
* **Nirandhara (निरन्धर):** A temple plan lacking an internal circumambulatory pathway around the inner sanctum chamber [cite: 6802, 7543].
* **Pañcaratha / Saptaratha (पञ्चरथ / सप्तरथ):** A ground plan and elevation profile featuring five (*pancharatha*) or seven (*saptaratha*) vertical offset projections or buttresses [cite: 6734, 6739, 6976, 7543].
* **Pīṭha (पीठ):** The heavily molded basement, platform, or stone socle forming the lowermost base of a structural temple [cite: 7078, 7401, 8213].
* **Ratha / Salilāntara (रथ / सलिलान्तर):** A vertical offset projection along the wall; the narrow vertical recess, channel, or flute running between two *rathas* [cite: 6708, 6806, 6985].
* **Sabhāmaṇḍapa (सभामण्डप):** The large, open pillared congregation or assembly hall fronting the vestibule [cite: 6741, 6833].
* **Samatalapadmaka Vitāna (समतलपद्मक वितान):** A flat, non-concentric stone ceiling panel deeply chiseled with central geometric lotus patterns [cite: 6840, 7110, 7336].
* **Śukanāsā (शुकनासा):** A prominent integrated gable or large antefix arch design projecting from the lowermost front face of the spire over the vestibule roof [cite: 6706, 7087, 8216].
* **Śūrasenaka (शूरसेनक):** A large triangular or gable-ended decorative face housing sculptured medallions (often *Nataraja* positions) at the immediate base of each central *lata* band on the spire [cite: 7087, 7091, 7235].
* **Talacchanda / Urdhvacchanda (तलच्छन्द / ऊर्ध्वच्छन्द):** The horizontal ground plan layout; the vertical elevation profile blueprint of a structural building [cite: 6803, 6810].
* **Udumbara / Candraśilā (उदुम्बर / चन्द्रशिला):** The highly carved lower stone threshold of an entrance doorjamb; the semicircular moonstone step preceding it [cite: 6829, 7678].
* **Vedībandha (वेदीबन्ध):** The heavy, horizontal group of foundational base moldings forming the lowermost zone of the vertical temple wall [cite: 6742, 6814, 8331].
* **Vṛtta (वृत्त):** A circular or circular-stellate ground plan obtained by rotating a square on its axis around the central gnomon [cite: 6175, 6719, 6730, 6807].
