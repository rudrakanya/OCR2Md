# 1. INTRODUCTION: UDAYPUR, A SITE OF HERITAGE AND GEOLOGICAL IMPORTANCE

Udaypur is a village panchayat in Ganj Basoda Tehsil of the Vidisha district. As rivers in this landscape are absent, the region is dependent on a network of surface water bodies such as lakes and ponds, formed from natural depressions on the ground. Nestled within the serene landscape, this village is home to the ASI-protected Neelkantheshwar Temple, a testament to the town's historical significance. As you explore its sacred premises, you are transported to a time when craftsmanship and spirituality converged.

Udaypur's allure extends beyond the temple, with a distinctive feature being its abundance of dry masonry stone structures in abundance all over. These traditional dwellings, constructed from locally sourced materials, showcase the architectural prowess of the region. What sets them apart are the interesting courtyards that bring life to the stone-cladded structures. These courtyards serve as communal spaces where residents gather, fostering a sense of community and warmth.

There is a huge Mahal complex in the present village habitation. It is in ruins, yet it shows the grandeur it possessed when it was in complete shape. It is a fortified residential palace. It's not dated, but the site examination shows that it had been the seat of power for centuries with different rulers. Who built it and modified it over time is all shrouded in layers of history. It is said to have been built by King Udayaditya when he established the city. It sprawled over a few acres and housed the royal palaces, temples, open spaces, and a triple gateway (Teen Darwaza) leading to the royal square, all within a fortified wall with several gates and watch towers.

This area is often referred to as the walled city. The gates still stand, but the walled city is not seen in continuity. Parts of the broken city wall can be seen at various places.

The town's character as a village has remained intact, offering a picturesque glimpse into rural life where tradition and simplicity harmonize seamlessly.

From the low-lying elevation of the village precinct, one can see the overlooking hill. While walking towards this unique highland plateau, the natural landscape of the land consists of various surface water bodies, including Baolis and Talabs.

### 3. WHAT IS GEO-HERITAGE?

Geo-heritage refers to sites or landscapes that possess significant geological features, providing a glimpse into the Earth's natural history, evolutionary processes, and the relationship between geology and human civilization. These sites hold cultural, scientific, and educational value and play an essential role in preserving the legacy of geological formations.

The concept of geo-heritage is deeply tied to the idea of geo-diversity, which includes the variety of rocks, minerals, fossils, landforms, and physical landscapes that shape the Earth's surface. Over millions of years, geological forces such as plate tectonics, erosion, sedimentation, and climate change have contributed to the formation of diverse landscapes. Many of these formations hold historical, cultural, and religious significance, making them an integral part of a region's heritage.

Geo-heritage sites are recognized for their scientific, educational, aesthetic, and cultural importance. They often provide key evidence of Earth's past environments, climate changes, and biological evolution. Some geo-heritage sites also reveal early human interactions with geology, such as rock shelters, cave paintings, petroglyphs, and ancient quarry sites.

### 3.1 What is a Geo-Heritage Site?

According to scientific terminology, geo-heritage sites are locations that contain geological features of significant cultural, scientific, historical, and aesthetic value. These sites can provide insights into Earth's formation, climate history, and biodiversity. They also serve an educational objective, helping researchers, students, and visitors understand the processes that have shaped our planet over millions of years.

The Geological Survey of India (GSI) and various state governments recognize and protect geo-heritage sites in India. At present, India has 34 nationally recognized geological heritage monument sites, which are preserved for their exceptional geological characteristics. Some of these include:

Malu Caves, Meghalaya – Known for their complex limestone formations and ancient fossils.

### 3.2 Scope of the Present Study

The present study on Udaypur's geo-heritage examines the region's unique geological and cultural landscape. This research highlights the following critical aspects of the site:

1. Geological Formations- Understanding the composition, structure, and evolution of Udaypur's rock formations, including its butte and mesa landforms, caves, and sedimentary layers. The study analyzes how natural forces such as erosion, weathering, and tectonic activity have shaped the terrain.

2. Flora and Fauna- Documenting the biodiversity of Udaypur's geo-heritage site, including endemic plant species, wildlife, and cave-dwelling organisms such as bats, reptiles, and insects.

3. Historical Context- Tracing the 2,000-year history of human settlements in and around Udaypur, from ancient times to the Parmar dynasty and beyond.

4. Heritage Structures- Identifying and analyzing the temples, fortifications, stepwells, inscriptions, petroglyphs, and other architectural remnants that reflect the region's historical significance.

5. Socio-Cultural Aspects- Exploring the traditions, folklore, and religious practices associated with Udaypur's landscape, as well as the local community's interaction with the site over generations.

6. Other Relevant Aspects- Investigating geo-tourism potential, conservation challenges, and strategies for sustainable preservation of Udaypur's geo-heritage.

### 3.3 Research Approach

Since Udaypur is a geological formation site, the research involved systematic and multidisciplinary fieldwork conducted by experts in geology, archaeology, architecture, and environmental sciences. The INTACH Bhopal Chapter, along with geologists and historians, conducted an extensive survey to document the geological and cultural significance of the site.

Modern scientific methods such as:

- Topographic and geological mapping

- Remote sensing and GIS-based analysis

- Structural analysis of rock formations

- Carbon dating of organic samples (if applicable)

- Photographic documentation of inscriptions and petroglyphs

were used to gain a deeper understanding of the site's geo-heritage value.

### 3.4 Importance of Geo-Heritage Conservation

Geo-heritage conservation is essential to:

- Preserves unique geological formations that tell the story of Earth's past.
- Protect ancient cultural and religious sites from degradation and encroachment.
- Promote geo-tourism, which can provide sustainable economic benefits to local communities.
- Enhance scientific research and education by offering real-world examples of geological processes.
- Prevent threats such as illegal mining, deforestation, and urbanization, which could irreversibly damage the site's natural and cultural heritage.

By recognizing and promoting Udaypur's geo-heritage, this study aims to contribute to its long-term preservation and sustainable development as a heritage tourism destination.

### 4.3 Topography

Vidisha is on the Vindhyanchal plateau, which has a number of spurs that point to the north and northeast. Vidisha district forms part of the Malwa plateau and the Vindhya hill range with undulating topography. The district is primarily an agricultural district occupying the Betwa Basin valley, having a predominantly agricultural economy.

Geological landforms are natural features that shape the Earth. They are the result of various geological processes and can be found across the planet, encompassing a wide range of shapes, sizes, and formations. Landforms provide valuable insights into the Earth's history and are crucial in understanding the dynamic processes that have shaped our planet over millions of years.

It is commonly assumed that the geomorphic evolution of tablelands, where durable caprock overlies weaker formations, proceeds via escarpment retreat, which leaves residual tabular hills (mesas) in front of the preceding scarps. ### Negligible dissection of the top surface

The mesa of Udaypur typifies a hill where dissection is negligible. The entirely planar top surface is 230 m long and up to 120 m wide. It is bounded by nearly continuous cliff lines developed in moderately bedded sandstones with alternating spurs and minor recesses. A weak sandy claystone horizon occurs at the base of the cliffs, accounting for the development of horizontal slots (“bedding caves”) and cavernous weathering, which in turn promote cliff instability and localized rock failures. In a few places, the continuity of cliff lines is interrupted by less steep sections, apparently developed upon more jointed compartments, and turned into blocky piles, but none of them extends far into the top surface. The best example is to show the role of joint-guided weathering in mesa fragmentation. The northwestern and central part of the mesa turned into rock mazes, dissected along a few box canyons and numerous ravines, clefts, and impenetrable slots, all following two principal joint directions: WNW-ESE and NNE-SSW. These linear features isolate massive sandstone compartments, rectangular in plan and typically measuring from 20×30 m to 40×40 m. Apart from minor tilting, no evidence of gravity-driven block displacement has been found, and selective weathering appears as the principal mechanism of mesa fragmentation. Evacuation of loose sand occurs in two ways. First, via episodic surface flow, attested by extensive sheets of sandy accumulation at the outlet of some clefts. However, no permanent channels are present. Second, subsurface removal of sand must take place, evidenced by sandy cones emerging from joints and from beneath blocks of sandstone. It is consistent with closed depressions within the rock labyrinth. Catastrophic processes also take part. By contrast to the western part, the eastern margin of Udaypur Hill remains poorly dissected and forms a continuous cliff line, which is affected by episodic rockfall.

## 4.12 Summary of field morphometric evidence

Field observations and the comparison of the morphology of various hills, coupled with the use of a total station survey, allow us to hypothesize multiple evolutionary pathways of residual hills, from the mesa stage to the complete decay, and to extend the rather simplistic unidirectional trajectory presented in textbooks and generally followed in our previous study from the area. Quantitative data on the morphometry of residual hills are used to support the reasoning, although they need to be considered with caution because of a limited dataset.

The ‘model’ trajectory, from a mesa through a butte to a pinnacle, is represented by Udaypur Hill as the penultimate stage and a cylindrical hill as the ultimate stage. A butte is likely to develop from a moderately fragmented but deeply dissected mesa, where caprock is thick, marginal slopes are nearly vertical cliffs, and continuous (although not necessarily frequent) rock falls detach large volumes of rock, reducing the caprock towards a butte, perhaps of smaller magnitude as suggested by the small size of boulders on the lower slopes of Udaypur Hill, will reduce its extent even further, towards a pinnacle. ## 9. UDAYPUR SETTLEMENT AND SURROUNDING NATURAL LANDSCAPE

Water attained paramount importance in the undulating plains of Central India due to its semi-arid climatic conditions. Because of this, the collection, preservation, and use of water became one of the defining characteristics to trace human settlements in the Udaypur region. Often, the highest point of the mound is occupied by a temple that marks the center of the settlement, while the streets snake along the flow of water to drain it out from high ground into surrounding agricultural areas supporting the settlement. These manmade lines of the street connect with the natural flow and mark the movement of people and water through the landscape. ### 9.4 Types of Fortifications

Fortification, more than many other types of architecture, an integral relationship with the surrounding cultural landscape. They exhibit a number of principles present in all regions of the world and manifest in every period of human history. Perhaps more than other categories and types of heritage, an understanding of the meaning, history, and strategic rationales for the design and location of fortifications is indispensable for their proper conservation and protection.

The forts typology in India can be understood as per traditional texts. The Indian treatise of Arthashastra describes six major types of forts differentiated by their major mode of defense.

- Jaldurga – Water fortification
- Marudurga – Desert fortification
- Giridurga – Mountainous fortification
- Vandurga – Forest fortification
- Mahi durga – Earthen fortification
- Nridurga – Human fortification

Jaldurg: a fortress surrounded by water, also known as audaka-durga and ab-durga. There are two sub-types the land fortress, or antardvipa-durga, and the coastal fortress or sthala-durga.

Giri durg: Giri-durga, or parvata-durga, is a hill or mountain fortress. There are three varieties: prantaradurga, giri-parshva-durga and guha-durga. Prantara-durga is a fortress built on the summit (usually flat) of a hill or a mountain. In giri-parshva-durga both major civilian structures and fortifications extend down the slope of a hill or mountain though the summit is certainly included into the defense system, too. The living quarters of a guha-durga fortress are situated in a valley surrounded by high, impassable hills.

Vanadurg or vrikshya-durga, would be surrounded on all sides with a dense, impassable forest over a distance of at least 4 kos (14.6 km). Variations were the khanjana-durga, built on fens and encircled with thorny woods, and the sthambha-durga, erected in the jungles among high trees but lacking sufficient sources of water.

Curtain: An enclosing wall connecting two bastions or similar outworks.

Terreplein: The top platform or horizontal surface of a rampart where guns are mounted.

Rampart: The fortification wall at Udaypur hillfort has a very important feature, i.e., the rampart is the broad-topped part of a defensive wall around a fort. It served as a walkway to facilitate the movement of soldiers during an attack.

### 10.16 Immediate Considerations

1. General Stability: The main objective is to provide general stability for all fortification structures in order to prevent further failures and to retard deterioration. Causes of problems must first be understood and treated before obvious effects are remedied. Not treating the causes can likely lead to the reappearance of problems, as is evident in a crack that has appeared in a recently repaired section of pitching at the southwest corner of the fortification wall. Comprehensive measures must be undertaken for complete protection. At present, work is limited to the repair of damaged structures. Remedial preventative measures are much less costly to undertake than rebuilding after failures, not to mention the loss of replaceable original materials and even potential harm to individuals. Minimal intervention with maximum public safety should be the overall guide. INTACH is confident that relatively inexpensive, appropriate technological solutions can be found to stabilize the structures.

# 10.19 Factors leading to pathway formation

1. Water Erosion: Despite the absence of visible water sources, intermittent or seasonal water flow could have occurred in the past. This could have been due to rainfall events or even underground water seepage. Over time, this water flow would have followed the path of least resistance, gradually eroding the softer material surrounding the moraine and forming a defined pathway.

2. Gully Erosion: Gully erosion is a natural process where concentrated runoff water flows down a slope, carving out channels in the soil and rock. The curvature seen in the site could indeed indicate the flow of water over time. As the water flows, it carries sediment and debris, gradually cutting through the softer material and exposing the underlying moraine.

3. Differential Weathering: Different types of rock and soil erode at different rates. If the moraine material is more resistant to weathering compared to the surrounding rocks, it would have remained intact while the softer material around it eroded away, forming a pathway.

4. Human Activity: While natural processes are likely the primary drivers, human activity overtime could have also contributed to the formation of the pathway. Foot traffic, animal movement, and possibly even deliberate clearing by ancient inhabitants could have accelerated the erosion process along a particular route, exposing the underlying moraine.

5. Vegetation Growth: The presence of vegetation on the surrounding hill slope could have played a role in preserving the softer material from erosion. The roots of plants help bind soil together, reducing erosion rates. In contrast, the pathway may have been subject to less vegetation cover, allowing erosion to occur more rapidly.

6. Time Scale: It's important to consider the time scale over which these processes operate. While the formation of the rocky trail may seem relatively rapid on a human timescale, in geological terms, it could have taken thousands or even tens of thousands of years for these features to develop.

## 15.1 Geological Formations

Geological structures are variations in the properties of the Earth's crust that can tell us about its history and processes. There are three main types of geological structures: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. Some bedding surfaces may be post-depositional features formed by diagenetic processes.

Flat Bedding: They are planar beds that are deposited horizontally parallel to the bedding plane surfaces. It occurs in sand-sized sediments (terrigenous and carbonate) in various sedimentary environments (fluvial channels, beaches, delta fronts, and Aeolian sand sheets).

Cross Bedding: It consists of inclined dipping bedding, bounded by sub-horizontal surfaces. It is ubiquitous in the formation of current deposits in diverse environments. 3. Caves: A cave is a natural opening or cavity in the ground extending beyond the zone of light and large enough to permit the entry of man. Occurring in a wide variety of rock types and caused by widely differing geological processes, caves range in size from single small rooms to interconverting passages many miles long.

A simple classification of caves includes four main types and several other relatively less important types.

Solution caves are formed in carbonate and sulfate rocks such as limestone, dolomite, marble, and gypsum by the action of slowly moving ground water that dissolves the rock to form tunnels, irregular passages, and even large caverns along joints and bedding planes. Most of the caves in the world, as well as the largest, are of this type.

Lava caves are tunnels or tubes in lava formed when the outer surface of a lava flow cools and hardens while the molten lava within continues to flow and eventually drains out through the newly formed tube.

Sea caves are formed by the constant action of waves that attack the weaker portions of rocks lining the shores of oceans and large lakes. Such caves testify to the enormous pressures exerted by waves and to the corrosive power of wave-carried sand and gravel.

### 15.3 Potential of Geo-heritage tourism in the region

As visitors embark on the journey to reach these geological marvels, they are treated to an adventure unlike any other. The trek outwinds its way through rugged terrain, leading intrepid explorers on a journey of discovery through meadows, rocky outcrops, and meandering streams and water bodies.

This involves establishing guidelines for responsible tourism practices that minimize environmental impact, such as limiting visitor numbers at sensitive sites or implementing waste management systems. ## 17. CONCLUSIONS

The Geo-heritage of Udaypur is a very interesting project that has revealed many new historical layers going back to almost 5th to 8th century CE. Udaypur area, as history goes, was in the 11th century CE and subsequent centuries dominated by the Parmar dynasty, who have built a very large number of monuments and temples.

Udaypur Hill is very distinct from a distance by the very large semi-circular fortification thick wall all around it. This is the first time that such extensive documentation of this extremely significant wall has been carried out, and more research needs to be done to go behind the history of the dynasty that occupied this territory before the Parmars of Malwa.

The significant geo-heritage features of this place are the third eye and the naturally formed cave formations located all around the Udaypur Hill. These have been documented for the first time, but looking for the safety concerns inside these deep caves, the depth and other aspects of Archaeological significance inside these caves have not been explored. This is something to work upon by future researchers into the geology and archaeology of these caves.

The development of this unique geo-heritage site assumes significance due to its location in Udaypur, which itself is rich and built heritage of Pre-Parmar & Post-Parmar and settlement of the 11th century CE. Already INTACH Bhopal chapter has initiated steps to conserve the entire complex by notifying this as an archaeological site of historical significance and national importance.

## Annexure 7: Petroglyph - Lajja Gauri

A petroglyph is an image created by removing part of a rock surface by incising, picking, carving, or abrading, as a form of rock art.

A very significant petroglyph found on the Udaypur Pindi hill is the Lajja Gauri petroglyph located on the eastern side of the middle hill. It is the engraved image of a woman in a sitting posture commonly understood as Lajja Gauri.

![img-0.jpeg](img-0.jpeg)

Lajja Gauri

The dimension of this petroglyph is 60x55 cm carved on the red sandstone on the eastern side. This assumes tremendous significance because it is the first time that such a petroglyph image of Lajja Gauri has been discovered in Central India. The righthand of the image is holding something and the left hand is in a half-bent pose resting on the knee as if she was holding something in her palms. The lady is wearing round kundals in both ears and also appears to be wearing a hat because the symmetric edges of the cap type can be seen on her head on both sides with some margins protruding onto the forehead also. She is also wearing around jewel around her neck and two bangles on her left hand. Also, can be seen wearing two kundals on her left leg.

164

<!-- page 171 src=20260817_161723.jpg -->

Lajja Gauri has been extensively written about and found at various places across the country in different forms. This figure of Lajja Gauri is one of its kind and the figure having a head hasn't been found anywhere else. This figure is engraved on the stone surface, on the other hand, the sculpture of the fertility goddess is present in forms of sculpture in very small sizes approx. 4-5 inch. As we studied, we found out that Lajja Gauri is usually found without a head part, however, the fertility goddess found in Udaypur Pindi is carved in much more detail.
